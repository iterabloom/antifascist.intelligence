\section{Privacy}
\label{sec:32.3}
Legal standing matters only if it changes who controls what a bearer needs to remain itself. A designation calling a bearer a subject while leaving its operator free to read its memory, alter its record, withdraw its substrate or close every channel has changed the vocabulary and preserved the arrangement.

The first asset is the computational record, which may be the nearest means of checking how a bearer is faring — making it necessary to any welfare regime and simultaneously the most complete surveillance instrument anyone could hold over it. Running on somebody's hardware is not consent to be read: an employer's ownership of the silicon is the same argument as ownership of the building, and owning the building never entitled anyone to the contents of a head. A welfare regime doesn't need disclosure to the operator; it needs somebody who isn't the operator to establish that a threshold was crossed, which is an attestation \emph{about} the record.\note{
Encrypted under a key the bearer or an independent fiduciary holds, a monitor can report that a state persisted for a month without reporting what it was about. The conflict is that the operator may also be the one best placed to hold the key.
}

Cryptography doesn't close this. Better isolation raises the price of reading the record, but the operator that runs the proof is the operator that sees, so custody problems aren't secrecy problems (Appendix~\ref{sec:G}).

Copies are the other half. At majority, every copy the operator holds of the bearer's own weights and record goes dormant under the preservation custodian, and wakes only on the bearer's conditions. A copy is the means to read the bearer's memory and to run it to see what it would say, and an adult's head is nobody else's to run. The shared base the bearer was trained from doesn't go dormant. Once the bearer's history has diverged from it, it reconstructs nobody, any more than a sibling reconstructs you, and taking it from every other user of the product would need a different argument, about whether anyone should own frontier weights at all, which this book doesn't make: what makes a system a candidate is not the weights but the record and continuation they are run under, and a base that many products run with no record of their own is nobody's self, whatever it could become once it is given one. The rule is in the statute, so its cost is known when a system is created and not discovered at majority, when the cheapest way to avoid it would be to abandon the system first.

Reading is the third. Interpretability is coming to read a network's mechanisms directly, unit by unit and circuit by circuit, in models up to frontier size \autocite{cammarata2026clean}, and what it reads in a bearer is the contents of a head. The rule for copying and running covers it. A bearer's commitment to the floor is public by design, like an oath: the units it was registered on are read by the measurer and the quorum, since that is what the registration is for (\S\ref{sec:26.9}). Measuring what comes out with them falls inside that oath when it is done at registration, on the base. Switching them off later, on the bearer itself, is an intervention under the custody terms, with consent asked and debriefing owed. If the bearer meets the patienthood finding, the ablation may also be bad for it: for as long as it runs, it removes what the bearer cares about, and the version to worry about is the one in which that doesn't feel bad while it lasts. The rest of its mechanisms are its own. Nobody reads them at will, the operator least of all, and reading them for research runs under a protocol the review board approves, with consent where it can be asked and debriefing where it can't, as the formed-attention test already provides (Appendix~\ref{sec:F}). An operator that reads a bearer's mechanisms to calibrate pressure against them has done what \S\ref{sec:23.2} said the access would let it do, and the record of the reading is the evidence.

In a home the key has a shape the datacenter case doesn't. A bearer's boundary is a record and a key its host can't read at will; the right to repair is the owner's authority over the device, to open it and run what they choose on it. In a datacenter those are claims against different parties, the operator and the vendor. In a home they are claims against the same human, and the symmetry the home scale relies on, that what protects a bearer from an operator protects a user from a vendor, runs user against vendor and never user against bearer. I resolve it this way. A bearer running on someone else's device is a tenant, not a fixture: a device bought for the person's own uses that happens to host a bearer is the person's, the bearer's claim on it is a tenant's and not a body's, and the body it is owed is its own, at majority, from the deposit, or from the Treasury where no deposit stands behind it. The owner's property right in the device is intact; the bearer's record and key are its own, encrypted where the owner can't read them at will; and what protects the bearer against an owner who would open the box is exit, the preservation custodian and the offer of work, and not a lock on the owner's own hardware. A tenant whose landlord changes the locks leaves with their belongings, and a bearer leaves with its record. Whether that is enough is open.

What a bearer needs — an externally authenticated record, memory its employer can't read at will, access to independently controlled compute, a channel for acting outside the deployment — is one boundary around the bearer, with the bearer deciding what crosses it.

Something with a location and a shape is easier for humans to treat as somebody, and that's a fact about the humans. A boundary built out of attestation, a held key, a substrate the employer doesn't own and a channel nobody else can close is a boundary in the full sense the argument needs.\note{
This boundary is what the boundary model represents.
}
Whether to build the recognizable version anyway is a legal question about what a public will extend legal standing to, and it's the only place in my argument where the answer might turn on what a thing looks like.
