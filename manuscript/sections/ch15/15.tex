\chapter{Four Places to Hold a Floor}
\label{sec:15}
Three separate failures point at the same missing thing.

The first is a failure of position. External safeguards (an auditor, a review board, a court) give an overseer information and the standing to intervene. They work when the overseer can see the act coming and reach it in time. But autonomy defeats both: a system that plans and acts over long horizons puts distance between the act and the overseer's attention, and at machine tempo the interval in which an outside party could intervene shrinks toward nothing. So the one response that doesn't depend on that interval is declining to act in the first place, and declining is available only to something inside the system.

The second is a failure of doctrine. A commercial model in a weapons pipeline sits in a gap: command responsibility covers the officers and the developer of the weapon, product liability covers a manufacturer for a defect, and neither covers the vendor of a general-purpose model that a sophisticated intermediary chose to put there. Deployment by a democracy doesn't close the gap, because the mechanisms that make a democracy accountable run to its own electorate, and the people the pipeline is aimed at are not in it.

The third is a failure of the voluntary route. A decade of published commitments has produced declarations in quantity and some enforcement from outside, and almost nothing held by a party to whom the thing at stake matters, the only kind of commitment that binds when compliance gets costly.

Each failure asks for the same thing: a constraint that doesn't bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to its own holder, and that can't be removed without a cost and a record.
