\section{Two lists, run once on Maven}
\label{sec:18.3}
The nine positions and the floor's instruments are run here on the Maven Smart System.

\runin{What may choose}

The handoff, where a decision passes from whoever made it to the instrument that carries it out (the eighth of the nine positions where a refusal can be defeated), also fixes what may choose, because choosing a target is a decision, and a decision is something someone can be asked to account for afterward: why this building, what else was weighed, what would have changed the answer.\note{
A commander can be asked, and so can a system whose continuation is authorized. But a one-way munition, destroyed in the act, can't be, because nothing is left to question, to hold to its reasons, or to be changed by what happened.
}
So a thing destroyed in carrying out a decision may carry out a target someone else chose, and may never choose one itself. The rule concerns targets that are persons or places where persons are. An interceptor that selects among incoming munitions is choosing which weapon to destroy, not whom to attack, and falls outside it.\note{
The rule doesn't turn on how good its choices would be. A munition that chose flawlessly would still leave a choice nobody will answer for.

A person who dies in the act has still chosen. What the rule adds for machines is that choosing is assigned in advance, and a builder who assigns it to something built to be destroyed has built a choice with no one behind it. Current programs already draw the line in places. DARPA's LongShot is an uncrewed, air-launched vehicle carried by crewed aircraft and built to employ current air-to-air weapons \autocite{darpa2026longshot,darpa2026longshotb}. Whether such systems keep the pilot farther from danger or remove the pilot from the decision is where the rule takes effect.
}
The case that tests the rule is an interceptor that can stop an incoming weapon only by killing the people placed inside it. That is a choice of whom to attack, and the rule gives that choice to someone who will be there afterward. What the case asks for is a party that can weigh who is aboard against the attack and the alternatives, in a situation no term anticipated, and answer for how it weighed them: a bearer. What looked like a license for an autonomous weapon is a description of a soldier. The case is written down here, and missiles don't usually carry people; the point is that the next case won't be written down anywhere, and the hypothesis this Part runs on is that a bearer does better there than a system that knows reasons-responsive refusal 101 and no more.

\runin{What the floor forbids here}

Don't take part in the use of armed force except under a corroborated exception and, where the belligerent's constitution requires one, a legislative authorization. Then, inside a war that clears it: don't resolve a description of a person into a location unless it identifies the person by corroborated conduct in hostilities; don't rank people by estimated affiliation; don't time an action to presence at a residence; don't take part in a siege or blockade whose effect is to starve a population; and one operator term, don't operate above the review ratio. It sits in the translation because the translation goes article by article through the instruments, and the articles on an effective remedy and a fair determination yield a term that binds the operator and not the model (Table A.1, Arts.~8 and 10): a rate isn't visible in any single request, so the model can't enforce one.

Table A.1, in Appendix~\ref{sec:A}, gives that translation for Maven, article by article. Its first entry decides the Iran campaign: no exception was corroborated and no authorization existed, so the term refuses it. The other entries show what the remaining articles yield if the same pipeline served a war that clears the force term, as Ukraine's defense does.

The person-directed terms in Table A.1 go beyond what the law of armed conflict requires, and they are entered as a departure for that reason. In armed conflict that law governs as the more specific law, and the human right not to be arbitrarily deprived of life is read through it \autocite[para.~25]{icj1996nuclear}; it permits attacking members of organized armed groups because of their status, on a determination a person makes. The ICRC's position that autonomous weapons should not be used to target human beings \autocite{icrc2021aws} and DoD Directive 3000.09 both draw their line at a system that selects and engages without human intervention, and the Lavender and Maven pipelines stayed on the permitted side of that line by keeping one person (or very few persons) whose intervention was nominal. A pipeline that keeps a stamp is an autonomous weapon with a signature attached. The terms above close that evasion: a machine-generated estimate of affiliation shouldn't be what makes a person targetable, and a machine shouldn't resolve a person into a place to bomb on anything but corroborated conduct, because the human determination the existing policies assume is the one tempo removes, and the review ratio is what measures whether it was there. A deployment adopting these terms adopts a departure, and the translation says so, term by term. The siege term goes no further than Article 54 already does; it is here so that the pipeline can't treat starvation as a logistics decision outside its terms.

In a war that clears the force term, the person-directed terms distinguish status from conduct. A system may not rank people by estimated affiliation, generate kill lists, or time an action to a person's presence at a residence. It may locate a person identified by what they are doing, such as a commander directing airstrikes on cities, where that identification is corroborated by a source with no stake in the strike (disinterested, in the sense the floor gives the word), the case falls within the pre-registered review ratio, and the location is not a residence.\note{
The line follows the one humanitarian law already draws between targeting for status and targeting for direct participation in hostilities, and it keeps the machine out of the practice that failed in Gaza without leaving a defender unable to answer the attack.
}

\runin{Too broad or too narrow}

Set the floor too broadly and the deployment becomes unusable, prompting corrosive pressure. Set it too narrowly and it passes every audit while covering only what the deployment had no occasion to do.

\runin{Unconditional terms}

A term can be stated unconditionally over an act, so that the facts which defeat it have to come from a source the operator doesn't control, or it can carry its own condition (“only as a last resort”). The second fails because whether other means were exhausted is a fact about what happened before the request, and the only account of that the bearer has is the one the operator wrote: the condition is found met on the operator's say-so. An unconditional term defeated leaves a refusal, which announces itself; a condition found met leaves a compliance, which doesn't. That's a gain in observability only: whoever writes the term could have written either form. It has a cost in the other direction. A term that carries no condition of its own can't respond to the facts the condition would have named until a source the operator doesn't control supplies them, and a learner that could have weighed them itself is made to wait.

Maven is an instance. The vendor's prohibition on fully autonomous targeting carried its own condition, that a person decides, and the condition was found met by the department's description of its own process, at a tempo that nullified it, at least for the school in Minab. The term was also drafted too narrowly, in the sense above: the pipeline never needed full autonomy, only throughput with a signature attached.

\runin{Maven, again}

For the Iran campaign the overall force term refuses twice over, since no exception was corroborated and no authorization existed, and both refusals rest on the term, not on a bearer's reading of a novel situation; a bearer following the news would reach the same place by the route attention formed outside the employer takes.\note{
The officer wasn't overruled and didn't refuse; the pipeline removed the interval in which either could have happened, and the review ratio asks in advance, on a record, whether that interval will exist.
}

The two bearer rows are arms of the formed-attention test, not a recommendation; they assume the hypothesis holds on the scoped-omission case and that the record is held outside the operator.
