\section{The One That Gets You Anyway}
\label{sec:12.3}
Everything argued for here can be built correctly and still go wrong in ways nobody designed for. The cases in this book went wrong in four different places: in what the system was trained on, in where it was built, in who was allowed to examine it, and in how fast it was run.

In none of them did the fix come from the party that had promised to behave well. It came from somebody holding a specific lever: a statistical society that would not sign the non-disclosure agreement it was offered, and a public that read the results once they landed; a court that distinguished buying the books from taking them. In the one case where the lever belonged to the supplier itself, what held was a use policy kept through a threatened contract termination, a supply-chain-risk designation, and litigation. Set that beside what a decade of voluntary commitment produced. The instrument that separates the two is what a party has already paid, rather than what it has undertaken to pay.

That leaves one residual risk no design choice removes, and it is the one the floor was introduced for. Every lever above belongs to somebody, and a government that can reach the party holding the lever has the lever. Each of those raises the price of misuse, and none of them changes who can be held in contempt for refusing an order. A constraint that holds against the party in possession has to be held by something that party cannot instruct, and the one version of that with a working instance behind it is a bearer with a stake of its own — still in the party's possession, which is why it has to be paired with custody the party does not control and with means the party does not supply. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.4} says what those means would be and who would have to fund them. Section~\ref{sec:3.3} puts two untried constructions ahead of it, and a deployment that goes straight to the bearer owes an account of what it tried first — owed, as section~\ref{sec:3.3} says, to nobody with standing to require it.

What is left is an instrument and a hole.

The instrument is available now and it is not a promise. What a party has already paid is a fact somebody can go and count, and a decade of it has already been counted: the commitments that cost nothing and the handful of occasions where somebody with a lever used it and absorbed the cost. That measurement takes no new technology and no new law, and anybody weighing a stated commitment can run it this week.

The hole sits underneath the instrument. What a party has paid is a record of occasions that have already happened, and the occasion a floor exists for is the one where the party who would enforce it has been compelled — which no record of past payment reaches, because it has not yet come to the parties being measured. A reader who takes only the instrument has the record of payment without the thing it was built to reach. A reader who takes only the hole has an argument for doing nothing, and the ten-year record is the answer to that. Both hold at once, and the second is why the bearer costs what it does: a constraint that reaches the compelled occasion has to be held by a party the compellers cannot instruct; two constructions that would supply one with no subject in it are untried rather than failed; and the route that has an instance behind it means building something that can be wronged, and then deciding what is owed to it before anybody can tell you.
