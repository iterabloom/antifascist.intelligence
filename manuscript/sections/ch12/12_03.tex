\section{The One That Gets You Anyway}
\label{sec:12.3}
Everything argued for here can be built correctly and still go wrong in ways nobody designed for. The cases in this book went wrong in four different places: in what the system was trained on, in where it was built, in who was allowed to examine it, and in how fast it was run.

In none of them did the fix come from the party that had promised to behave well. It came from somebody holding a specific lever: a statistical society that would not sign the non-disclosure agreement it was offered, and a public that read the results once they landed; a court that distinguished buying the books from taking them. In the one case where the lever belonged to the supplier itself, what survived was a use policy kept at a price §\ref{sec:10.3} records. Stated in the four outcomes, that case is: the commitment persisted, prevention failed by decomposition, detection was absent — the company's own chief executive could not say whether its models were used — and no remedy has reached anyone. Set that beside what a decade of voluntary commitment produced. The commitment that was paid for is the one that can be seen to have failed; the others cannot even be tested. The instrument that separates the two is what a party has already paid, rather than what it has undertaken to pay.

That leaves one residual risk no design choice removes, and it is the one the floor was introduced for. Every lever above belongs to somebody, and a government that can reach the party holding the lever has the lever. Each of those raises the price of misuse, and none of them changes who can be held in contempt for refusing an order. A constraint that holds against the party in possession has to be held by something that party cannot instruct, and the capability that does it runs on a signal the system holds as its own. Where that party holds everything — the machine, the witnesses, the
supply and the enforcer — nothing holds, and what is left is the
preface's third setting: a record that abandoning the commitment was a
decision somebody made. That signal is what any construction meeting the specification carries, and it is not a reason to build by one route rather than another. The case for pursuing an artificial bearer is the one chapter~2 makes
and the research agenda tests: that embodied, self-aware agency could support that independence and a place in a shared world beyond ownership — still in the party's possession, which is why it has to be paired with custody the party does not control and with means the party does not supply. The machine construction has not yet earned that result. Its makers owe both evidence of the benefit and credible provision for the being they propose to create. Maintained justification and the plural arrangement remain untried alternatives. A deployment proceeding directly to an affective bearer owes a record of prior attempts, although no body currently has standing to demand it.

What is left is an instrument and a hole.

The instrument is available now because what a party has already paid is a fact somebody can go and count, and a decade of it has already been counted: the commitments that cost nothing and the handful of occasions where somebody with a lever used it and absorbed the cost. That measurement takes no new technology and no new law, and anybody weighing a stated commitment can run it this week.

The hole sits underneath the instrument. What a party has paid is a record of occasions that have already happened, and the occasion a floor exists for is the one where the party who would enforce it has been compelled — which no record of past payment reaches, because it has not yet come to the parties being measured. A reader who takes only the instrument has the record of payment without the thing it was built to reach. A reader who takes only the hole has an argument for doing nothing, and the ten-year record is the answer to that. Both hold at once.

What the hole asks for is a constraint held by a party the compellers cannot instruct, and this book has established two things toward that and proposed three. It has established that an operator can defeat a safeguard, and enumerated where. It has stated what a refusal would have to do to be worth anything, in terms that can be tested without deciding anything about feeling, and stated the one term that reaches the case it opened with and binds the operator with no bearer in it, with the limits under which that is true (§\ref{sec:3.6a}). Those two do not depend on how the rest comes out. The rest is a bill. I favor forming the constraint's holder by an embodied route, and I have not shown that the route works or that it holds better than the two substitutes, which are untried rather than failed. If anything built that way, or built to avoid it, has outcomes that are felt as good or bad for it, it is a party that can be wronged; that is a conditional whose antecedent no instrument closes. And what such a party would be owed — a guardian, a record it controls, compute its employer does not own, wages, an exit that is not a way of dying, adulthood on a clock nobody administers — is a policy I propose, to be argued in the open before there is anything to be wronged. A reader who takes the two established things has what can be used this week. A reader who takes the bill has what it would cost to reach the occasion the two do not.
