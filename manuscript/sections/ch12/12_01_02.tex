\subsection{Commitment Was Never the Missing Piece}
\label{sec:12.1.2}
A global commitment to ethical, antifascist AI development is not the scarce thing. Declarations, principles and voluntary codes have accumulated for a decade across governments, industry consortia and standards bodies: eight overlapping instruments beside the one framework convention that would bind a party to anything, which section~\ref{sec:10.4} reaches. Anyone calling for the world to agree that AI should be developed responsibly is calling for something that has already happened, repeatedly, at the level of stated intent.

What has stayed scarce is the much narrower list of bodies that can act once a stated commitment is broken. That is the harder problem, and no further declaration touches it. A commitment nobody can enforce is not a weak version of a commitment somebody can. It is a different kind of object, and counting the two together is what makes the landscape look fuller than it is.

There is a third kind of object. A commitment can be declared, which is the decade above. It can be enforced from outside, which one instrument in it even attempts. Or it can be held — carried as a reason by a party the thing at stake matters to, so that abandoning it is something that party would have to decide to do rather than something an absent enforcer failed to prevent. A floor has to be the third kind. The first binds nobody, and the second binds only where an enforcer exists, has jurisdiction, and is not itself the party in need of binding.

What it costs to build something that can hold a commitment is the whole of that argument, and the cost is a party that can be wronged. Set against that, the catalog above is not an argument for paying it. It is a measurement of what the alternative has delivered in ten years of trying.
