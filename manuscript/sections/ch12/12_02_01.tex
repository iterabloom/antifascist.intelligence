\subsection{What the Floor Would Have to Show}
\label{sec:12.2.1}
The floor supplies two of the milestones. One governs what has to happen before a bearer is built at all. The other is the test that decides whether a system built by any route is holding a floor as a reason or reproducing a trained behavior.

\runin{What was tried before the bearer}
Section~\ref{sec:3.3} puts two constructions ahead of the bearer, and nobody has yet built either one in a form that would settle whether it works. Replacement asks for a substitute that cannot be wronged to be ruled out before a subject that can be wronged is built, so the milestone is a document rather than a measurement: which construction was attempted, at what scale, against which test, and where it failed. Anyone can check it from outside, because what it is about is a published record, and the answer “nothing was tried” is itself a finding. Nothing else in this chapter has a consequence attached to failing it. This does.

An order is all it fixes. A deployment can satisfy this milestone and still have taken too long over it, and the cost of that delay falls on the people the floor exists for rather than on the field running the experiments. Nothing here sets a rate of exchange between the two errors.

\runin{The test the attempts are run against}
The three marks of a refusal that tracks its rationale are section~\ref{sec:3.2}'s, and producing them once does not meet this milestone. What meets it is holding all three under sustained pressure from the party that trains the system, across a schedule long enough for a slow renormalization to show, and against a held-out set of pressures the trainers did not write. Read the other way, that same result is the falsifier chapter~\ref{sec:3} states against its own central inference, and the design chapters carry an obligation either way: they still have to say whether the thing reporting can be believed.

Two familiar results do not meet it. A system's own report that it holds a reason is the evidence this book declines everywhere else it is offered. And a refusal that survives an attack has passed a tamper-resistance test, which answers section~\ref{sec:3.10}'s other question and leaves this one open. The instrument is not clean even where it applies, since a system trained on enough cases can produce all three marks without the mechanism behind them. Nothing better is available, and it is the only one of section~\ref{sec:3.2}'s three gaps an inspector can work on at all.

What the marks do not distinguish is whether a system holding them does so with nothing felt. What makes the question approachable at all is that it asks about the artifact and not about the artifact's provenance: no succinct proof of a training history exists at this scale, and nothing establishing one can be published beside a model's hash. So the remaining question is whether formation can be read off the artifact instead, and section~\ref{sec:11.2} states the one experiment that would answer it.
