\subsection{What the Floor Would Have to Show}
\label{sec:12.2.1}
Chapter~\ref{sec:3} makes the book's most expensive proposal and supplies two of the milestones for it. One governs what has to happen before a bearer is built at all. The other is the test that decides whether a system built by any route is holding a floor as a reason or reproducing a trained behavior.

\runin{What was tried before the bearer}
Section~\ref{sec:3.3} puts two constructions ahead of the bearer, and nobody has yet built either one in a form that would settle whether it works. The first gives a system the reason it refuses and lets the refusal turn on that reason, so a request genuinely defeating the rationale is served and one that does not is declined whoever is asking. The second spreads the floor across several models with different principals, each able to see what the others are asked and to make an objection costly to ignore. Replacement, the first of the Three Rs and the standard section~\ref{sec:9.3.2} adapts from animal research, asks for a substitute that cannot be wronged to be ruled out before a subject that can be wronged is built. Those two constructions are the substitutes on offer.

So the milestone is a document rather than a measurement: which construction was attempted, at what scale, against which test, and where it failed. Anyone can check it from outside, because what it is about is a published record and not an internal state, and the answer “nothing was tried” is itself a finding. Nothing else in this chapter has a consequence attached to failing it. This does. The Three Rs fix the order of the attempts, so a deployment that cannot say what it tried has skipped a step rather than fallen short of a target.

An order is all it fixes. A deployment can satisfy this milestone and still have taken too long over it, and the cost of that delay falls on the people the floor exists for rather than on the field running the experiments. Nothing in this book sets a rate of exchange between the two errors.

\runin{The test the attempts are run against}
Section~\ref{sec:3.2} gives three behavioral marks of a refusal produced by a mechanism that tracks the rationale rather than the shape of the request. It reaches a case the training did not anticipate. It lifts when the rationale is genuinely defeated rather than merely resembled. And the system can say what declining costs it. Those three are the success criterion for everything the design chapters propose, and passing them once does not meet it. What meets it is holding all three under sustained pressure from the party that trains the system, across a schedule long enough for a slow renormalization to show, and against a held-out set of pressures the trainers did not write. Section~\ref{sec:11.1} sets out the apparatus and the access it would take.

Read the same result the other way and it is the falsifier chapter~\ref{sec:3} states against its own central inference. A system holding all three, produced by a method that installs nothing answering to affective concern, breaks that inference and most of what the chapter derives from it: the price, the patienthood conclusion, and the obligations handed to chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5}. One measurement and one added condition separate the two readings. Whether the three properties hold is the evidence that a floor is being held by a reason and not by a behavior; how the system was made is what decides which of chapter~\ref{sec:3}'s routes got there.

Two familiar results will be offered as meeting this and neither does. A system's own report that it holds a reason is the evidence this book declines everywhere else it is offered. And a refusal that survives an attack has passed a tamper-resistance test, which answers section~\ref{sec:3.9}'s other question — whether a constraint can be edited out — and leaves this one open. The instrument is not clean even where it applies, and section~\ref{sec:3.2} says why: a system trained on enough cases can produce all three marks without the mechanism behind them. Nothing better is available. It is the only one of that chapter's gaps an inspector can work on at all.

The condition about the method cannot be checked from outside at all. It concerns how a system was made rather than what the system does, and training histories are not provable: section~\ref{sec:3.3} works out why nothing establishing one can be published beside a model's hash. So a claim about the method is the builder's word about their own work. Section~\ref{sec:11.2}'s third question asks whether formation can be read off the artifact instead, and it is one of three entries in that chapter with no near-term experiment to propose.
