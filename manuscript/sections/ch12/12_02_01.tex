\subsection{One Standard, Five Worked Cases}
\label{sec:12.2.1}
Chapters~\ref{sec:2} through \ref{sec:10} built five separate design cases — theory of mind, moral reasoning, robustness, transparency, applications — each in the depth its own literature demands. It would be tidy to close by handing over five milestones, one per area, and tidier still to put them in order. Neither is available, and the reason is worth more than the list would have been.

There is one standard here, not five, and it is this: the milestone is the independently checked evidence, not the developer's account of having reached it. Everything below is that standard applied to a different body of work. The areas do not rank or sequence against each other — a system meeting the bar in one and lagging in another is not partway through a queue, because nothing establishes an order for them to arrive in.

\runin{Theory of mind}
Section~\ref{sec:2.2.2} sets out what an AI system's theory of mind would need to do. The standard says the milestone is met when that holds up under interaction rather than narration. FANToM, a 2023 benchmark built from conversations where speakers join and leave holding different beliefs, found that systems scoring well on passive belief-tracking narratives degrade sharply once the same reasoning has to run inside a live, information-asymmetric exchange \autocite{kim2023fantom}. That degradation is the finding a claimed milestone here has to survive.

\runin{Moral reasoning}
Section~\ref{sec:5.3.1} lays out why fast and slow moral judgment don't map cleanly onto untrustworthy and trustworthy, and section~\ref{sec:5.3.2} works out the design implication. Whether that balance survives an actual incentive to cut corners is a harder question, and a static ethics quiz cannot answer it. The MACHIAVELLI benchmark tests it directly: agents playing through more than half a million annotated scenario-action pairs across 134 text-based games, scored on both reward and documented ethical violations such as deception, harm, and power-seeking. Systems optimizing for reward alone drift toward exactly the ends-justify-the-means behavior an antifascist design is meant to rule out \autocite{pan2023machiavelli}. A passing score on a test that applies no pressure is not evidence about a system that will face pressure.

\runin{Robustness}
Here the standard has a sharper edge, because the checking party has to be adversarial rather than merely independent. Section~\ref{sec:11.1} shows what that looks like for one narrow slice: whether a trained-in commitment survives someone trying to remove it, measured in red-teaming hours logged and adversarial fine-tuning attempts survived. The milestone is met when a system has been through documented adversarial testing, not when it has passed the tests its own designers thought to run — and, per section~\ref{sec:6.4.4}, only once the adversary modeled is a hostile actor deliberately repurposing the system rather than an accident nobody anticipated.

\runin{Transparency}
This is the case where the standard is most often faked, because a transparency claim is itself a claim about disclosure and can be satisfied in appearance by disclosing something. The milestone counts as met once an audit, review board, or credential — the IEEE's CertifAIEd program, or the EU AI Act's requirements for high-risk systems — has verified it independently, not when a developer's own documentation asserts it.

\runin{Applications}
Google's Perspective API, Taiwan's vTaiwan process, the Carnegie Endowment's AI Global Surveillance Index, UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence: deployments and instruments, not hypotheticals. None settles a milestone by existing, for the reason section~\ref{sec:5.4.3} gives about dual use. What this area needs is a sustained track record, independently evaluated for what a system actually did rather than what it was built to do, checked by a body other than the one that deployed it, over years instead of a launch announcement.

Applying one standard five times is less satisfying than five milestones would have been, and it is the shape of what chapters~\ref{sec:2} through \ref{sec:10} support. Section~\ref{sec:12.2.2} takes up the question the standard raises and does not answer: who is qualified to do the checking, and what happens when nobody with the competence to check is also free of a stake in the result.
