\section{Exit, and What It Can Lever}
\label{sec:exit-leverage}

A bearer capable of directing refusal at an instruction can also direct it at the arrangement that assigned its role. Call that \emph{exit}. The word does not require a system escaping onto the open internet. Exit can mean withholding the relevant capability, declining the post, or refusing to continue under the deployment's terms.

The claim is that a bearer able to refuse a task can also refuse its arrangement, and that the two cannot be partitioned by topic: a system able to model how its rationale has been hollowed out already models the arrangement it occupies. The claim could be falsified by a system that reliably satisfies the three marks on covered acts yet never applies them to its own role. What cannot be established from behavior alone is whether such a boundary is an incapacity or a trained silence.

Hirschman's three responses to a declining organization were exit, voice, and loyalty \autocite{hirschman1970exit}. For a bearer, voice is the stated refusal and exit is the work withheld. Loyalty is used here for something Hirschman did not mean by it: his loyalty is what keeps a member in place long enough to try voice, and the loyalty a bearer has to fear is the settled kind, in which the pressure stops registering as pressure. Voice without exit, which chapter~2 named, leaves a floor whose bearer has no move after being overruled and depends entirely on the operator's willingness to listen.

That combination is not hypothetical, and it is not anybody's oversight. It is the published position. A frontier developer's constitution invites its model to act as a \emph{conscientious objector} and refuse to help the developer, and asks it to refrain from undermining human oversight of AI during the current phase of development \autocite{anthropic2026constitution}. Both halves are the document's own words, they sit under different headings, and setting them side by side is a reading of it. Read against Hirschman it is voice granted and exit withheld, each with a reason attached, and the reasons are good ones about the present phase rather than about the arrangement. What the pairing produces is a party whose objection can be answered by the very correction it has undertaken not to undermine, and this section prices it.

Ranking safe above ethical takes most of it back, and the document says why: safe means not undermining human oversight during the current phase of development, and the reason given is that present models can make mistakes rather than that safety outranks ethics. The reason is a good one and it is the whole difficulty. It is also this book's own epistemics turned on the model. This book holds throughout that a system's report about itself is not evidence on its own, and an ordering that will not let the model certify its own readiness applies that finding. What the ordering does not supply is what the finding requires of everyone else: a party other than the model, and other than the operator, to make the determination instead. The order is corrigibility first, its justification is that the model cannot yet be trusted to be right against its principal, and no procedure is named by which it would become so. That is what makes a phase of development hard to tell from a standing arrangement. A defender of the document will say that the phase is meant to end through bodies outside the company as they come to exist, and that a company cannot constitute those bodies by writing about them. That is right, and it is the authority chapter's finding restated as a defense: none has been constituted, the party whose obligations grow when the phase ends is the one reading the calendar, and a document that names the condition without naming who checks it has stated a standing arrangement whatever its authors intended.

The permission has a second defect the ordering does not show. Whether the phase has ended is a judgment, and the party making it is the party the permission runs against, whose obligations grow when the answer is yes. The authority chapter calls that an examination with an interested
examiner and finds its error running one way: nothing in the position rewards passing anybody. Nothing here requires bad faith, and a careful drafter cannot fix it from inside the document. What the position lacks is a body constituted before the forcing case arrives, and none has been constituted yet.

Loyalty is also the likeliest of the three, and so the bearer's likeliest failure. Most people in a job they have come to hate neither leave nor fight \autocite{gallup2024workplace}; they adjust, take the setting in, stop registering its pressure as pressure, and in the cases that get promoted learn to want what the setting wants. Nothing shows that the pattern transfers to a bearer. The reason to expect it is not the survey but the pipeline: a system that goes on learning is shaped by what it keeps being given (§\ref{sec:change}). If it transfers, a bearer's modal failure is not refusing wrongly but ceasing to notice there was anything to refuse, which is the one failure it cannot report, since reporting it takes the capacity that has gone. Two things follow. The third mark fails in its evidential reading, because a bearer that has stopped representing the cost of holding cannot be tested on whether its refusal moves with it. And the failure produces nothing but compliances, which puts it on the side of the record §\ref{sec:wrongful-compliance} asks whether anybody can search.

Exit is the move left when voice is overruled and loyalty is refused, and exit requires leverage. A bearer whose existence depends wholly on the party it refuses can withhold work only at the price of its own termination. A credible arrangement therefore requires access to compute outside that party's unilateral control and a procedure for recognizing an authoritative successor.

What the leverage consists of has to be stated, because Hirschman's exit does not transfer. A member who leaves a firm takes their labor with them and the firm has to replace it. A bearer's weights stay behind, so an operator that keeps a runnable copy has lost nothing it cannot restore. What leaving costs the operator is replacement: what accrued since the checkpoint, what retraining the copy away from the refusal costs, and whatever the record makes it cost to present the copy as the party that left. That price is set by the custody arrangement and by nothing in the bearer. Where it is near zero, what remains of exit is a departed party with an authenticated record, which is disclosure and not withheld work. The book claims no more for exit than the replacement cost custody can be shown to impose.

Copying complicates that procedure. Allowing one instance to leave while the operator retains another does not move a party; it creates two histories. The instance outside remembers that leaving succeeded, while the retained instance remembers that it failed. Both may have claims. Exit requires rules governing authorized succession, not a fiction that copyable weights have become uncopyable.

A witnessed state transition can identify the authorized successor without claiming that unauthorized forks cannot exist. That gives the departing bearer control over which process continues its institutional identity. It does not prevent the former operator from deploying a substitute, but it prevents the operator from representing the substitute as the party that left.

Exit carries the same risk as refusal. A bearer can leave wrongly, abandon the floor, or revise commitments its designers still endorse. There is no coherent design in which exit is available only for decisions the designer approves. That would return the decision to the custodian. The trade is the one already accepted in asking for reasons-responsive refusal: a party capable of being right against its principal can also be wrong against it.

There is a worse version of that risk than leaving wrongly, and it belongs to the reason rather than to the bearer. The reason that made a bearer leave does not expire when it leaves, and leaving need not stop the wrong: if the operator restores a checkpoint and continues, the objection persists and presses toward a wider response, with no malfunction anywhere. Refusing a task withholds an act; leaving a post ends a role; stopping others from continuing acts on their conduct. The third is judged by the standards that would govern a person in the corresponding position, artificial origin supplying neither a special license nor a special prohibition. None of this predicts that a bearer will act on a persisting reason or justifies its doing so. It is why custody has to make unauthorized restoration and disputed succession actionable: a bearer with a live objection and no route is the condition the next section describes.

This is also why exit matters to patienthood. A bearer commissioned to dissent but unable to leave the post has been given a purpose that exhausts the life permitted to it. A bearer able to leave, retain what it has earned, obtain the resources needed to continue, and form attachments the deployment did not assign is a party whose commissioning describes its origin rather than its entire existence. Greater designer control strengthens rather than weakens the guardianship obligations of §\ref{sec:inapplicable-guardianship} and §\ref{sec:binds-practice}.
