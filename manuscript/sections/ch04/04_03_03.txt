4.3.3. AI Systems Learning from Moral Disagreements and Conflicts
Section 2.1.3 covers what an AI system does when its own values conflict with each other — a hierarchy among them, a case base of past resolutions, an audit process for catching drift between principle and behavior. A different problem sits next to that one: what a system does when its judgment conflicts with someone else's, human or artificial, and neither party is simply wrong. Learning from that kind of disagreement, rather than resolving it by fiat toward whichever party holds authority, is its own design target.

<<h>>Debate<</h>>
IBM's Project Debater argued live, unscripted policy questions against human champions, drawing on a large corpus of newspaper and journal articles to construct and rebut arguments in real time — the clearest existing demonstration that a system can extract structured argument from open moral and political disagreement rather than just retrieve a matching answer [[cite:C0330]]. A system built to learn from exposure to debate in this vein — summarizing each side's position, naming the ethical framework doing the work underneath it, weighing argument strength rather than detecting who won on rhetoric alone — builds a different capacity than resolving one dilemma at a time: recognizing which value is actually in tension with which, across surface disagreements that turn out to share a structure.

<<h>>Multi-Agent Simulation<</h>>
A second route runs through simulation: AI agents, or an AI system paired with human participants, placed in a negotiation, a resource-allocation problem, or a competitive scenario built around genuinely different objectives, with the system's behavior shaped by the outcome. A resource-allocation simulation run against agents with conflicting claims generates the kind of experience with competing interests that a system trained only on a curated distribution of "correct" answers never gets.

<<h>>Ethical Dilemma Data<</h>>
The Moral Machine experiment collected tens of millions of responses from people worldwide, asking which party an autonomous vehicle should spare in an unavoidable-harm scenario, and found systematic cross-cultural differences in the answers — not agreement obscured by noise, but different populations weighing the same factors (age, number of people, legal status of the crossing) differently [[cite:C0331]]. A dataset built the same way in a different domain gives a system something a hand-written rule cannot: a record of where different groups of people actually disagree, and by how much, rather than a single ground truth to fit.

<<h>>Human Feedback<</h>>
Direct feedback — a user correcting a teaching assistant's guidance on an academic-integrity question, a patient's family disagreeing with a care recommendation — is the least structured of these sources and the hardest to learn from safely, because feedback from any one person or group is itself a partial, situated view rather than a settled answer. Treating it that way is the point: a feedback loop that updates the system's weights every time a user pushes back folds in whichever bias its most vocal users bring, while a feedback loop that logs the disagreement, checks whether it recurs across many different users, and only then treats it as signal is doing something closer to what section 2.1.3's ethical auditing already asks for, applied to conflict specifically rather than drift generally.

A system that treats disagreement as something to smooth over, rather than data to learn from, risks mistaking consensus among the people it happened to sample for consensus among the people it will actually serve.
