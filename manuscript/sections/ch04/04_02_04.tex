\subsection{Model-Generated Feedback and Constitutional Approaches}
\label{sec:4.2.4}
Collecting preferences from people is slow, expensive, and hard on the people, for the reasons section~\ref{sec:7.1} sets out. The response has been to generate the feedback with a model instead. Anthropic's Constitutional AI, published by Yuntao Bai and forty-seven coauthors in 2022, runs in two phases: the model critiques and revises its own outputs against a written list of principles, and is fine-tuned on the revisions; then a model rather than a person judges which of two samples is better, and the resulting preferences train the reward signal \autocite{bai2022constitutional}. Human labels identifying harmful outputs are not required anywhere in the loop. What the humans supply is the document.

This is section~\ref{sec:3.1}'s first branch, in production, and it is the clearest instance the book has of it. A constitution is a constraint written down and installed. Everything section~\ref{sec:3.1} says about such a constraint is true of it in the most literal available form: it is a text, held by a party, and that party can revise it between one training run and the next. The revision leaves no trace in the model's behavior that an outside reader could distinguish from a change of taste.

The gain is real, and it is a different gain from the one usually claimed. A written constitution can be published. It can be versioned, quoted, dated, and argued with by people who never saw the training data — which is more than a reward model fitted to a hundred thousand comparisons offers anybody. Section~\ref{sec:9.3.4}'s test is available against it in a way it is not available against an aggregate: has a principle ever been changed against the preference of whoever wrote it, on what evidence, raised by whom. Legibility is not the same property as durability, and this stage delivers the first without the second.

\runin{The loop closes}
There is a second consequence that belongs to chapter~\ref{sec:7} and is easier to see from here. Section~\ref{sec:7.2} describes what happens to rater disagreement when it is compressed into a preference signal. Model-generated feedback takes the model that resulted from that compression and installs it as the judge of the next round. The party whose disagreement was collected is now absent from the channel entirely, and what stands in for it is an artifact of the previous averaging. Nobody designed that as a way of closing a feedback loop against the people it was collected from. It is what you get from an efficiency argument applied twice.

Section~\ref{sec:11.1} carries the evidence on how far constraints trained this way survive deliberate attack, which is the separate question of whether the installed constraint holds against somebody trying to remove it rather than against the party who installed it.
