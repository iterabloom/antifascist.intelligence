\subsection{Pretraining and Instruction Tuning}
\label{sec:4.2.1}
A model is pretrained by learning to predict the next piece of text across a corpus assembled at web scale. Almost everything it will ever know about how people treat each other, it met there first, as text somebody wrote for other reasons. Section~\ref{sec:6.1.1} covers how such a corpus is acquired and what courts have begun to say about the acquisition; what matters here is what a corpus is, considered as a source of values.

A pretrained model completes text and answers nobody. What turns it into something that responds to a request is instruction tuning — supervised fine-tuning on a comparatively small set of demonstrations, written by people hired to write them, showing what a good answer looks like. Long Ouyang and nineteen coauthors reported in 2022 that a 1.3-billion-parameter model tuned this way was preferred by human raters to a 175-billion-parameter model that was not \autocite{ouyang2022training}. Read as a statement about efficiency that result is unremarkable. Read against chapter~\ref{sec:3}’s question it says something else: the stage that sets most of what a user would recognize as the system's character is the small one, the late one, and the cheap one. Whoever wants different character buys a different demonstration set.

\runin{What a corpus and a demonstration set have in common}
Both are aggregates, and that is the whole of the problem for a floor. Pointed at ethics, supervised learning means a body of decisions marked acceptable or not: MIT's Moral Machine project, for one, collected tens of millions of human judgments about who an autonomous vehicle should spare \autocite{awad2018moral}. Every label came from somebody. The model recovers the central tendency of what those people said, and a system trained this way will reproduce a moral consensus rather than stand against one. That chapter's opening argument is that no procedure over what people want yields a floor, because a floor is precisely what has to survive the answer coming back wrong, and a labeled corpus is what people want, written down and counted.

The Moral Machine data makes the limit concrete. Its headline finding was systematic cross-cultural variation: different populations weighed age, numbers and the legal status of the crossing differently, and the differences were structured rather than noise. Fit a model to the aggregate and it encodes a compromise nobody holds. Fit it per region and it encodes each region's answer, including the answers a floor exists to refuse.

The unsupervised version escapes none of this. Clustering over a body of moral judgment surfaces which dilemmas group together and which distinctions do most of the separating, and generative models can build cases no annotator wrote; all of it is a way of finding what is in a corpus, and structure recovered from text is the structure of the text.

So this stage belongs above the floor. It is how a system learns the enormous amount of ordinary moral competence that is a matter of what people think — what counts as rude, what a reasonable delay is, when an explanation is owed. What it cannot supply is the part that has to hold when the labels change.
