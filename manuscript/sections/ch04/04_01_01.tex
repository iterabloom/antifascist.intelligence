\subsection{Cognitive Architectures: Integrating Knowledge, Memory, and Reasoning}
\label{sec:4.1.1}
Two architectures dominate the cognitive-architecture literature. The Adaptive Control of Thought-Rational (ACT-R) framework \autocite{anderson2007human} wires together separate modules — memory retrieval, pattern recognition, decision-making — and lets an AI system learn and reason by connecting them: identifying patterns and relationships between concepts to support problem-solving and decision-making from prior knowledge. The Society of Mind (SoM) architecture \autocite{minsky1986society} takes the opposite premise: cognition arises from the interaction of many simple agents, each performing one function, with intelligence emerging from their collective interaction rather than being designed into any one module. Built this way, a system can show context-sensitivity or adapt to novel situations without anyone having programmed either capability directly.

\begin{esbox}
\boxtitle{Which picture is right?}
ACT-R and Society of Mind answer a design question — how should an engineer organize a system? — and each answer is defensible on those terms. Neither is a claim about how the brain is actually organized, and treating them as rival descriptions of biology, rather than rival strategies for building software, is a category error the literature slides into easily.

The dominant account in contemporary computational neuroscience is neither a pipeline of modules nor a swarm of independent agents. It is a single loop \autocite{friston2009freeenergy}: the brain generates a prediction, compares it against incoming signal, and updates on the error, repeated continuously, at every level, on every kind of input. Perception is that loop pointed at the world, the brain's best guess so far; memory is the loop's store of priors, replayed; attention is not a separate spotlight but the weight the loop assigns to which errors matter; working memory and executive function are that same weighting held active toward a goal, not a separate warehouse or overseer. One mechanism, aimed at different targets, produces what look from the outside like distinct faculties.

This matters for engineering choices. ACT-R's module boundaries and SoM's agent boundaries are conveniences for the programmer; nothing forces an AI system built on either one to place its actual dividing lines where the human brain places its own, because the brain does not place them where either architecture assumes. A system engineered on the predictive-processing model draws its cognitive processes from what the mechanism actually needs, not from the shape of an org chart. Section~\ref{sec:4.1.2} goes further into the specific brain systems this mechanism recruits.

\end{esbox}
What that leaves for a designer is a negative result and a narrow positive one. The negative result is that the module boundaries in either architecture are conveniences for the programmer and carry no authority about where a system's real dividing lines should fall. The positive one is that if a single predictive loop is doing the work, then a commitment is not a component that can be located and removed. It is a standing bias on what the loop predicts and what it treats as an error worth correcting, which is a harder thing to excise and a harder thing to verify — a trade section~\ref{sec:4.1.2} takes up at the point where it becomes a claim about what not to build.
