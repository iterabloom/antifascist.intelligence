4.3.2. Balancing Intuition and Deliberation in AI Decision-Making
If the fast and slow labels do not map cleanly onto untrustworthy and trustworthy, as section 4.3.1 argues, an AI system's moral judgment cannot be built on the assumption that whichever answer survives deliberation is thereby the better one. A more defensible balance treats the two processes as different lenses on the same situation, not as a producer and its editor.

The fast, pattern-matched read of a situation is not simply noise to be filtered out. It is often tracking something a slower calculation would otherwise miss or discount — the difference between pushing someone into a trolley's path with one's own hands and throwing a switch at a distance is a difference in what the agent is doing, not a distraction from an identical arithmetic problem. Some intuitive responses run the other way, though. The psychologist Paul Slovic's research on aid and disaster response found that people's willingness to help falls as the number of people needing help rises, and that a single named, photographed victim draws more donations than a statistic describing thousands of anonymous ones [[cite:C0329]]. That gap between the vivid and the abstract is exactly the kind of intuitive signal deliberation exists to catch — not because intuition is wrong in general, but because caring less as the stakes grow larger is a well-documented, reliably backward pattern.

The design implication: a system that asks two different questions of its own judgment, not one process checking the other by default. First, what did the fast read notice that a from-scratch calculation would not have gone looking for — personal agency, directness of harm, a vulnerable party easy to lose in an aggregate? Second, which of deliberation's own known failure modes — scope insensitivity, in-group bias, the identifiable-victim effect — might be distorting the fast read here? Disagreement between the two is a signal to gather more information or escalate the decision, not an instruction to defer to whichever process ran slower.

<<h>>Medical Diagnosis<</h>>
An AI system supporting a clinician's diagnosis makes the same balance concrete. A pattern-matched first read against prior cases is informative — close to the judgment an experienced clinician makes well — and stripping it out in favor of a from-scratch calculation on every case would throw away signal, not eliminate bias. The deliberate pass earns its place by checking that first read against the specific patient's history for the ways pattern-matching characteristically fails: overweighting a memorable case, underweighting a rare condition the training data underrepresents. Neither pass overrides the other; each checks the other's blind spot.

<<h>>Autonomous Vehicles<</h>>
An autonomous vehicle facing an unavoidable-harm scenario poses the same question at higher stakes. A system hard-coded to take whichever option a slower calculation ranks highest has already made the footbridge case's decision by default — the coolest, least-flinching answer wins because it ran slower, exactly the move section 4.3.1 warns against. The harder, more defensible design specifies in advance which features of a scenario the system should weigh — proximity of harm, the number and vulnerability of the parties involved, whether the vehicle's own action or another actor's created the danger — and requires a fast risk assessment and a check against that specification to agree, or to flag where they do not. That puts the values into the system ahead of time, instead of trusting whichever process runs fastest under pressure to supply them.

Training a system to run this check, rather than a fixed sequence that defers to the slower answer, is itself a learning problem: exposing the system to cases where the fast and deliberate reads disagree, and using techniques like inverse reinforcement learning to infer, from how humans actually resolve those disagreements, which situations call for weighting one process's signal over the other's.
