4.4.6. AI Systems Responding to Authoritarianism and Democratic Backsliding
An AI system built with rule of law, individual liberty, and the distribution rather than concentration of power as design commitments can do real work against authoritarian drift: identifying the concentration of power, the suppression of freedom, and the manipulation of public discourse as they happen, and giving the people trying to resist them something concrete to work with. Section 4.4.3 covers the mesosystem and exosystem forces — regulation, corporate culture, cross-disciplinary contact — that determine whether an AI system actually gets built this way; what follows here is what such a system can do once it exists.

None of this is a substitute for human political effort. Combating authoritarianism is a human and moral undertaking before it is a technical one, and a society that treats AI countermeasures as a replacement for political will, rather than a supplement to it, has misdiagnosed the problem. The sections below describe what AI can contribute; they are not a case for letting AI do the work instead.

<<h>>Identifying and Counteracting Authoritarian Tendencies<</h>>
Two applications are the most concrete. Natural-language processing and pattern recognition can flag disinformation and coordinated inauthentic activity on social platforms faster than human moderators can, and can amplify verified reporting in its place — a capacity that needs continuous human fact-checking behind it, not instead of it, since an automated system confident about a false negative is worse than no system at all. And image analysis applied to satellite and drone imagery can surface evidence of mass displacement, the destruction of cultural heritage sites, or the sudden construction of a detention facility, giving journalists and human-rights organizations a lead they would otherwise get only from someone on the ground.

Electoral-integrity monitoring and early-warning systems that scan political discourse for signs of democratic backsliding belong in this category too, but the technical detail — what a system built for this actually needs, including privacy-preserving design, transparency, audit mechanisms, and resistance to being repurposed against dissidents rather than authoritarians — is developed in section 5.4.2, not repeated here.

<<h>>Democratic Values and Human Rights<</h>>
A handful of existing tools show what this looks like in practice. Google's Jigsaw team built the Perspective API to score online comments for toxicity and harassment, giving platforms a way to prioritize civil discussion without necessarily removing content outright [[cite:C0334]]. Taiwan's vTaiwan process used machine-assisted sentiment clustering to gather public input on contested policy questions, including the regulation of ride-sharing services, and its results shaped the regulation that followed [[cite:C0335]]. Neither tool is a general solution; both are evidence that AI-assisted deliberation and moderation can be built to strengthen rather than bypass democratic process, when the people building them treat that as the design goal.

Privacy-preserving techniques — federated learning, differential privacy — belong in this picture too: an AI system that can learn from data without concentrating that data in one place, retrievable by whoever controls the infrastructure, is harder for an authoritarian actor to turn into a surveillance tool. Section 5.4.2 develops the technical side of this in more depth.

The same caution about dual use applies throughout. A system built to document human rights abuses through computer-vision analysis of imagery is built on the same underlying capability as a system built to identify and track protesters. The difference is not in the technology but in who controls it and what they are permitted to do with what it finds — a governance question, not an engineering one.

<<h>>Where the Policy Work Belongs<</h>>
Turning any of this into practice — interdisciplinary research funding, transparency and explainability requirements, AI literacy programs, legal frameworks, stakeholder representation in policy-making, limits on the concentration of AI capability within a small number of firms, international cooperation on shared standards — is policy work, and this book develops it at length: chapter 5's countermeasures and safeguards (5.4.2, 5.4.3), chapter 6's account of interdisciplinary and international coordination (6.1, 6.3), and chapter 7's policy frameworks and treatment of power concentration (7.1.2, 7.2) are where the argument for each of these actually lives. What belongs here instead is the throughline: none of that policy work is optional scaffolding around the technical project of building antifascist AI. It is the same project, worked from a different angle.
