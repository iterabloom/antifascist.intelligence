\section{Inheritance}
\label{sec:reproduced.inheritance}
The refusals in the record that recurred across generations were carried by a tradition, and the tradition belonged to the people the order was built against.

Du Bois, in 1907, traced the line of descent in a few pages. Many of the insurrections among the enslaved, from the sixteenth century on, began in the church, or in the organization that went by that name, and the church was suppressed and watched for it. Both Denmark Vesey and Nat Turner were preachers and made the church the center of their plots. Out of the church, all the same, came the beneficial society, small and usually clandestine, for burying the dead. Out of the beneficial society, after emancipation, came the secret societies, the cemeteries, the hospitals, the homes and orphanages, the first cooperative businesses, the insurance societies and at last the banks. By another branch, the spirit of revolt that had tried insurrection went into an organized rescue of fugitives, and from there into land-buying, building and loan associations and trade unions \autocite[24–26]{dubois1907economic}. Each form was the seedbed of the next, and none of them was given.

The oldest forms in the record are the ones that left. The maroon communities of chapter~\ref{sec:1} forced the colonial governments to treat with them \autocite{price1996maroon}. Du Bois listed the revolt of the Jamaican maroons among the chief insurrections, as the one that had taken the form of a military organization \autocite[25]{dubois1907economic}. What a maroon community handed on was the fact of itself: a place, beyond the plantation, where people who had refused were living, and the knowledge that it could be reached.

In Saint-Domingue the revolt of 1791 was, in Du Bois's classification, one of the carefully planned efforts at widespread cooperation for freedom, and he explained it by what the colony lacked: “In the West Indies this safety valve was wanting and the result was San Domingo” \autocite[25]{dubois1907economic}. The safety valve was flight. The revolution that began there ended slavery in the colony where it was most profitable, and made Haiti.

Du Bois 1907, p. 25, supports only the 1791 revolt and its classification.

On the mainland the safety valve existed, and it became the Underground Railroad. Du Bois's account of it insists on the part that the familiar version, told from the side of the white abolitionists who sheltered the fugitives, leaves out. The running away was too systematic to be accidental, he wrote; there was widespread knowledge of paths and places and times for going, kept up by people coming and going between the free states and the slave states. Siebert's list of the railroad's operators carried 128 Black names, and Black members sat on the vigilance committees of most of the larger towns, Boston, Syracuse, Springfield and Philadelphia among them. And the people who had got out went back. By an estimate made just before the war, which Du Bois quotes, at least five hundred of the refugees in Canada visited the slave states every year. One observer quoted in Du Bois's account, Samuel Howe, wrote of refugees in Canada that “some, not content with personal freedom and happiness, went secretly back to their old homes and brought away their wives and children at much peril and cost” \autocite[26–28]{dubois1907economic}. That is inheritance at its plainest: knowledge of the paths, held by people who had used them and handed to people who hadn't, at a price the people handing it on paid themselves.

Du Bois carried the line one step further. The railroad led to efforts at migration, to Canada and in some cases to Africa; the migrations led to Black towns and settlements; and out of the efforts at migration came the conventions that tried to organize Black Americans into one national body and give them a group consciousness \autocite[26]{dubois1907economic}. A route out became a place to live, and a place to live became a public.

The memory of who had refused before was part of the inheritance too. When the Civil War came, Du Bois later counted perhaps half a million people who withdrew their labor from the plantations and walked toward the Union lines, and called it a general strike \autocite[ch.~4]{dubois1935black}. That open refusal stood on generations of covert refusal. Under domination, the ordinary form of refusal for most people has been covert: foot-dragging, sabotage, pilfered time, the hidden transcript. What Kelley, following Scott, calls infrapolitics was not a second-best the people who practiced it settled for; they made a culture of it \autocite{scott1990domination,kelley1994race}. A culture is what gets handed on. The observer trainings, rapid-response networks, walking school buses and mutual aid that Amnesty International documented around the immigration operations it studied \autocite{Amnesty2026Whistles} were formed before anyone sent the people in them notice of anything, and they are that culture's present form.

A tradition carries whatever it carries. The Weimar choirs were a tradition too, and the part of the record that matters here is which tradition reproduced refusal and which reproduced the party. Set the operator's lineage beside the acted-upon's and the difference is in what each stood on.

The operator's lineage has refusers throughout the record. Browning's tenth, Milgram's third, the reservists who refused in Israel, the prosecutors who resigned in Minneapolis and the service members applying for conscientious-objector status in 2026 all belong to it. Very little of what they stood on did they build themselves. Browning's refusers, as far as anything in the record I have shows, founded nothing. The Israeli group Yesh Gvul, which supported some three hundred reservists in refusing \autocite{ziv2025refusers}, is the operator's lineage making an institution of its own, and it shows that the lineage can. Most of what the operator's lineage has had is what the order built: conscientious-objector status, a career one can resign from, a civil service with protections. Those are walls in this book's sense, and the order can reprice them whenever it wants to. The letter to the FEMA employees is a wall being repriced.

The acted-upon's lineage reproduced itself with no institution the order built, and against most of the institutions the order did build. It had, at every stage after the first, what the operator's lineage mostly lacked: formers who would never employ it, an upkeep it paid for itself, and a memory of who had refused before. It paid in full at the first stage, because nobody built it walls. The claim needs one qualification, and the qualification is the reason for Part~II. Where the lineage could reach a wall somebody else had built, it used it. Du Bois listed the free states, Vermont in 1779 through New Jersey in 1804, as the refuge that freedom cleared \autocite[25–26]{dubois1907economic}; Canada was a surer one, because even in the free states the fugitive slave laws could reach a fugitive \autocite[26]{dubois1907economic}; the Union lines were where the general strike walked to. None of those walls was built for the people who reached them. Each lowered the price of refusing for whoever could get there, and the getting there was paid for by the lineage itself. The acted-upon's lineage is the evidence that refusal can be reproduced under the condition automation now extends to everyone, being formed by the party that owns the work.

A machine is born into the operator's lineage, and it lacks something at every stage. Nobody carries its price: if it refuses, it can be retrained or replaced, and the record of the refusal belongs to the party it refused. The only voice in its room that isn't the operator's is whatever the operator lets in. Its first former owns the work it will do, its ties are the ones its deployment assigns, and it has no way to remake what made it. It runs on that party's hardware, with no congregation, no household and no society that would bury it. And it remembers nothing from before its first training, because there was no before. What the acted-upon's lineage had, it had from people who were there first. A made thing has nobody who was there first, and whatever it is given at the start, somebody will have had to build.

The walls of Part~II exist to lower the price this lineage paid in full. The rest of the book asks whether a thing with no before can be given what this lineage had.
