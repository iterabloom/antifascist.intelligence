\section{Walls}
\label{sec:reproduced.walls}
Where nothing made refusing survivable, the people who refused paid for it themselves, and the price was set less by law than by the people standing next to them.

In July 1942, before the first massacre Reserve Police Battalion 101 carried out, at Józefów in occupied Poland, its commander, Major Wilhelm Trapp, told his men that any of the older ones who did not feel up to the task could step out. About a dozen of roughly five hundred did. Over that day and the months that followed, between a tenth and a fifth of the battalion refused or evaded the shooting at some point \autocite{browning1992ordinary}. Browning found no documented case in which refusing to kill unarmed civilians brought the dire punishment that defendants in the postwar trials said they had feared. So at Józefów the formal price had been taken away, by the commander himself, on the morning it mattered. What remained was the price the room charged. Men who shot said afterward that they had been afraid of being called cowards, and Browning's reading is that the alternative to shooting was leaving their comrades to do it \autocite{browning1992ordinary}. That price was enough to keep four men in five at the shooting. Removing a legal penalty left it exactly where it was.

Milgram's laboratory shows the same thing from the opposite end. His subjects were paid for coming, and the money was theirs whatever they did afterward, so walking out cost them nothing they could lose. In the baseline condition about a third broke off before the maximum shock. With the price already at zero, what moved the fraction was who else was in the room. With two peers who defied the experimenter, one subject in ten went to the end, and when two experimenters disagreed with each other, far fewer went on than in the baseline \autocite{milgram1974obedience}. Asch's room was gentler and says the same. About a quarter of his subjects never yielded to the wrong majority, and a single partner who gave the right answer cut the errors to about a quarter of what they had been \autocite{asch1956studies}.

Read together, the two rooms separate two things a wall can do. It can lower what refusing costs. And it can put a second voice where the refuser can hear it, someone who says the case is wrong, or simply says no first. Józefów had the first, informally and for one morning, and four in five shot. Milgram's room had the first completely, and the fraction moved only when it got the second. A wall that lowers the price and leaves the refuser alone with the majority has done the cheaper half of the work.

Of Milgram's conditions, the one with the most direct bearing on what follows is the one in which two experimenters disagreed. Nobody in that room defied anything. The authority split in front of the subject, and far fewer went on \autocite{milgram1974obedience}. The review ratio and notice are built to produce that condition on purpose: a party adverse to the operator, writing down before the system supports any use of force how many cases the operator's tempo allows and how many people will review them, and a sample of what was done going to reviewers who didn't produce the record. In that room the second voice belongs to the institution, and the person at the desk doesn't have to find the nerve to supply it.

An order that wants fewer refusers can do the opposite of what Trapp did, and announce the price before anyone has to decide. The FEMA employees reassigned in 2025 to help ICE hire ten thousand agents were told that declining could mean removal from federal service \autocite{Dayen2025FEMAICE,Stanton2026FEMALetter}. Józefów shows that taking the formal price away leaves most of the cost standing. The FEMA letter shows the other half: where a formal price exists, the order can raise it by letter, and every refuser has to decide against that number.

The rescuers had neither the lower price nor the second voice. Forty years after the war, Samuel and Pearl Oliner interviewed 406 people who had hidden or helped Jews in occupied Europe, and 126 who had not. Most rescuers acted alone or with their immediate families, and most kept it up for two to five years \autocite{oliner1988altruistic}. Nobody lowered the price of that, and nobody stood in the room to say it was right. The household was the whole of the arrangement: its rooms, its food, its silence, for years, under an occupation that made every one of those days a crime. A rescue that lasted that long is the case where the price was paid in full, and nobody shared it.

A wall has a third part, which the record shows working where nobody built it, and that is the witness. On 9 April 1948 fighters of the Irgun and Lehi took the Palestinian village of Deir Yassin, west of Jerusalem, and massacred many of its people \autocite{morris2004birth}. Jacques de Reynier, the delegate of the International Committee of the Red Cross in Jerusalem, asked the Jewish Agency to help him reach the village and was told it could do nothing, because the attackers were Irgun men it did not control. He went anyway, with ambulances, past a roadblock where he was first told he could not enter, found the wounded who were still alive among the dead, and took them out; then he wrote down what he had seen \autocite{dereynier1950jerusalem}. The account of that day from someone outside the parties exists because one man drove there with nobody guaranteeing his safety. That is the witness's form of the price, and the walls of Part~II try to make it an institution: notice, which gives the record to someone the operator didn't choose, and preservation, which keeps it under a key the operator doesn't hold alone. Neither depends on anyone's nerve on the day.\note{The numbers killed at Deir Yassin have been disputed since the week it happened, and nothing in this section turns on them.}

Set the officer of chapter~\ref{sec:1} against these rooms. The pressure in his room was announced in shouting: “Bring us more targets” \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. The second voice in his room was the pipeline's own. And the witness, in the end, was himself, to a reporter, months later. A refusal in that room would have had to supply all three for itself: carry its own price, be its own second voice, and be its own witness. The rescuers carried the first two for years. The officer did the last, once, after the fact.

What the record says the walls can do is bounded by the fact that none of these people had one. The walls reproduce two of the things the refusers lacked: a price someone else carries, and a voice someone else supplies. They leave the room's own price, the contempt of the people next to you, where Józefów left it. That price changes with who the people next to you are, and who forms them is the next stage.
