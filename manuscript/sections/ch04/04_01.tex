\section{Incorporating Human Cognitive Models in AI Systems for Ethical Decision-Making}
\label{sec:4.1}
A system that is going to hold anything under pressure has to notice the pressure. It has to register what is in front of it, hold why a constraint exists long enough to see that the reason has died, and keep its attention on the part of a situation that a well-designed process would rather it skipped. Those are perception, memory and attention, and they are the subject of this section — not as a tour of cognitive science, but because each one has a failure mode that a system cannot detect in itself, and a floor is exactly the thing that fails silently.

Section~\ref{sec:4.1.1} takes up how a system's parts should be divided, and argues that the standard architectures divide them where no brain does. Section~\ref{sec:4.1.2} is the long one and the load-bearing one: the findings from neuroscience that change a design decision, each with the decision stated. Section~\ref{sec:4.1.3} covers what happens when judgment is carried into a situation it was not built for, which is the case a floor will actually meet.
