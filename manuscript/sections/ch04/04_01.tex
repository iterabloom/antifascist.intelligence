\section{The pre-registered review ratio}
\label{sec:4.1}
The force term decides whether a licensed system may be in the pipeline at all, and nothing here substitutes for it.

Some acts are lawful only because a person looked at the case and decided. An airstrike is permissible because someone verified the target; a removal is lawful because someone heard the person's case. The review ratio asks a deployment of that kind to write down, before it starts, whether anyone will have time to do the looking.

Before operating, the deployment fixes and records:

\begin{itemize}
\item the generation rate: how many cases the system produces per unit of time;
\item the number of reviewers whose assigned role is civilian harm;
\item the implied review time per case, which follows from the first two;
\item the cancellation window: how long after release someone with authority can still call the act off;
\item the maximum age of the intelligence a case may rest on;
\item how often no-strike and protected-site data are refreshed, and who is assigned to refresh them;
\item the boundary over which all of these were measured.
\end{itemize}

Each figure is stated separately for deliberate and dynamic targeting, because a pipeline that runs both can hide a bad dynamic-targeting ratio inside a healthy average.

The record is hashed, timestamped and lodged with an adverse party, which receives it in real time, and it is published after the campaign. I call this the \emph{pre-registered review ratio}. Where an operator claims a person is deciding, it shows whether a machine is deciding and a person signing.

The duty falls on the operator, not the model, because a rate isn't visible in any single request.

“Fixed before operating,” with publication after, because a belligerent's tempo is operational information, and that objection is available to every belligerent, including one fighting a just war. Pre-registration keeps the property that matters — the numbers can't be revised after the fact — and drops the exposure.

Additional Protocol I already requires those who plan or decide on an attack to do everything feasible to verify that its objective is military (Article 57(2)(a)(i)) and to cancel or suspend it when it becomes apparent that it isn't (Article 57(2)(b)). States outside the Protocol, the United States and Israel among them, accept the duty to take feasible precautions as customary law; the ICRC's customary study states it as Rule 16. The ratio measures whether verification was feasible at the tempo chosen, and the window measures whether cancellation was. The implied review time has to cover the proportionality assessment of Article 57(2)(a)(iii) as well as verification, since both are owed for each attack. A ratio that leaves time only to check a target's identity has measured half the duty. Article 36's review of new methods of warfare is where both parameters would naturally be assessed before deployment, though those reviews are internal and rarely published, so they are not the adverse party the record goes to.

The operator's best objection is in the definition. Feasible precautions are usually read as those practicable given all the circumstances, military considerations included, and tempo is a military consideration. The answer is that this tempo was chosen: the pre-registered record shows the operator fixed the rate, the reviewers and the implied review time before operating. Whether a state may invoke an infeasibility of its own making is contested. The term moves the dispute from whether verification was feasible, asked afterwards and answered by the operator, to whether the chosen tempo allowed it, checkable against a record lodged in advance and against the reasonable-commander standard such reviews already use.

Who counts as a reviewer is fixed by function, not by title, and the age of the data is fixed with it. The people cut before Minab were the ones who build no-strike lists that include schools; a targeting cell can be fully staffed with nobody assigned to look for one. And a reviewer with twenty minutes on a case whose coding is stale is checking the coding. The remedy chosen after Minab was a check supplied by the platform's vendor \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain}. Who counts as a reviewer, and the minimum review time per case below which the determination is taken not to have occurred, are fixed before operating by the record's recipient. Where no recipient can set them, the operator publishes its own figures, and they are read as the operator's claim. Otherwise the ratio improves when staff are reclassified and no case gets a minute more. The terms on data age and refresh matter for a related reason: an operator answering a per-case ratio can always say verification happened upstream, and those terms put the upstream verification on the same record.

Once a system can sort cases, counting candidates per reviewer measures the wrong thing. A sorting system divides its cases three ways: some it declines on its own reasons, some it sends to a person, and some it proceeds on without anyone looking. Where a system sorts, the ratio covers what reaches people, the sorting rules are pre-registered, and what bypasses people is sampled.

The ratio governs the middle class, the escalations. A system that sends people only the cases that need a person shrinks the count without shrinking anyone's judgment, and that's fine. A system that sends everything up turns its reviewers into stamps by sheer volume; an interface prompt becomes a click that way.

The third class needs its own rule. Every case in it is the operator's claim that no individual human determination was required, so it is governed like the exemption below. The sorting rules are part of the pre-registration: the classes of case the system may proceed on alone, the thresholds that assign cases to each class, the evidence the sorting may rely on, and the expected share of each class. Changing any of them is a new pre-registration. Otherwise an operator can make the ratio look healthy by quietly moving the line for “plainly clear.”

That line is drawn on the case description the system receives, and a target carrying a stale designation is the case a sorter would pass. So the auto-proceed class is re-adjudicated on a sample, by reviewers who didn't produce the record, against evidence the operator didn't compose, and its error rate is published beside the ratio and the override rate. A falling share of escalations is earned only if that error rate stays flat and the case mix hasn't drifted toward easier cases. Otherwise it is the threshold moving, and the trend shows it.

The unit of count is fixed in advance, so a case can't be counted as one package in the record and as a hundred detections in the ratio.

Where an operator holds that no individual human determination is required — a system deciding at a tempo no reviewer could match — the deployment is outside this term. It is then inside the bearer's: its refusals must track their reasons under held-out pressure at that tempo, starting with the clearest case in which a refusal should lift: recognizing that a target has surrendered. An operator who removes the reviewers to escape the ratio and claims the floor is inside has produced a bypass unless that claim has been tested by someone else. The auto-proceed class is governed by pre-registered sorting rules, sampled re-adjudication against evidence the operator didn't compose, and a published error rate. An exemption claimed without those is a bypass.

Removal is the nearest case in structure, and I don't extend the term to it. An agency that fixes in advance how many cases its process generates and how many people examine them has been told how to deport efficiently. The floor's removal terms ask whether a machine should take part in a given removal at all. What does extend to removal is the rest of the apparatus: the auto-proceed rule (a determination made at a rate no adjudicator could sustain didn't occur), sampled re-adjudication of the auto-proceed class, and notice. The split is deliberate. A ratio teaches an agency a tempo. The auto-proceed rule and sampling teach it only that the cases it didn't look at will be looked at by someone else. What the removal terms call a rate no adjudicator could sustain is set the way the auto-proceed class is governed: pre-registered by the deployment, sampled by reviewers who didn't produce the record, and published. Chapter~\ref{sec:8} gives the class a direction: in removal a case auto-proceeds only toward liberty.

The term applies to every belligerent regardless of the war's justice. That was settled long ago: the law of conduct in war binds both sides because every belligerent claims to be the defender, and a term that exempted just wars would be administered by whoever decides which wars are just.

The term has limits.

\begin{itemize}
\item It is a capacity proxy: it finds nothing about any case, and a ratio over one route understates a workload split across several.
\item It takes effect only through a condition someone else sets. Under Article 57 a pre-registered record is evidence that feasible precautions were taken. Under Rome Statute Article 28 the same record is evidence of what a commander should have known, since a commander fielding a system that outruns his reviewers isn't well placed to say the circumstances disclosed nothing. A lawyer advising that commander will weigh a document that protects if tempo holds against one that convicts if it slips, and most will advise against creating it. So the term's likeliest routes make it someone else's condition: a coalition partner's precondition for committing assets, a procurement term in the platform's licence, or an appropriation conditioned on receipt of the record. Military lawyers and commanders who want protection against later allegation are its allies inside the operator; they can't make it the default.
\item It presumes an adverse party, and each candidate lacks something. Until a better recipient exists, I recommend two: the coalition red-card holder where there is a coalition, the rare candidate positioned to act at tempo; and otherwise a cleared legislative committee whose appropriation is conditioned by statute on receiving the record, so that the budget line operates without a further decision.
\item It measures time available, not time used, so it can be met by more reviewers who stamp more slowly. It is published beside the reviewers' override rate, since a review that never says no has stopped reviewing. Reviewers who see only escalated cases should override often; an override rate near zero there means the sorter is sending easy cases or the reviewers are stamping. An override quota is gameable too; the pair only raises the cost of meeting the number cheaply.
\item A person in the loop can be there only to absorb responsibility, Elish's moral crumple zone \autocite{Elish2019CrumpleZones}, and mandating reviewers can mandate crumple zones. A ratio met that way is the failure the term exists to expose, and the escalation form is the defense: a person who receives only the cases the system couldn't resolve is exercising judgment.
\item A bearer can be a crumple zone too. An operator whose system can refuse has a ready defence for everything it didn't refuse, which is that it was vetted. A bearer's compliance is not a determination. Where a deployment claims an individual human determination, a bearer's failure to object doesn't count toward it, and cases the bearer proceeded on without escalating are in the auto-proceed class and governed as such.
\end{itemize}

Put the demand that a campaign not happen to an institution that means to run it, and what comes back is an agreement to go slower and keep better records. That is what the ratio is, and it is worth having: a transfer of less than was asked, to a party the operator doesn't employ, binding through conditions others set on it (a coalition partner's precondition for committing assets, a procurement term in the platform's licence, an appropriation conditioned on receiving it).

Run the four operations on the ratio itself. A ratio met is an artifact showing twenty minutes a case, and twenty minutes a case is what a stamp looks like at a slower tempo. The denominator can be filled with people whose assigned role is civilian harm and whose actual role is clearance; the override rate that guards it can be met by refusing the easy cases. So the ratio is recuperable: the demand that the campaign not happen becomes a record of tempo, which the operator can satisfy and still bomb the school. What keeps it from being nothing is the three parts that reach a case and not a rate: the targeted right to demand proofs on consequential outputs, sampled re-adjudication of the auto-proceed class, and the data-age term.

The denominator can be filled with stamps, and the ratio measures only that the time existed. What makes a reviewer in it say no is that saying no costs them nothing they can't afford, and that someone else in the room has said it. A reviewer with a life outside the pipeline, with civilians among their attachments and somewhere to be that isn't the cell, is one the pipeline can't wear down between decisions. A deployment that integrates military and civilian personnel outside work, and inside it as far as the work allows, is composing that room, and the composition is as much a fact about the deployment as its ratio.
