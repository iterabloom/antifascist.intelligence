\chapter{How Refusal Was Reproduced}
\label{ch:reproduced}
People built refusal-reproducing institutions even under a totalitarian order hostile to them doing so. That is the evidence that it can be done, and that there is no outside of institutions or of power, only a choice of whose.

Browning's policemen, Milgram's subjects, the Oliners' rescuers, the maroons, and the people who ran the Underground Railroad were not produced by any institution the order designed, and most of them refused against the institutions that governed them. So the record can be asked: what did they have, at each of the four stages, and who supplied it?

The stages are the Introduction's. Something makes refusing survivable, or the whole price falls on whoever refuses. Somebody forms the refuser before the occasion, because nobody in the room when it comes can learn integrity in twenty seconds. Something keeps the refuser going between one decision and the next, because a refusal made once and then worn down is only a delay. And somebody hands the refusal to people who weren't there, or each generation starts from nothing. The rest of the book builds them in that order, walls first. The record supplied them in nearly the reverse order. For most of the people in it the last three stages came first, from their own people, and the first came, when it came at all, from somebody else's order.

Two cautions about the evidence. The record holds the refusals that left a record: a policeman's statement to a postwar investigator, a rescuer still alive forty years later to be interviewed, a burial society whose dues and dead somebody counted. The refusers who were killed before anyone wrote them down are missing from it, and so are the societies that were found out. The record therefore overstates how often refusal survived, and it can't say how often refusal was tried. And the evidence is mixed in kind: two laboratory rooms, a police battalion, a survey of rescuers, a Red Cross delegate's account of one village, histories of movement schools and of a sharecroppers' union, a compilation of Black economic history Du Bois published in 1907.

There are two lineages in it. The operator's is the officer's: people inside an apparatus, asked to do its work, some of whom said no. The acted-upon's is the lineage of the people the apparatus was built to sort. The Introduction said the acted-upon's lineage reproduced itself with no institution the order built. I test and qualify that claim one stage at a time. This analysis yields a list of what each stage needed. A synthetic autonomous system starts with nothing on the list. Actually, most humans start with a list which, if not completely empty, is severely deficient. The record can't show that the list can be supplied by design. It shows what a design would have to supply.
