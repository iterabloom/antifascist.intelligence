4.4.1. Applying the Moral Ecosystem Approach to AI Development
Applying this approach to AI development means tracing the environmental influences that shape how an AI system learns, behaves, and forms its ethical dispositions, level by level, the way Bronfenbrenner traced them for a developing child.

At the microsystem level — an AI system's immediate environment, made up of its data sources, its code, and its relationships with the developers and users it interacts with directly — a development team's own diversity of viewpoint shapes what the system is built to notice and to value in the first place. Section 4.4.2 takes this level up in detail.

At the mesosystem level — the connections between those microsystem components — consistency matters more than any one component does on its own. An AI system whose ethical principles are interpreted one way by its developers and another way by its policy team has, in effect, no consistent principles at all; sustained contact between AI researchers, ethicists, and policymakers is what keeps that interpretation aligned. Section 4.4.3 develops this level and the exosystem together.

At the exosystem level — regulation, industry standard, corporate culture, the structures an AI system is embedded in without touching directly — these external forces can push development toward authoritarian uses as easily as away from them. Which direction they push depends on whether regulation and corporate incentive are built to notice the difference, not on anything intrinsic to the technology.

At the macrosystem level — the cultural, political, and economic context an AI system is built and deployed inside — building toward democratic values and human rights takes more than any one team's intent. Cross-border collaboration, shared ethical standards, and public AI literacy all affect whether that intent survives contact with a wider system that may not share it. Section 4.4.4 takes this level up directly, and 4.4.5 adds what time contributes on top of it.

None of these levels operates alone, and none is sufficient by itself. A microsystem staffed by a genuinely diverse team still answers to a macrosystem's incentives; a well-drafted regulation still depends on a mesosystem willing to interpret it consistently. The sections that follow take each level in turn, but the reason to trace them at all is that an AI system's ethical trajectory is set by how they interact, not by any one of them alone.
