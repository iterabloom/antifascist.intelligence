\subsection{Human-Like Learning and Generalization in AI Systems}
\label{sec:4.1.3}
People learn and generalize from very few examples, and AI systems that approach this extend judgment to situations they were never explicitly trained on instead of needing a full retraining cycle for every new context. The technique inventory changes yearly; the evaluation problem underneath it does not.

Few-shot learning aims at models that classify or predict accurately from a handful of examples, and for an ethical AI system the appeal is direct: judgment would not need retraining from scratch for every new scenario, but could extend to a new case the way a person's existing values apply to situations they have never seen. A system deployed in disaster recovery has to work from a handful of relevant precedents rather than a dataset built for the specific flood in front of it; a system built to flag authoritarian speech patterns has to extend to new phrasings and new languages without a corpus assembled in advance for every regime it might encounter. Large pretrained language models demonstrate the capacity at system scale, performing a task from a few examples in the prompt with no parameter update at all \autocite{brown2020fewshot}. That result is now the smaller half of how a deployed system comes by its behavior: section~\ref{sec:4.2.1} covers the tuning stages that do most of it, and what a few examples in a prompt can still shift is what those stages left open.

The problem this leaves is an evaluation problem rather than a modeling one. A handful of examples is easy to overfit, moral dilemmas do not arrive in clean representative sets, and telling whether a system's few-shot ethical judgment generalized — or merely matched surface features of the examples it saw — is hard. Building that test is not a machine-learning task alone; it needs people trained in ethics to say what the system was supposed to have learned. Section~\ref{sec:4.2.9} takes the same difficulty up for transfer, where a floor carried into a setting it was not built for cannot tell that it has stopped applying.

A system built for empathy and altruism can also follow a developmental arc rather than being assembled at once: perception and elementary problem-solving first, then the integration of those skills into something that guides a decision, then specialization to a particular setting and the people in it. That ordering is borrowed carefully, and section~\ref{sec:5.1.1} sets out what the developmental stage theories will and will not carry.
