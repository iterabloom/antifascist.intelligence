\subsection{Human-Like Learning and Generalization in AI Systems}
\label{sec:4.1.3}
People learn and generalize from very few examples. AI systems that approach this — through few-shot learning, transfer learning, and a developmental trajectory modeled on human cognitive growth — extend judgment to situations they were never explicitly trained on, instead of needing a full retraining cycle for every new context. What matters here is not the technique inventory, which changes yearly, but two properties of transferred judgment that a designer has to plan around.

\runin{Few-shot learning, and the evaluation problem underneath it}
Few-shot learning aims at models that classify or predict accurately from a handful of examples. For an ethical AI system the appeal is direct: empathy, altruism, and ethical judgment would not need retraining from scratch for every new scenario, but could extend to a new case the way a person's existing values apply to situations they have never seen. A system deployed in disaster recovery has to work from a handful of relevant precedents rather than a dataset built for the specific flood in front of it. A system built to flag authoritarian speech patterns has to extend to new phrasings and new languages without a corpus assembled in advance for every regime it might encounter.

Several technique families support this — external memory a network can write to without retraining, meta-learning that extracts a strategy for adapting across many tasks rather than one task's solution \autocite{finn2017modelagnostic}, and similarity scoring against known examples instead of direct classification. Large pretrained language models demonstrate the same capacity at system scale, performing a task from a few examples in the prompt with no parameter update at all \autocite{brown2020fewshot}.

The problem this leaves is the one that matters for ethics, and it is an evaluation problem rather than a modeling one. A handful of examples is easy to overfit, moral dilemmas do not arrive in clean representative sets, and telling whether a system's few-shot ethical judgment actually generalized — or merely matched surface features of the examples it saw — is genuinely hard. Building that test is not a machine-learning task alone. It needs people trained in ethics to say what the system was supposed to have learned.

\runin{Transfer, and the failures that arrive silently}
Transfer learning carries knowledge from a source task into a related target, so a system does not learn the second from nothing. Domain adaptation is the special case where the task stays fixed and the data shifts. Both matter concretely for the systems in this book: a model trained to parse legal arguments in one jurisdiction can be adapted to flag rights violations in another, and a model that recognizes one kind of surveillance overreach can be adapted to the same pattern in an unfamiliar setting. In practice this mostly happens through fine-tuning a broadly trained model on a narrower corpus \autocite{devlin2018bert}.

Two failure modes deserve the attention the techniques usually get. Negative transfer occurs when source and target are similar enough to invite the connection and not similar enough to share anything useful, and forcing it degrades the target task rather than accelerating it. Domain shift is the subtler version: a driving-assistance system trained in daylight performs markedly worse at night because the visual statistics it learned no longer hold.

What makes both dangerous is the silence. Nothing in the transfer process warns a system that its assumptions have stopped applying, and the confident wrong answer is indistinguishable at the interface from the confident right one. This is the same property section~\ref{sec:4.1.2} identified in attention and in perception, arriving a third time: the system's blind spot is not visible from inside the system. For an ethical deployment, that means a transferred judgment needs an external check tied to the domain it moved into, and cannot be validated by asking how confident the model is.

\runin{Developmental trajectory}
Human development moves through recognizable stages, and a system built for empathy and altruism can follow a comparable arc rather than being assembled at once: perception and elementary problem-solving first, then the integration of those separate skills into something that guides a decision rather than only labels an input, then specialization to a particular setting and the people in it.

That ordering is borrowed carefully, and chapter~\ref{sec:5} says how carefully. Section~\ref{sec:5.1.1} sets out what the developmental stage theories will and will not carry — an order is not a mechanism, and nothing here should be read as a claim that a system must pass through these stages or that arriving at the last one is maturity.
