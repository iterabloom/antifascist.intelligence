4.1.2. Biologically-Inspired AI: Gleanings from Neuroscience and Cognitive Psychology
Backpropagation, the mechanism that trains most modern neural networks, is a distant cousin of synaptic weight adjustment in the brain: both strengthen and weaken connections in response to error. That distant-cousin relationship — real but partial, useful but easy to overstate — runs through everything below. Getting it right matters for more than accuracy: an AI system built on a wrong model of how a mind works will fail in ways that are hard to predict from the wrong model alone.

This section is selective on purpose. There is a great deal of good cognitive neuroscience that has no design consequence I can name, and rehearsing it here would pad the chapter without changing what anyone would build. What follows are the findings that change a design decision, and in each case the decision is stated.

<<h>>What the architecture borrows, and where the borrowing stops<</h>>
Artificial neural networks are the foundation of most contemporary AI systems: artificial neurons in layers, a weight on each connection, adjusted during training to reduce the gap between predicted and actual output. Convolutional networks, tuned for grid-structured data like images, borrow the visual cortex's layered escalation from edges and contrast toward objects and motion — which is why they do well at classification and detection. Recurrent architectures carry a hidden state forward through sequences. Spiking and neuromorphic designs push closer to the brain's actual signaling, chasing the energy efficiency of firing only when there is something to say.

The borrowing stops at a specific place, and it is worth marking because the rest of this section keeps arriving at it. Cortical processing is not one-directional. Feedback connections carry information back down the hierarchy, so that higher-level context reshapes how a lower-level signal is interpreted. A system built only for the feedforward pass has given up the mechanism that lets biological vision handle ambiguous and noisy input, and it will fail on exactly that input.

One borrowing that did not survive scrutiny is worth keeping visible. A mirror-neuron-style matching mechanism was once treated as the biological basis of empathy. Section 2.2.1 takes up why that claim does not hold. What survives is more modest and still useful — a system that maps an observed action onto its own action repertoire supports imitation learning and gesture recognition, with no claim about what it is like to have one.

<<h>>Attention decides what is seen, which makes its failures blind spots<</h>>
Ask people to count basketball passes on a video and about half of them will not notice a person in a gorilla costume walk into frame, face the camera, and thump their chest for nine seconds [[cite:C0269]]. The effect is not a laboratory curiosity, and it does not spare expertise: radiologists searching lung images for nodules miss a gorilla deliberately embedded in the scan 83 percent of the time [[cite:C0270]].

The design consequence turns on how attention is modeled. If attention is a resource allocator bolted onto a system that has already seen everything, its failures are inefficiencies. If attention is the selection that decides what gets perceived at all, its failures are blind spots — and a system that has learned which regions of its input reward processing has also learned which regions to be blind to. The human case establishes the property that makes this dangerous: the blind spot is invisible from inside. The viewers who missed the gorilla did not experience a gap.

Self-attention in transformer architectures has the same shape [[cite:C0272]]. A model that weights some tokens heavily weights others near zero, and nothing in the architecture announces which relevant thing was assigned a low weight. Attention schema theory pushes this one step further: a system that models its own attention — a simplified internal representation of what it is attending to and what it will attend to next — acquires the materials for a form of self-awareness [[cite:C0273]]. What such a schema tracks is not the world but the system's own relation to it, with the simplifications and failure modes definitional to any model, which is section 2.3.3's subject.

For a system meant to act ethically, the requirement is that it treat the regions it has learned to discount as an always-open question rather than as that which, as Westworld's Hosts often remarked, "doesn't look like anything to me".

<<h>>Perception is inference, so the priors are the exposure<</h>>
Perception is not passive registration. Predictive coding makes the point precise: the visual cortex sends predictions down from higher, context-rich areas to lower, sensory ones, and the lower areas return only what the predictions failed to anticipate [[cite:C0284]]. What propagates upward is the surprise. Anatomy fits — backward connections outnumber forward ones and carry the predictions, forward connections carry the errors. Anil Seth's phrase for the result is controlled hallucination: ordinary perception is what hallucination would be if sensory data were allowed to constrain it, and the difference between seeing a room and hallucinating one is whether corrections are arriving and being applied [[cite:C0285]].

Two design consequences. The first is about efficiency: the next step for machine perception is a model that generates a prediction and asks only what violates it, instead of reprocessing the whole input at every layer regardless of how much was already expected. Progress on this measure is a better-calibrated set of expectations and a cheaper way of finding where they broke, and not more layers.

The second matters more for this book. A system's perception is only as reliable as the priors it perceives through, so priors trained on skewed or incomplete data will hallucinate in the specific direction of that skew — confidently, and with no internal signal that anything is wrong. Chapter 6's account of bias is this mechanism at work, and it is why bias in a perceptual system cannot be caught by asking the system.

<<h>>Memory is rebuilt, and the capacity number is four<</h>>
Memory is not played back. Frederic Bartlett established this in the 1930s by having people retell an unfamiliar folk tale over days and weeks: they did not lose pieces the way a recording degrades, they remodeled the story, replacing strange details with ordinary ones and drifting toward a tidier version that fit their expectations [[cite:C0286]]. Elizabeth Loftus turned the finding into something with courtroom consequences, showing that the verb used to ask about a filmed car accident changes the speed people report and, a week later, whether they remember broken glass that was never in the film [[cite:C0287]]. Later work implanted whole episodes that never happened. The subjects were not lying. None of this correlates with felt confidence: a vivid, detailed, emotionally charged memory can be substantially manufactured.

This has become load-bearing elsewhere in the book. Section 3.4 asks what it would take to trust what a machine witness reports, and the answer turns on the fact that no witness has an honest memory in the strong sense — which is why human institutions corroborate testimony instead of vetting it.

The nearer design point concerns capacity, and it is a correction. The number most people half-remember from an introductory course — seven items, plus or minus two, from a 1956 study — was already a measure of chunked information, where a phone number becomes three chunks rather than ten digits and a chess master reads a whole position as a handful of familiar patterns [[cite:C0288]]. Control for chunking, so that no language or expertise can pack information into larger units, and the real capacity narrows to about four, plus or minus one [[cite:C0289]]. The two numbers are not in conflict: seven is what expertise buys, four is the bare attentional floor underneath it. Four is the number that should size an AI system's working-memory analogue and define what overload means for it.

Long-term memory can still draw on the standard divisions — semantic as a knowledge graph, procedural as a learned policy, episodic as retrievable context tied to past interactions — provided retrieval is built expecting reconstruction. A system that treats a retrieved memory as ground truth inherits the overconfidence of Loftus's subjects, with none of the excuse of having a body that reconstructs by default rather than by design.

<<h>>Control without a controller<</h>>
The prefrontal cortex does much of the work of reasoning and executive control in humans, and the textbook division of labor across its regions is tempting to read as a committee: several named areas, each handling one job, together adding up to executive control. That reading has a name — the homunculus — and a specific failure mode. If some region is a controller that decides what to attend to, inhibit, and hold in mind, then intelligent coordination has been explained by positing an intelligent coordinator inside the system, which explains nothing: the controller would itself need to attend, decide, and remember, and would need its own controller, without end.

Earl Miller and Jonathan Cohen proposed a model that gets control without a controller [[cite:C0290]]. The prefrontal cortex does not house an agent issuing commands. It maintains representations of goals — sustained patterns of activity encoding the rule currently in force — and those representations work by biasing the rest of the brain, the same way attention tips a competition among neural representations toward one interpretation. Control is that biasing mechanism turned to the service of a goal. In the Stroop task, where naming the ink color of the word "red" printed in blue pits a practiced habit against an instruction, there is no homunculus reading the word and overriding it. There is a goal, held active, lending its weight to the weaker pathway until it wins.

An AI system built on this model does not need a dedicated executive module enumerating working memory, cognitive flexibility, goal-directed behavior, evaluation and impulse control as five capacities to install. It needs a goal representation with the standing to bias competing processes, which is a narrower and more tractable engineering target than a controller that does all five jobs itself. This is the section's most direct payload, and it is a claim about what not to build.

Metacognition sits close by: error detection, conflict monitoring, and evaluation give a system a route to catching its own mistakes and adjusting when the situation has changed. It is not free. It adds overhead, and a system with an inflated model of its own reliability has a specific and dangerous failure mode — confidence about exactly the judgments it should be least sure of. A metacognitive capacity is worth building only if it is calibrated to detect that failure, and not only to produce fluent self-reports.

<<h>>Emotion, and the capability it is identical to<</h>>
Chapter 2 does the argumentative work on emotion, and section 2.2 is where the case against empathy as a design target is made. What belongs here is what the neuroscience adds to a design decision, which is less than the length of the literature suggests.

Emotion recognition draws on facial expression, vocal tone, text, and physiological signal, and it is unreliable at the human end before any machine touches it. People understate, mask, and misread their own states; a smile can be happiness, politeness, or irony; and what counts as an appropriate display varies by culture. Section 9.2.1 takes up how far the underlying claim — that emotion is legible from a face at all — has held up. The single-region stories are also weaker than they are usually told: the regions that respond to fear respond to novelty, surprise, and salience of many other kinds, in shifting combinations rather than one dedicated circuit per emotion.

Emotion is nonetheless a real input to human judgment, and a decision system that ignores it is not more rational, only blinder. A medical system weighing a patient's and a family's emotional welfare alongside clinical efficacy is tracking part of the outcome, not decorating it. Emotional signal can also serve as a learning input — a tutoring system adjusting on detected confusion, a reinforcement signal keyed to human response — and each such route is equally a route to a system optimizing for the appearance of whatever the training signal rewards.

The governance point is the one to carry forward, because it is the same point in every application. Deployed systems already do this work: a cognitive-behavioral-therapy chatbot reading emotional state from text [[cite:C0291]], a tutoring system responding to frustration and boredom [[cite:C0292]], a robotic companion for people with dementia [[cite:C0293]]. A system built to interact responsibly and empathetically is mechanically identical to a system built to know what will move a person. Expressive fidelity has to be calibrated rather than maximized, and the line between modulating a user's emotional state and manipulating it is drawn by transparency, consent and who the capability serves — and not by technical sophistication. The same capacity that detects emotionally manipulative rhetoric would, pointed the other way, optimize for it.

<<h>>Plasticity, and what it is for<</h>>
A system that cannot update itself cannot stay aligned with values that themselves develop. Biological learning offers three principles specific enough to build with: spike-timing-dependent plasticity, which anchors learning to temporal structure; Hebbian association, which links co-active units and shows up in machine form as associative memory; and homeostatic plasticity, which holds overall activity in a stable range and is the biological answer to the runaway synchrony and catastrophic forgetting an unconstrained learner is prone to.

Machine analogues of the last are where the practical work is. Continual learning absorbs new data without overwriting old knowledge. Curriculum learning orders tasks from simpler to harder, which is how a person builds moral sophistication — harder cases only once the easier ones are secure. Transfer learning carries structure across domains. Social learning builds a knowledge base by observing and interacting with other agents instead of from specification alone. A healthcare system shows the stakes: to stay ethically current it has to keep absorbing new findings, changed expectations, and regulatory change, rather than freezing its judgment at the moment it was trained.

<<h>>One mechanism, several jobs<</h>>
No single mechanism above is sufficient alone, and integration is not a further module bolted onto the others. What it requires is the same predictive, error-correcting loop — generate an expectation, compare it against what arrives, update — implemented consistently across perception, memory, attention and control, so that a corrected prediction about the world propagates into an updated memory, a redirected attention weight, and a revised goal-bias without a separate synchronization mechanism between separately built parts.

That is the architectural bet this section has been making throughout: one mechanism recruited for several jobs. It is a more falsifiable bet than a hand-designed module for every named cognitive process, which is the main reason to prefer it.
