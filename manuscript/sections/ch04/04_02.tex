\section{Aligning AI Systems with Humane Values and Antifascist Principles}
\label{sec:4.2}
This is the section where a book like this one is expected to list the techniques by which a system's objectives come to track humane values, and where chapter~\ref{sec:3} makes the list read differently. Three of the four methods below learn what to value from someone: from a reward a designer wrote, from behavior a demonstrator produced, from labels an annotator applied. A floor is the part that has to hold when that someone changes their mind or is replaced by somebody worse.

So each method is taken twice — for what it does, and for whether what it does could produce a commitment that survives its own teacher. Reinforcement learning (section~\ref{sec:4.2.1}) makes the reward the whole of what a system wants, which puts everything on who writes it. Inverse and cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (section~\ref{sec:4.2.2}) treat what to want as an open question the human answers, which is the most corrigible design available and, by section~\ref{sec:3.3}'s argument, the least able to hold a line. Supervised learning (section~\ref{sec:4.2.3}) learns from labeled cases, which is an aggregate, and section~\ref{sec:3.1} has already established what an aggregate cannot deliver. Transfer learning (section~\ref{sec:4.2.4}) is asked in this book to carry an antifascist judgment into cases it has not seen, and the evidence that it can is thinner than the request.

None of that makes the methods useless. It locates them: they are how the judgment above the floor gets built, and none of them is how the floor gets built.
