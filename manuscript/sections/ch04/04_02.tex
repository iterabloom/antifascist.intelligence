\section{What a Custody Arrangement Holds}
\label{sec:custody-arrangement}

The bearer and the external record solve different failures. The bearer can notice that a rationale no longer fits what the constraint has become. The record can show that the bearer itself has been replaced, restored, or altered. Neither substitutes for the other.

A credible custody arrangement therefore combines:

\begin{itemize}
  \item publication of the floor's contents before deployment, taken entire from a set somebody else drew up rather than drafted by the operator, since publication shows what was written and not what was omitted;
  \item a published identity for the model authorized to act;
  \item threshold control over retraining and replacement;
  \item attestation of the running model against the published identity;
  \item a sequential, witnessed record of changes and interruptions;
  \item a quorum whose members are adverse in interest rather than merely organizationally distinct; and
  \item a bond forfeited when the record shows that the floor was removed or bypassed.
\end{itemize}

\runin{What attestation and proof establish}

Attestation and proof secure less than their names suggest, and their shortfalls divide into a cost and a limit. A sealed enclave reports what it is running under a key its manufacturer issued, which puts the root of trust in a party a government can reach, and it is built against an adversary who controls the software rather than one who owns the machine. The software form does without the vendor: a proof that an output came from the weights whose hash was published, disclosing nothing about those weights, has been demonstrated, and so have proofs that a training run happened as claimed. (A confidential-computing design of the first kind was defeated with a memory module modified for about ten dollars \autocite{demeulemeester2025badram}; the inference proof has been run on a thirteen-billion-parameter model at under fifteen minutes per inference \autocite{sun2024zkllm}, and the training proofs exist three orders of magnitude smaller \autocite{abbaszadeh2024kaizen}. Those are costs at a date, and they will fall.) Three limits will not fall with them, because they are not facts about the state of the art. A proof establishes which model answered and not what that model holds, and the obstacle is in the writing rather than the proving. Verification quantifies over unenumerated inputs routinely; what it needs first is a predicate, and nobody has written one for \emph{the reason for refusing is present in this case} that does not presuppose the judgment it would be used to check. A predicate over natural-language requests would also have to be connected to them by an interpretation nothing certifies, so the regress reopens a level down. The proofs cited above do not attempt a behavioral property at all: they establish which weights answered and that a run happened as claimed, which are facts about execution. The operator is the prover, so the secrecy runs the operator's way — the proof hides the weights from whoever is checking and nothing from the party being checked. And cryptography keeps a secret from a party that does not have it, while copying, restoring, retraining and deleting a model one legitimately possesses are custody problems rather than secrecy problems, which is what the list above is for.

None of these makes circumvention impossible. Copying and retraining a model one legitimately possesses are custody problems, not secrecy problems. The attainable standard is that circumvention becomes expensive, visible, attributable, and compensable. Constitutional entrenchment works the same way: it does not make amendment impossible but raises the number of parties who must agree and places the attempt on the record.
