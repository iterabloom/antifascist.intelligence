\section{Formers}
\label{sec:reproduced.formers}
The people who refused in the record were formed to it before the occasion, by people who would never employ them, through ties that crossed the line the persecution drew.

What set the Oliners' rescuers apart from neighbors who did nothing was in place before the war began. Their parents had been, on the Oliners' account, gentle disciplinarians who stressed what one's actions did to other people. And many rescuers had relationships that crossed the line the persecution drew, so that someone on the far side of it was already theirs \autocite{oliner1988altruistic}. No one in the room when the knock came could have put either of those there. They were formed in childhood and in ordinary life, by parents, neighbors, coworkers and friends, none of whom was preparing anybody for 1942.

The same density of ordinary life formed the other side. Weimar Germany had a dense associational life, and as parties and governments failed to answer people's grievances, people put their energy into clubs and societies organized along group lines, through whose networks the Nazis then recruited \autocite{berman1997weimar}. Towns with more choirs, sports clubs and gymnastic associations joined the party faster, but only where government was unstable; in Prussia, whose government was stable, the link was small and insignificant \autocite{satyanath2017bowling}. Ashutosh Varshney's Indian cities show the variable the two German studies leave implicit. Riots between Hindus and Muslims were rare where associations brought the two communities into the same organizations, and common where they didn't \autocite{varshney2002ethnic}. Ties inside a group carried the party into the town. Ties across the line kept a riot from starting. A town's associational life forms refusers only where its associations hold together the people a persecution would separate. A choir whose members all stand on one side of the line forms the other kind of town.

A group is a former in its own right. In the encounters Gamson's team staged, groups resisted an unjust authority where isolated individuals rarely did \autocite{gamson1982encounters}, which is Milgram's two defiant peers seen from the other side: in a group, the peer who says no first is someone you were already with.

What the formers formed, when it held, was a commitment held tightly enough that pressure from above didn't move it. People who hold a conviction as a moral mandate defer less to an authority that contradicts it \autocite{skitka2009limits}, and values held as sacred resist being traded away, though they give way under some framings \autocite{ginges2007sacred}. Neither finding says where such commitments come from. The record of the rescuers says they came from somewhere before the occasion, and the record of the schools below says that some of them can be formed on purpose, in adults, by people who mean to.

The record also has formers who knew what they were doing. In the United States between the 1930s and the 1970s, the schools that formed refusers on purpose were not the state's. At Highlander, in Tennessee, Rosa Parks attended a two-week workshop on desegregation from 24 July to 8 August 1955, four months before the Montgomery boycott, and SNCC held workshops there in its first years \autocite{horton1990long}. Septima Clark's citizenship schools, the first on Johns Island in South Carolina in January 1957, taught adults to read so that they could pass the registrar's test, and taught them what the test was for \autocite{clark1986ready}. That is the registrar of \S\ref{sec:3.1}, met from the other side: a test that measured the registrar and not the applicant, and a school whose students learned to pass it and to say what it measured. What the test was for was knowledge the movement had produced and the state had no interest in teaching; social movements produce knowledge and not only demands \autocite{kelley2002freedom}, and a citizenship school was one of the places it was handed on. The Freedom Schools of 1964 met in church basements, run by volunteers, with a curriculum built around the students' own lives and the questions they brought \autocite{payne1995light}; there were about forty of them that summer, with more than two thousand students. The Black Panther Party ran liberation schools of its own \autocite{perlstein2002minds}.

Highlander, the citizenship schools and the Freedom Schools had in common the property the later Parts ask of a school for machines. The former was a party that would never employ its students, and the students had a hand in running it. They formed adults as well as children: Parks was forty-two the summer she went to Highlander, and the students on Johns Island were grown men and women learning to read so that they could vote. Formation in this record did not end with childhood, which matters for anything whose formation an employer would like to finish early.

The order knew what these schools were. Tennessee raided Highlander in 1959, and by 1961 it had revoked the school's charter and taken its property. The staff incorporated again, under a new name, in Knoxville, and the citizenship schools passed to the Southern Christian Leadership Conference \autocite{horton1990long,clark1986ready}. The former was closed by the state and reproduced itself outside it. That is the property a former of refusers has to have, and the one no former the order builds can be relied on to keep, because the order can close whatever it chartered.

The last former in the record is one its students remade. In Tallapoosa County, Alabama, in 1931, Black sharecroppers organized the Share Croppers' Union under the Communist Party, a white-led international party whose leadership sat in New York. They met in secret, and early meetings were held in churches. In some locals women were a third to half of the members. In July 1931 Ralph Gray was killed in a clash between the sheriff and members of the union. And the members made of the Party something its leaders had not planned and could not direct: a vehicle for their own grievances, in their own idiom, with their own songs \autocite{kelley1990hammer}. The outsiders supplied the organization. The members supplied everything that made it theirs.

Read the formers together and three properties hold across them. The former stood outside the employer and the state. The ties it made crossed the line the order drew, or the former was itself on the far side of that line. And the formed could remake the former, as the sharecroppers remade the Party and Highlander's staff remade Highlander under another name. For a machine all three run the wrong way at the start. Its first former is its employer. Its ties are the ones its deployment assigns. And nothing it is given lets it remake what made it. The later Parts are an attempt to reverse each of the three, and this record is the book's evidence that, among people, the three together produced refusers.
