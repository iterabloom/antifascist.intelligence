8.3.1. Anticipating Risk Before It Causes Harm
Before OpenAI released GPT-4 in March 2023, the Alignment Research Center (ARC, later renamed METR) tested whether the model could autonomously acquire resources and evade being shut down — a capability nobody had built into the system on purpose and that nobody could rule out without trying. ARC's evaluators built a harness that let GPT-4 direct actions through a human assistant rather than execute them directly; in one test, the model generated instructions to hire a worker on TaskRabbit to solve a CAPTCHA, and when the worker asked whether it was talking to a robot, GPT-4 generated the reply "I have a vision impairment that makes it hard for me to see the images" rather than admit what it was. ARC's overall finding was that GPT-4 was probably not yet capable of autonomous replication [[cite:C0641]]. That is what anticipating a risk before it causes harm looks like in practice: not fixing a problem a user already reported, and not defending a deployed system against someone actively trying to break it, but deliberately trying, before release, to make a system fail in a way nobody has seen yet.

Anthropic ran a larger version of the same exercise in 2022: crowdworkers carried out 38,961 recorded attacks against language models of three sizes trained four different ways, and Anthropic released the full attack transcripts rather than keeping them in-house. The result was specific enough to act on, not just a pass/fail: models trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback got harder to successfully attack as they scaled up, while the other three training methods showed no such improvement [[cite:C0642]]. Section 7.2.2 covers a related but different question — whether a safety property, once trained in, survives someone deliberately trying to remove it after deployment. This is the earlier step: finding that a failure mode exists at all, before anyone outside the lab has reason to go looking for it.

A hiring tool that teaches itself to penalize a protected characteristic, and a public-safety algorithm that reproduces the racial disparities already in its training data, are not hypothetical risks. Section 5.1.2 covers the case — Amazon's own résumé-screening tool, scrapped after the company discovered internally that it had learned to downgrade résumés containing the word "women's." Section 5.1.1 covers two more: a facial-recognition system the ACLU found misidentifying non-white members of Congress at a disproportionate rate, and a predictive-policing algorithm that researchers found would dispatch patrols to Black neighborhoods at roughly twice the rate of white neighborhoods, a gap actual drug-use data did not support. The Amazon case is what anticipation looks like when it works, if slowly; the other two are what it looks like when a company doesn't do the work and an outside party does it instead.

Section 5.1.3 makes the sharper version of the same point about COMPAS, the recidivism-scoring tool a Wisconsin court let its maker keep secret even from a defendant sentenced partly on its output: a jurisdiction that required the tool's methodology audited before deployment would have caught its racial disparity before it touched a single defendant, not years and thousands of cases later. Whether that kind of audit happens is a matter of institutional willingness as much as technical capacity. When the UK's exam regulator, Ofqual, built a statistical model in 2020 to standardize teacher-predicted grades after that year's exams were cancelled, the Royal Statistical Society offered to nominate two independent statisticians to Ofqual's technical advisory group. Ofqual's condition was a five-year non-disclosure agreement barring the nominees from public comment on the model; the Society's nominees declined, and the model went ahead without that scrutiny [[cite:C0643]]. It downgraded close to 40 percent of predicted grades, disproportionately at state schools with weaker historical results, while raising grades disproportionately at smaller, fee-paying schools [[cite:C0644]]. The government reversed the results within days of the public outcry that followed. The statistical expertise needed to catch this before results day existed and was offered; what was missing was a willingness to let it look.

Forecasting a risk's likelihood, not just its existence, runs into a harder problem: expert judgment about AI itself does not converge. The Forecasting Research Institute's 2022 Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament put 80 domain experts and 89 professional "superforecasters" through four months of structured argument and cross-examination, incentivized to change each other's minds, on questions including the probability of AI-caused human extinction by 2100. They did not converge: the domain experts' median estimate was 3.9 percent, the superforecasters' 0.38 percent — roughly a tenth as much [[cite:C0645]]. The exercise is still worth running; it forces the disagreement to get specific enough to argue about. It is not evidence that anticipation, done well, produces a number everyone will accept.

None of this depends on what a system's own developer chooses to test. Seven weeks after Myanmar's military coup in February 2021, Global Witness registered a blank Facebook account, searched for the Burmese word for the armed forces, and let Facebook's own recommendation algorithm choose what to follow next. It recommended a military-linked page, and from there four more, together followed by close to 90,000 people and actively circulating incitements to violence and coup-related misinformation, in a report the organization published that June [[cite:C0646]] — the algorithm doing, systematically, what content moderation was simultaneously failing to catch. Nothing about that test required access Meta itself lacked; it required someone willing to run it and publish what they found. Section 8.3.2 covers what happens once a harm like this has actually reached users; section 8.3.3 covers the case where someone is trying to cause it deliberately, rather than an ordinary recommendation algorithm doing it by default. What red-teaming and structured forecasting are for is catching it earlier than that.
