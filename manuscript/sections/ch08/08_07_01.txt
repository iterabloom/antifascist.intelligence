8.7.1. How Research Institutions Cooperate Across Borders

Altruistic, antifascist AI cannot be built inside one country's research institutions alone; training data, safety techniques, and researchers themselves cross borders more easily than most other engineering work does. What that cooperation actually looks like is narrower than a treaty and less formal than a certification body: shared tools, shared training runs, and shared publication norms — the layer underneath both the technical standards section 8.7.2 covers and the government-to-government structures section 8.7.6 covers.

The clearest example is also one of the oldest. OpenAI released Gym, an open-source toolkit for building and comparing reinforcement-learning algorithms [[cite:C0491]]. Any lab working on reinforcement learning, in any country, could build against the same benchmark environments instead of writing its own, and results from competing labs became directly comparable because their authors had trained against the same suite. That is not a partnership, and it produced something a partnership cannot: comparability between organizations that compete with each other for the same funding, researchers, and attention, and that had no reason to coordinate anything.

A more recent example runs the same logic at a larger scale. The BigScience workshop, coordinated by Hugging Face, brought together more than a thousand researchers from over seventy countries to build BLOOM, a 176-billion-parameter multilingual language model released under a license written specifically to restrict harmful use, its training data and code published openly for anyone to inspect [[cite:C0492]]. Nothing compelled any of those researchers to take part, and nothing compels the next comparable effort to happen at all. The model exists because enough institutions decided cooperating on that particular project was worth more than competing on it.

That is the limit of the mechanism and the reason it is worth naming anyway. Shared infrastructure binds nobody and is therefore available where nothing binding is: a lab in a country that has signed no convention can still train against the same benchmark and publish against the same baseline. It also stops at the resource line. A research institution without the compute to run the benchmark is outside the arrangement no matter how open the arrangement is, which is the digital-divide problem section 8.7.6 covers, showing up here as a membership condition nobody wrote down.
