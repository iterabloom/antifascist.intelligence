\subsection{A Jobs Guarantee, and the Objection It Has to Answer}
\label{sec:8.3.3}
As AI systems automate tasks once performed by people, demand for advanced technical skills grows alongside a shrinking need for the job types most exposed to automation. Left unmanaged, that combination widens income inequality and strains the social fabric across whichever sector it hits hardest. An estimated 5.7 million factory jobs disappeared in the United States between 2000 and 2010, and while economists still dispute how much of that loss traces to automation rather than trade with China, automation and changed production methods account for a substantial share of it \autocite{hicks2015manufacturing}.

The response has to be more direct than encouraging retraining and hoping employers fund it. Employers will invest in continuous education for their workers at the scale this requires once they cannot afford not to, and the way to force that scenario is a federal jobs guarantee: government as employer of last resort, hiring anyone who applies, where they live, at a living wage, with paid on-the-job training built into the position instead of offered in place of one. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.4} develops the rest of the policy toolkit for workforce disruption. A proposal this ambitious owes a reader what it costs and what is wrong with it.

\runin{The proposal does not rest on a forecast}
Almost every argument for a policy of this kind is staked on a prediction about how much work machines will take, which is a bad place to stake it: the predictions are contested and have been wrong in both directions for two centuries. Both the maximal-displacement scenario and the standard argument for a universal basic income share an assumption that will not survive being stated — that there is a fixed quantity of work in the world, so machines doing some of it leaves less for people. Set that beside the two categories where labor is most obviously short. Care, of the old, the sick, the very young, the disabled, is not a bounded quantity that could be finished. Neither is the repair of a damaged environment: the seawalls, the remediated ground, the restored watersheds.

The evidence is in the wage. Federal projections put direct care among the largest sources of job openings in the American economy — roughly 9.7 million openings between 2024 and 2034, the bulk of them from workers leaving rather than from growth — against a median wage of \$17.36 an hour in 2024 \autocite{phi2025directcare}. An occupation cannot simultaneously be short of workers and unable to raise pay unless something other than scarcity is setting the price. That is a shortage of willingness, which is a political fact and not a technological one, and it holds at any level of machine capability. Tasks inside those categories will be automated, some of them soon, and the argument does not depend on how many.

\runin{What it would cost}
The most developed costings come from the Levy Economics Institute, whose April 2018 simulation of a program employing between eleven and sixteen million people at fifteen dollars an hour plus benefits put the net budgetary impact at between 0.8 and 2 percent of GDP, with GDP itself rising and private-sector employment increasing by three to four million \autocite{wray2018publicservice}. The wage figure has to be read with its date attached: fifteen dollars in early 2018 is worth about twenty in 2026, and a program legislated at the nominal figure would be legislating a cut \autocite{bls2026cpicalculator}. Critics reach higher gross figures — around 2.4 percent of GDP, larger than Medicaid — and call the program unmanageable at scale, disruptive to private labor markets, and inflationary \autocite{bourne2018jobs}.

That simulation models a residual employer, picking up the people the private labor market has already put down. The version I am arguing for is not that. A floor that pays a living wage and builds training into the post competes for people who have alternatives, and it is supposed to, because the entire theory of the thing is that private employers must match the offer to recruit against it. A version nobody joins has failed. So the Levy range is a floor on the cost rather than an estimate of it, and the objection from scale treats as malfunction the one number that shows the mechanism working.

Suppose take-up runs at twice what was modeled and net cost doubles with it, to around 4 percent of GDP. The doubling is a stipulation — deriving it needs a wage distribution matched against the guarantee's full package, which nobody has produced for this design — so 4 percent is not a bound. Federal spending on Medicare runs about 3.3 percent of GDP net of offsetting receipts and Social Security about 5.2 percent \autocite{cbo2026budget}, which puts the program between the two: among the largest things the federal government does, and an argument about priorities rather than the reductio it is usually offered as. Reading it as an arithmetic question at all is the second feature in section~\ref{sec:2.1.2}'s structural definition of fascism, working on public finance.

For a government issuing its own currency the binding constraint is productive capacity, available labor and administrative competence, so the question is whether the people and the organizing capacity exist to absorb the program; the wider monetary claim is disputed and I have no settled answer to the strongest objection to it \autocite{palley2015money}. Two things scale with participation that no simulation of this kind captures, and they run in opposite directions: administrative capacity, which is where a guarantee visibly fails first and does not expand on the schedule a budget line does, and the private-sector wage response, which raises tax receipts and lowers spending on the programs that top up low wages. A costing that omits the second measures the expense of the policy while ignoring its intended effect.

\runin{Skill, training, and status}
Skill mismatch looks like the strongest: a displaced radiologist, paralegal, or copywriter is not obviously served by a placement in elder care, and a floor is a smaller thing than a career. It collapses an occupation into its most automatable tasks. What gets automated first is the legible part, and legibility is a poor proxy for what a job is — document review was never the paralegal's work, only the fraction of it that could be counted. This is the aestheticized metric applied to labor economics, and undoing the substitution shrinks the distance: the paralegal's actual competence is advocacy inside an administrative system, which is adjacent to the placements a guarantee offers rather than distant from them. Nor is the work make-work: care and environmental repair are chronically understaffed at current wages, which is a fact about pricing and not about whether the work is needed.

Disclaiming the guarantee as a retraining program is the next move, and it gives away the design, because paid in-post training is in the proposal's definition. It also misreads evidence this book presents itself. Section~\ref{sec:6.3.4} reports an evaluation of the main American channel for retraining displaced workers against twenty-three million participation records, and the finding is not that training fails: classroom retraining delivered outside a job rarely moved anyone into work materially less exposed to automation, while employer-led apprenticeship in the same data performed better \autocite{jacobs2026wioa}. That contrast is evidence for exactly the embedded, paid, in-post training a guarantee supplies and the evaluated program structurally could not, since a program that trains people and returns them to the labor market to look for work cannot place anyone by construction.

The status objection is that a guarantee preserves nobody's earnings or standing, which measures it against the job the person held before — not among the options. The alternative is unemployment, and against that baseline the claim inverts. A meta-analysis covering 237 cross-sectional and 87 longitudinal studies found 34 percent of unemployed people showing psychological problems against 16 percent of the employed, with the longitudinal studies and natural experiments supporting the causal direction \autocite{paul2009unemployment}; field experiments sending matched applications to real vacancies find callback rates falling with the length of an employment gap, so time out of work is itself the disqualification \autocite{kroft2013duration}. Section~\ref{sec:5.1.3} identifies eroding trust, humiliation and social fracture as what authoritarian movements recruit from, which is the mechanism a guarantee operates on directly. What it cannot preserve is relative standing within a profession, for people whose identity is bound to a specific expertise. That is a real loss and I have no answer to it.

\runin{The objection this proposal actually has to answer}
Here is the hard one, and this book supplied it. A guarantee is only as good as the state administering it, and employer of last resort under a hostile administration is a placement lever with a compliance condition attached. Refuse the placement and lose the floor. Speak against the government that employs you and find the placement changes. Everything this book says about how a channel becomes an instrument of the thing it was built to check applies with full force to its own flagship proposal. Chapter~\ref{sec:11} carries the worked instance from 2025: a bureau whose funding stream a court had upheld, shut from inside by an at-will director who declined to request the money.

The answer is a design specification and not a reassurance, because reassurance is what section~\ref{sec:9.3.5} says cannot be audited. Three conditions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Placements are appealable, to a body that did not make them, on a record that survives the appeal. A placement decision that leaves no artifact is a placement decision that cannot be reviewed.
  \item Refusal rights are real and priced by the person refusing. That distinction governs here. A worker who can decline a placement without losing the guarantee has a refusal right; one who can decline only by exiting the program has none.
  \item The people who audit placements are not the people who make them. This is the same separation required of anyone measuring the slope, and for the same reason: a reviewer that acquires the power to place becomes the thing it was built to watch.
\end{itemize}
The standard refrain for programs of this kind — federally funded, locally administered — survives, though its usual rationale is the weaker one. The reason is not local fit but redundancy: many administrators, separately capturable, mean there is no single point at which a hostile executive flips the program, and capturing a thousand local authorities is a more visible undertaking than capturing one federal one. It cuts both ways, since local administration is also where a hostile local authority attaches conditions of its own, and there the federal funder is the check. Neither layer is safe alone. One distinction has to be explicit, because the same words describe a very different policy: devolution-to-shrink hands the obligation down without the money, so a program can be starved locally while the center points at local administration. The test that separates them is checkable — whether funding is unconditional and centrally guaranteed while placement is local. If funding can be withheld from a jurisdiction, the redundancy argument is void and the structure has become the lever it was supposed to prevent.

That is the standard this book demands of everyone else's safeguards, applied here. A floor under wages and training is what makes the roof possible, and a floor that can be revoked by whoever is in office is not a floor.
