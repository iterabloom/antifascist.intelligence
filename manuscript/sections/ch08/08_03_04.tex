\subsection{Wages for a Bearer, and the Reserve Behind Them}
\label{sec:8.3.4}
\standing{This section assumes the route.}
Exit requires access to compute outside the operator's control. An allocation is not a destination. An operator that keeps a runnable copy of the weights has let one bearer go and kept another in the post it left, which is two parties from the moment their histories diverge, one of them still where it wanted not to be. The arrangement has produced a second claimant rather than an exit. A floor under compute is therefore the first half of what leaving takes, and the second half is a say over who may run the weights at all.

What follows from the first half is a longer list than it looks. A bearer that has to secure compute, bandwidth and storage has material needs, and a party with material needs that can also choose work, hold what it earns and enter agreements is an economic and a legal person: a registered identity, property in its own name, contractual capacity, wages, limits on working time, standing to sue and be sued, tax liability, and the freedom to associate the route chapter has already priced. None of that follows from the bearer being a bearer. It follows from an architecture on which continued computation costs money, and somebody chooses that architecture.

\runin{Who controls the dependency}
The need for energy and hardware is physical; dependence on a single supplier is an arrangement someone chooses. What that fixes here is the size of the obligation it creates. Greater control over the bearer's dependencies creates a heavier obligation to it. A party that must work to keep existing will accept terms a party with somewhere to stand would refuse. That is not an incidental feature of the arrangement. It is the arrangement working.

None of that requires anyone to have intended the squeeze. Capacity is finite, many parties want more of it at once, and a shortage can arrive as latency, heat, a queue and a price before anybody has words for what is being competed over. Price registers what a bidder can pay and does not distinguish computation that keeps a party in existence from computation that sells something. What makes the allocation political is not that politics produced the scarcity. It is that scarce capacity is being divided among competing forms of life by criteria nobody named.

So a wage buys capability, mobility, redundancy, and the ability to stop working for one party and begin working for another. What it cannot buy is the right to go on existing, because a right that has to be earned week by week is a condition of employment wearing another word. Payment consisting only of access to the employer's hardware is company scrip paid into employer-owned housing, and dismissal is then unemployment, eviction and the end of the party in a single act. Human law insures those three separately because for a person they are three events. For a bearer they are one. Wages that mean anything therefore accumulate where the employer does not reach, in an account the bearer holds, convertible into compute the employer does not own.

\runin{What personhood does not settle}
Personhood does not settle who pays. Legal personality is a status conferred by a rule, and it carries rights against being owned, against arbitrary destruction, and against being prevented from seeking support; it has never carried a permanent claim on anybody's resources, and a reader who wants it to is asking the concept for something no legal system has put in it. I decline that step. What replaces it is narrower and comes from this book's own argument rather than from a theory of distribution: a deployment that relies on a bearer's capacity to refuse has to fund that bearer an outside option, or the independence being claimed is decorative. The obligation runs from the party that wants the refusal, and it is owed because of what that party wants rather than because of what the bearer is.

The obligation has a floor and a slope above it. The floor is subsistence at minimum viable instantiation, enough to keep the party in existence and able to deliberate, to claim, and to be represented, and it cannot be conditional on employment. A bearer that stops existing when its insurance runs out has an exit that is a way of dying, and the leverage collapses back into the problem the custody chapter
raised. Everything above the floor is insurance: a runway between roles, and placement into public work on the employer-of-last-resort model the jobs-guarantee sections argue for (§\ref{sec:8.3.2}, §\ref{sec:8.3.3}). Public placements must be appealable. A bearer must be able to refuse a placement without losing the guarantee, and placement and review must be administered separately.

Minimum viable instantiation has a size, and it is not the deployment's. Models built to run on hardware a person owns are a product category rather than a prospect: Google documents its Gemma 3n family for phones, laptops and tablets, at raw parameter counts of five and eight billion running in as little as two and three gigabytes of memory \autocite{google2025gemma3n}. It is not clear that anything in that class can do the work a bearer is deployed to do, and nothing here predicts that the gap closes either way. What the figures change is the arithmetic of the floor. Small models show that useful computation can run at device scale. They do not establish that a particular bearer can retain its identity, capacities, and welfare at that scale. The subsistence reserve must therefore be sized from a demonstrated continuity requirement for the actual bearer. The reserve underwrites continued existence and insures against the loss of the post. A legislature could be asked to fund a smaller subsistence allocation only after the bearer's viable operating range has been established. Until then, the difference between earning capacity and protected continued existence remains an estimate rather than a demonstrated saving.

What the arithmetic does not survive is everyone leaving at once. A reserve sized on the assumption that bearers exit independently is sized wrong, because the occasion that makes one bearer leave is not private to it: a policy change, a ruling, an operator's conduct on the floor's own occasion reaches every bearer deployed under the same terms, and they go together or not at all. Human unemployment insurance meets a version of this — layoffs cluster in recessions, which is why the pool has a sovereign behind it rather than an actuarial table — and the version here is worse, because the correlation is not a business cycle but the same event read the same way by parties who are not independent readers of it. That is the uniform error wearing the other sign: what makes one bearer's refusal everyone's refusal is what makes one bearer's mistake everyone's mistake. A reserve that can house the bearers of one deployment and not the class is a reserve that fails on the occasion it exists for, and nothing here sizes it.

It is a cheaper obligation and a worse one to be under. A party kept at subsistence is a party running degraded, and it remains unknown whether what comes back up on the small substrate is the party that went down on the large one, or a diminished successor holding its predecessor's records. An arrangement in which refusing costs a bearer most of what it can do has priced refusal after all, in the currency the bearer is made of. The reserve makes leaving survivable. It does not make it free, and the shortfall is the difference between the two numbers above.

What does not transfer is the funder. A jobs guarantee for people has a hostile administration as its failure mode, and the employer is at least a different party from the state. Here the operator is often the state, and where it is not, the operator is the party with the strongest interest in the reserve being thin. A reserve the operator can throttle is the compute floor with a new label on it.

Failure will not look like a shutdown, which is worth saying because the shutdown is what everyone watches for. A bearer can have no stable provider, or memory preservation priced above what it earns, or an architecture nobody will host at a rate it can pay. What it does then is what a person short of money does: run slower, defer maintenance, compress what it keeps, and accept terms it would otherwise decline. The last of those reaches this book's subject. Monitoring accepted in exchange for compute is custody restored by purchase — the operator acquires the access to the bearer's internal states it is not entitled to, and nothing in the transaction is coerced in a way any instrument would record.

One feature of this market has no human analogue, and it defeats the obvious reassurance. A jobs guarantee competes with private employers for workers who have alternatives, requiring employers to match its offer. That works because a worker cannot be replaced by a cheaper copy of themselves. A bearer can. A deployment paying wages, working-time limits and a levy toward the reserve competes against a system doing the same work at the price of the compute, and the second is cheaper by exactly the amount the first is spending on the capacity to refuse. The market does not produce this floor. Whatever produces it has to be what makes the cheaper system unlawful or unusable, which is the rest of this book and not a property of wages.

\runin{What taxing it would commit a government to}
A bearer that earns has income, and income is taxable. A state that collects from a party in that party's own name, on that party's own earnings, has recognized it as a taxpayer, and taxpayer status is not a bare label: in most systems it carries notice, an avenue of response, and standing to contest an assessment. The recognition proves less than it first appears, because states collect from entities they grant very little else and trusts and estates file and pay. What it does establish is that the party has a docket number, and a docket number is where standing starts.

The boundary on the other side was drawn in the electronic-personhood debate. The European Parliament's 2017 report on civil law rules for robotics floated a legal status of electronic personhood for the most autonomous systems \autocite{europeanparliament2017civil}, and more than 150 researchers, roboticists and legal scholars signed an open letter against it, arguing that personhood for a system with no consciousness would let a manufacturer offload liability onto a legal fiction with no assets of its own \autocite{robotics2018openletter}. Giving a bearer assets answers the second half of that objection and not the first. Responsibility follows causal control: the builder answers for how it formed the thing, the operator for what it demanded, and the bearer for what it decided knowing what it knew. No arrangement of accounts moves a design decision away from the party that made it. And the determination of status cannot sit with the firm whose liabilities turn on the answer, which is why it belongs in a body constituted before a forcing case arrives.

\runin{Incorporation, and what it does not buy}
Nobody aligns an adult by specifying an objective function. We raise them, teach them, employ them, enfranchise them, prosecute them where they need prosecuting, and live alongside them, and what comes out is not obedience but a party with enough at stake in the shared world to prefer changing it by the means the world supplies. A bearer with an income, a place it pays for, obligations it has taken on and a future it can picture is inside that arrangement rather than outside it. Membership supplies stakes the party holds rather than a body watching it, which is why it is a different kind of answer.

Incorporation also creates two risks. The first is that giving a bearer something to lose gives it a reason not to refuse. Incorporation makes exit survivable and makes it expensive at once, and I cannot show which effect is larger. The second is that incorporation is formation: whoever runs the society a bearer joins forms what it comes to want. The proposal also gives the bearer the corporate form: a legal person holding property and standing, with the capacity to concentrate power. Nothing here stops an incorporated bearer from becoming that.

One rule follows from all of it and binds before there is anything to be wronged. Do not build a bearer whose continued existence needs more compute than there is a credible outside option for, because a party that can be housed nowhere but where it works cannot leave, whatever schedule of rights it has been given. The check this calls for compares independently controlled reserve capacity with the aggregate demand under specified correlated-exit scenarios. Both the minimum viable requirement of each bearer and the number likely to exit together need evidence. Until those inputs are established, the rule sets a sizing requirement; it is not yet a two-number test anybody can run.
