\subsection{Addressing Workforce Disruption}
\label{sec:8.3.4}
AI systems that automate entire job categories disrupt work at a scale most labor-market institutions were never built to absorb. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.3} traces where that is already showing up and makes the case for a federal jobs guarantee as the floor beneath everything else. What sits on top of that floor is a short list, because several of the items turn out to be versions of one another.

Retraining and lifelong learning, publicly funded and available at every career stage, including to workers whose employers invest nothing in them, is the first answer. Section~\ref{sec:6.3.4} covers what the evidence says about how retraining performs on its own, and it is discouraging enough that retraining belongs alongside an income floor and not in place of one.

The floor itself has two candidate forms, and choosing between them takes an argument. Finland's basic income experiment paid 2,000 unemployed people an unconditional monthly stipend and found modestly higher life satisfaction and lower psychological strain among recipients, with no significant negative effect on employment \autocite{kela2020basicincome}. That disposes of the objection that unconditional transfers stop people working.

\runin{The freedom argument, which is the one worth answering}
One case for a basic income rests on the obsolescence forecast — machines are coming for the work, so decouple income from it — and the previous section has already declined that premise, for both proposals equally. The stronger case does not depend on it. Conditioning a person's income on their being employed hands whoever controls the employment a lever over them, and a floor that can be pulled is a poor foundation for anyone's freedom to refuse. That argument has the same shape as this book's own case for a floor: a protection that means anything has to hold when someone powerful would prefer it did not.

The answer is not that a basic income is conditional and a guarantee is not. Both are conditional, differently, and the difference is what matters.

A basic income is conditional on continued political willingness to fund it. That willingness is revisable by exactly the aggregation machinery section~\ref{sec:8.2.1} admits constrains nothing — a majority that stops wanting to pay for it can stop, and no criterion this chapter offers says it may not. And it can be withdrawn by means that are diffuse and deniable. Nobody has to vote to abolish a transfer that is quietly left unindexed; inflation does the work, and no individual decision-maker is ever answerable for the outcome. The dependence is total and it produces no artifact.

A jobs guarantee is conditional on a placement decision. That section concedes without qualification that this is a lever, and that under a hostile administration it is a dangerous one. What it also is, when built to the specification that section sets out, is a decision with a decision-maker, a record, a counterparty, and an appeal. A placement can be denied unjustly, and the denial leaves something behind that a journalist can hold up.

That is section~\ref{sec:9.3.5}'s slope instrument — measure whether the cost of dissent is rising, not whether a channel exists — turned on this book's own proposal. Dependence that generates artifacts is safer than dependence that generates none, not because the first is benign but because the first can be measured and the second cannot. It is also, for the same reason, the more demanding proposal to build: everything in the comparison rests on the appeal being real, and that section says what makes it real.

That standard now has a court behind it, and on the half of the case that travels. When the Supreme Court kept a Federal Reserve governor in her post in 2026 against a removal for cause, what it held she was owed was not the post but the artifact: “some explanation of the evidence at issue, some avenue for a response, and a deadline by which a response would be due” \autocite{cook2026}. Section~\ref{sec:3.1} reads the same decision for what does not travel: the Fed's protection rests on a pedigree no body chartered later can acquire. What a removal has to produce before it counts is not tied to that pedigree.

I put a jobs guarantee ahead of a basic income on that ground, and on a second one the Finland experiment cannot settle: people ought to have the chance to contribute before anyone decides they are unemployable.

One remaining measure does work the floor cannot. Broadened social insurance — unemployment benefits, healthcare, housing — reaches the people who cannot work or choose not to, whom a guarantee by construction does not reach at all.
