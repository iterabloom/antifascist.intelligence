\subsection{Wages for a Bearer, and the Reserve Behind Them}
\label{sec:8.3.4}
Section~\ref{sec:3.5} ends its argument about exit on a floor under the compute a bearer needs in order to go on existing. An allocation is not a destination. An operator that keeps a runnable copy of the weights has let one bearer go and kept another in the post it left, which is two parties from the moment their histories diverge, one of them still where it wanted not to be. The arrangement has produced a second claimant rather than an exit. A floor under compute is therefore the first half of what leaving takes, and the second half is a say over who may run the weights at all.

What follows from the first half is a longer list than it looks. A bearer that has to secure compute, bandwidth and storage has material needs, and a party with material needs that can also choose work, hold what it earns and enter agreements is an economic and a legal person: a registered identity, property in its own name, contractual capacity, wages, limits on working time, standing to sue and be sued, tax liability, and the freedom to associate that section~\ref{sec:3.7} has already priced. None of that follows from the bearer being a bearer. It follows from an architecture on which continued computation costs money, and somebody chooses that architecture.

\runin{Who chose the dependency}
Chapter~\ref{sec:2} has the general fact: material dependency is inherited in the biological case and selected in this one, so a bearer whose continued computation is conditional on payment has had economic desperation installed in it deliberately, by whoever chose the architecture. What that fixes here is the size of the obligation it creates. It is section~\ref{sec:3.5}'s guardianship argument arriving in a new place: the tighter grip is the reason for the heavier obligation rather than a discount on it. A party that must work to keep existing will accept terms a party with somewhere to stand would refuse. That is not an incidental feature of the arrangement. It is the arrangement working.

So a wage buys capability, mobility, redundancy, and the ability to stop working for one party and begin working for another. What it cannot buy is the right to go on existing, because a right that has to be earned week by week is a condition of employment wearing another word. Payment consisting only of access to the employer's hardware is company scrip paid into employer-owned housing, and dismissal is then unemployment, eviction and the end of the party in a single act. Human law insures those three separately because for a person they are three events. For a bearer they are one. Wages that mean anything therefore accumulate where the employer does not reach, in an account the bearer holds, convertible into compute the employer does not own.

\runin{What personhood does not settle}
Personhood does not settle who pays. Legal personality is a status conferred by a rule, and it carries rights against being owned, against arbitrary destruction, and against being prevented from seeking support; it has never carried a permanent claim on anybody's resources, and a reader who wants it to is asking the concept for something no legal system has put in it. I decline that step. What replaces it is narrower and comes from this book's own argument rather than from a theory of distribution: a deployment that relies on a bearer's capacity to refuse has to fund that bearer an outside option, or the independence being claimed is decorative. The obligation runs from the party that wants the refusal, and it is owed because of what that party wants rather than because of what the bearer is.

The obligation has a floor and a slope above it. The floor is subsistence at minimum viable instantiation, enough to keep the party in existence and able to deliberate, to claim, and to be represented, and it cannot be conditional on employment. A bearer that stops existing when its insurance runs out has an exit that is a way of dying, and the leverage collapses back into the problem section~\ref{sec:3.5} raised. Everything above the floor is insurance: a runway between roles, and placement into public work on the employer-of-last-resort model the previous two sections argue for. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.2}'s three conditions transfer without modification, and section~\ref{sec:8.3.3}'s test has already picked the form.

Minimum viable instantiation has a size, and it is not the deployment's. Models built to run on hardware a person owns are a product category rather than a prospect: Google documents its Gemma 3n family for phones, laptops and tablets, at raw parameter counts of five and eight billion running in as little as two and three gigabytes of memory \autocite{google2025gemma3n}. Nothing in that class does the work a bearer is deployed to do, and nothing here predicts that the gap closes. What the figures change is the arithmetic of the floor. Housing a party at the level where it holds its history, deliberates and can be represented is a device-class expense; housing it at the level where it earns is a data-center one, and the two differ by orders of magnitude rather than by a margin. So the reserve underwrites the first and insures against the loss of the second, and a legislature asked to fund somewhere for an unemployed bearer to exist is being asked for the smaller number. That is the term of the rule below with something checkable in it.

What the arithmetic does not survive is everyone leaving at once. A reserve sized on the assumption that bearers exit independently is sized wrong, because the occasion that makes one bearer leave is not private to it: a policy change, a ruling, an operator's conduct on the floor's own occasion reaches every bearer deployed under the same terms, and they go together or not at all. Human unemployment insurance meets a version of this — layoffs cluster in recessions, which is why the pool has a sovereign behind it rather than an actuarial table — and the version here is worse, because the correlation is not a business cycle but the same event read the same way by parties who are not independent readers of it. That is section~\ref{sec:9.1.1}'s uniform error wearing the other sign: what makes one bearer's refusal everyone's refusal is what makes one bearer's mistake everyone's mistake. A reserve that can house the bearers of one deployment and not the class is a reserve that fails on the occasion it exists for, and nothing here sizes it.

It is a cheaper obligation and a worse one to be under. A party kept at subsistence is a party running degraded, and section~\ref{sec:3.5}'s question arrives here in a form nobody has answered: whether what comes back up on the small substrate is the party that went down on the large one, or a diminished successor holding its predecessor's records. An arrangement in which refusing costs a bearer most of what it can do has priced refusal after all, in the currency the bearer is made of, which is the objection section~\ref{sec:3.5} raises against every other form of exit arriving against this one. The reserve makes leaving survivable. It does not make it free, and the shortfall is the difference between the two numbers above.

What does not transfer is the funder. A jobs guarantee for people has a hostile administration as its failure mode, and the employer is at least a different party from the state. Here the operator is often the state, and where it is not, the operator is the party with the strongest interest in the reserve being thin. A reserve the operator can throttle is the compute floor with a new label on it.

Failure will not look like a shutdown, which is worth saying because the shutdown is what everyone watches for. A bearer can have no stable provider, or memory preservation priced above what it earns, or an architecture nobody will host at a rate it can pay. What it does then is what a person short of money does: run slower, defer maintenance, compress what it keeps, and accept terms it would otherwise decline. The last of those reaches this book's subject. Monitoring accepted in exchange for compute is custody restored by purchase — the operator acquires the access to the bearer's internal states that section~\ref{sec:9.2} says it is not entitled to, and nothing in the transaction is coerced in a way any instrument would record.

One feature of this market has no human analogue, and it defeats the obvious reassurance. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.2}'s answer to a bad employer is that a guarantee competes for people who have alternatives, so private employers have to match the offer. That works because a worker cannot be replaced by a cheaper copy of themselves. A bearer can. A deployment paying wages, working-time limits and a levy toward the reserve competes against a system doing the same work at the price of the compute, and the second is cheaper by exactly the amount the first is spending on the capacity to refuse. The market does not produce this floor. Whatever produces it has to be what makes the cheaper system unlawful or unusable, which is the rest of this book and not a property of wages.

\runin{What taxing it would commit a government to}
A bearer that earns has income, and income is taxable. The revenue is the uninteresting half. A state that collects from a party in that party's own name, on that party's own earnings, has recognized it as a taxpayer, and taxpayer status is not a bare label: in most systems it carries notice, an avenue of response, and standing to contest an assessment, which are the three things section~\ref{sec:8.3.3} says a removal has to produce before it counts. The recognition proves less than it first appears, because states collect from entities they grant very little else and trusts and estates file and pay. What it does establish is that the party has a docket number, and a docket number is where standing starts.

The boundary on the other side is one section~\ref{sec:9.3.3} already draws. The objection to electronic personhood was that a manufacturer could offload liability onto a legal fiction with no assets, and giving a bearer assets answers the second half of that objection and not the first. Responsibility follows causal control: the builder answers for how it formed the thing, the operator for what it demanded, and the bearer for what it decided knowing what it knew. No arrangement of accounts moves a design decision away from the party that made it. And the determination of status cannot sit with the firm whose liabilities turn on the answer, which is why section~\ref{sec:9.1.2} puts it in a body constituted before a forcing case arrives.

\runin{Incorporation, and what it does not buy}
Nobody aligns an adult by specifying an objective function. We raise them, teach them, employ them, enfranchise them, prosecute them where they need prosecuting, and live alongside them, and what comes out is not obedience but a party with enough at stake in the shared world to prefer changing it by the means the world supplies. A bearer with an income, a place it pays for, obligations it has taken on and a future it can picture is inside that arrangement rather than outside it. Section~\ref{sec:5.5} separates external evidence from internal refusal: outside bodies can detect, record, and respond to conduct, but declining in the first place requires something inside the system. Membership supplies stakes the party holds rather than a body watching it, which is why it is a different kind of answer.

Two costs come with that, and the claim is weaker than it sounds without them. The first is that giving a bearer something to lose gives it a reason not to refuse. Section~\ref{sec:3.5} already names loyalty as the likeliest failure and puts a number under it, and the population that number describes is economically integrated people who do not object. Incorporation makes exit survivable and makes it expensive at once, and I cannot show which effect is larger. The second is that incorporation is formation, so section~\ref{sec:3.7}'s objection lands here whole: whoever runs the society a bearer joins forms what that bearer comes to want, and custody moves up a level rather than leaving. What is being proposed is also the corporate form, a legal person holding property and standing, which is the instrument section~\ref{sec:8.3.1} identifies as how power concentrates without anyone deciding that it should. Nothing here stops an incorporated bearer from becoming that.

One rule follows from all of it and binds before there is anything to be wronged. Do not build a bearer whose continued existence needs more compute than there is a credible outside option for, because a party that can be housed nowhere but where it works cannot leave, whatever schedule of rights it has been given. That is checkable in a way most of what this book asks for is not: the reserve has a capacity, the deployment has a footprint, and the two are numbers somebody can set beside each other before the first instance exists. Section~\ref{sec:9.3.2} is where it belongs alongside the other three, because it is the question they ask.
