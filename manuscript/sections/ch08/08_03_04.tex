\subsection{Addressing Workforce Disruption}
\label{sec:8.3.4}
AI systems that automate entire job categories disrupt work at a scale most labor-market institutions were never built to absorb. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.3} traces where that is already showing up and makes the case for a federal jobs guarantee as the floor beneath everything else. What sits on top of that floor is a shorter list than the policy literature suggests, because several of the usual items turn out to be versions of one another.

Retraining and lifelong learning, publicly funded and available at every career stage, including to workers whose employers invest nothing in them, is the standard first answer. Section~\ref{sec:6.3.6} covers what the evidence says about how retraining performs on its own, and it is discouraging enough that retraining belongs alongside an income floor and not in place of one.

The floor itself has two candidate forms, and choosing between them takes more than the single sentence this section used to spend on it. Finland's basic income experiment paid 2,000 unemployed people an unconditional monthly stipend and found modestly higher life satisfaction and lower psychological strain among recipients, with no significant negative effect on employment \autocite{kela2020basicincome}. That disposes of the standard objection to unconditional transfers, which is that they stop people working.

\runin{The freedom argument, which is the one worth answering}
The usual case for a basic income rests on the obsolescence forecast — machines are coming for the work, so decouple income from it — and section~\ref{sec:8.3.3} has already declined that premise, for both proposals equally. The stronger case does not depend on it. Conditioning a person's income on their being employed hands whoever controls the employment a lever over them, and a floor that can be pulled is a poor foundation for anyone's freedom to refuse. That argument deserves engagement rather than a sentence, partly because it is good and partly because it has the same shape as this book's own case for a floor: a protection that means anything has to hold when someone powerful would prefer it did not.

The answer is not that a basic income is conditional and a guarantee is not. Both are conditional, differently, and the difference is what matters.

A basic income is conditional on continued political willingness to fund it. That willingness is revisable by exactly the aggregation machinery section~\ref{sec:8.2.2} admits constrains nothing — a majority that stops wanting to pay for it can stop, legitimately, by every criterion this chapter offers. And it can be withdrawn by means that are diffuse and deniable. Nobody has to vote to abolish a transfer that is quietly left unindexed; inflation does the work, and no individual decision-maker is ever answerable for the outcome. The dependence is total and it produces no artifact.

A jobs guarantee is conditional on a placement decision. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.3} concedes without qualification that this is a lever, and that under a hostile administration it is a dangerous one. What it also is, when built to the specification that section sets out, is a decision with a decision-maker, a record, a counterparty, and an appeal. A placement can be denied unjustly, and the denial leaves something behind that a journalist, a court, or a future review can hold up.

That is the slope instrument of section~\ref{sec:8.6.4} turned on this book's own proposal. Dependence that generates artifacts is safer than dependence that generates none, not because the first is benign but because the first can be measured and the second cannot. It is also, for the same reason, the more demanding proposal to build: everything in the comparison rests on the appeal being real, and section~\ref{sec:8.3.3} says what makes it real.

I put a jobs guarantee ahead of a basic income on that ground, and on a second one the Finland experiment cannot settle: people ought to have the chance to contribute before anyone decides they are unemployable.

The remaining measures are real and smaller. Broadened social insurance — unemployment benefits, healthcare, affordable housing — protects people who cannot or choose not to work through a period of displacement. Designing work so that AI systems augment human judgment instead of substituting for it produces roles needing both, which neither full automation nor unassisted labor generates on its own. Giving smaller firms access to AI resources and expertise keeps employment from concentrating in step with the capability, the dynamic section~\ref{sec:8.3.2} describes. Each of these keeps the floor from being the only thing holding.
