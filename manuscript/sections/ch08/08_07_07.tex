\subsection{Military Applications and the Limits of Autonomy}
\label{sec:8.7.7}
Lethal autonomous weapons systems raise a specific version of the general problem: a machine making a targeting decision that used to require a person is a machine exercising judgment where the law of armed conflict has always assumed a human exercised it. Four questions recur across the debate:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Autonomy versus oversight: how much decision authority a system can hold before human oversight becomes nominal, present on paper but not inside the timeline the system actually operates on.
  \item Discrimination and proportionality: whether a system can reliably distinguish combatants from civilians and weigh military advantage against civilian harm the way international humanitarian law's principles of distinction and proportionality require \autocite{icrc1977protocol}.
  \item Accountability: who answers for a wrong engagement, the operator who deployed the system, the command that authorized it, or the developer who built it, when no single person made the specific decision.
  \item Auditability: whether the reasoning behind a targeting decision can be reconstructed and reviewed afterward, or disappears into a system no one outside its development team can examine.
\end{itemize}
International criminal law already has a doctrine built to answer the accountability question for a human subordinate: command responsibility, under which a commander who knew or should have known a subordinate was committing a war crime, and failed to prevent or punish it, bears criminal responsibility without having pulled the trigger personally \autocite{icc1998rome}. The doctrine does not transfer cleanly to a machine subordinate. A commander cannot know an algorithm's intentions the way they can infer a soldier's; punishment does not deter a weapon the way it deters a person who fears it; and the chain a court would need to reconstruct runs backward through training data and design decisions made years before the engagement, not through an order given minutes before it. Whether an autonomous weapon fits inside an accountability framework built for human chains of command, or needs one built from scratch, is unresolved by more than a technicality — existing law was not built with a nonhuman subordinate in mind.

There is a fourth party the list above does not name, and by the time this book was finished it had become the live one. The operator, the command and the developer are all inside the military. The developer of the model is not. A targeting platform assembled by a defense contractor now routinely incorporates a general-purpose model licensed from a commercial vendor — a vendor with a published acceptable-use policy, no contractual obligation to log how its model is used downstream, and no right to inspect the deployment. Neither doctrine reaches that party, and the reasons are worth stating because they are structural rather than accidental. Command responsibility does not reach the vendor because the vendor is not in the chain of command; nobody at the company can order a strike, prevent one, or punish anyone for one. Product liability does not reach it either, because the model is not defective in the sense the doctrine requires — it did what it was built to do, and it was integrated by a sophisticated intermediary who chose the application. The vendor sits in the gap between the two, and the gap was not designed; it is what you get when you apply two bodies of law written for soldiers and for manufacturers to an actor who is neither.

The vendor's own account of this is the one quoted in section~\ref{sec:6.4.1}: responsibility remains with the military organization. Taken as a claim about who decides, that is true. Taken as a claim about who could have known, it is a choice presented as a fact, which brings up the failure mode I think is most useful to name.

\runin{“I do not know whether my system was used”}
Section~\ref{sec:9.9} covers the responsibility gap in the sense Matthias gave it: as control over a learning system's behavior distributes across designers, trainers, and operators, no one party retains enough of it to be fairly held answerable, and the gap grows as the system becomes more autonomous. That is a gap in causal control.

What the vendor case exhibits is different and, I want to argue, more tractable. It is a gap in causal visibility, which is a different thing. The vendor cannot say whether its model contributed to a particular engagement — not because responsibility is distributed, but because nobody instrumented the question. No logging requirement was written into the contract, no inspection right was reserved, no audit channel exists. The company can truthfully say it does not know, and the truth of that statement is the product of decisions it participated in making.

That distinction matters because the two gaps have different fixes, and only one of them has a fix at all. Matthias's gap is a genuine property of learning systems and may not be closable. Absent visibility is an instrumentation choice. Logging is a solved engineering problem. Contractual inspection rights are ordinary commercial instruments. A vendor that wanted to know could require, as a condition of licensing into a targeting platform, that categories of use be recorded and made available to a named auditor. That such terms are not standard is a fact about market power and procurement leverage, not about the difficulty of the problem.

Naming it as fixable is the useful move, because it converts a lament into a specification. And it is worth being fair about what the record shows: the same commitments are not all of one kind. A vendor that refuses to permit fully autonomous weapons targeting or mass domestic surveillance, and holds that line when a government threatens contract termination and a supply-chain-risk designation and the dispute ends in litigation, has made a commitment with a price attached and paid it \autocite{crs2026pentagon}. A commitment to a use policy nobody can verify because nobody is logging anything is a different object wearing the same words. The first kind can fail and be seen to fail. The second cannot even be tested. Section~\ref{sec:8.4.2}'s distinction between a claim checked by a party with a stake and one checked by a party without applies here exactly, and this is the case that shows why it is not an academic distinction.

DARPA's LongShot program is a useful test case precisely because it resists the extremes. It develops an uncrewed aircraft, launched from a crewed fighter, that carries and fires air-to-air missiles at a target while the crewed aircraft stays farther from the engagement \autocite{darpa2026longshot}. That is autonomy in the sense of independent flight and weapons carriage, not autonomy in the sense of a machine deciding on its own whom to kill: the engagement decision still runs through the launching platform. Where later variants of this kind of system draw that line, and whether “keep the pilot farther from danger” quietly becomes “remove the pilot from the decision,” is the design question this technology eventually forces on whichever country builds it.

No binding international agreement governs any of this. States have negotiated inside the United Nations' Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons framework for more than a decade without producing one, converging on general language — meaningful human control matters, existing international humanitarian law applies — without settling what “meaningful” requires at machine speed \autocite{ccwgge2026chairsummary}. What exists instead is a set of practices individual militaries adopt on their own. The United States' Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, most recently updated in 2023, is the clearest published example: it requires that autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems be designed to allow “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force,” and distinguishes a fully autonomous system that selects and engages targets without further human intervention, “human out of the loop,” from a human-supervised system and a semi-autonomous one that only engages targets a person has already selected \autocite{dod2023autonomy}. What “appropriate” requires is left to the officers approving each system, which is either a sensible acknowledgment that context varies or a loophole big enough to drive a fully autonomous system through, depending on who is answering. None of this binds a state that declines to adopt a comparable policy, the same enforcement gap section~\ref{sec:6.4.2}'s account of technical safeguards runs into: a safeguard only the building state can verify is a policy, not a check.

The dynamic that worries most people who study this is not a single rogue system but an arms race in speed. If one state fields autonomous targeting because it computes faster than a human-supervised alternative, its competitors face pressure to match the speed rather than the oversight, and the state that keeps meaningful human control loses whatever tactical edge the state that dropped it gains. Slowing that dynamic is not a problem any single government's domestic policy can solve on its own; it needs the kind of coordination the next section takes up.

Civil society has pushed for that coordination to take the form of an outright ban rather than a code of practice. The Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of nongovernmental organizations founded in 2013, has lobbied for a binding treaty prohibiting weapons that select and engage targets without human control, and a UN General Assembly resolution on autonomous weapons has passed by a lopsided majority in successive years without producing the treaty the coalition wants \autocite{unga2023laws}. The gap between a resolution most states will vote for and a treaty the states that build these systems will sign is the same gap this section keeps running into: agreement is easy at the level of principle and hard at the level of a specific weapon a specific military does not want to give up.
