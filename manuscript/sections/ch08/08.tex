\chapter{Collaboration, Policy, and Governance}
\label{sec:8}
Chapter~\ref{sec:6} asked what a design can do to resist authoritarian capture — technical safeguards, policy levers that raise the cost of misuse. None of that gets built by one person working alone, and none of it becomes the norm because it is a good idea. This chapter covers both halves: the people and institutions that have to coordinate for such a system to get built at all, and the policy that decides whether anyone has to use it.

Sections~\ref{sec:8.1} and \ref{sec:8.2} take up coordination. Interdisciplinary collaboration first — the practical mechanics of getting a neuroscientist, an ethicist, and a machine-learning researcher to work together rather than past each other — then the public: what stakeholder engagement looks like, as against the appearance of it, and why every mechanism in that section aggregates public preference without constraining it.

Sections~\ref{sec:8.3} through \ref{sec:8.6} turn to policy. What economic incentives shape whether an ethical system gets built at all; what policy frameworks and industry practice actually enforce; what privacy and security mean for a system trained on people's data; and what accountability looks like once a system might be a subject rather than only a tool. Section~\ref{sec:8.7} closes with governance across borders, where jurisdictions agree on none of this uniformly.

A well-designed system built inside institutions with no reason to demand one is a design nobody has to use. That is why the coordination and the policy belong in one chapter: neither works without the other.
