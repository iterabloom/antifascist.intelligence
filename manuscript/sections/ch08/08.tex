\chapter{Coordination, the Public, and the Political Economy}
\label{sec:8}
Chapter~\ref{sec:6} asked what a design can do to resist authoritarian capture, and chapter~\ref{sec:7} what becomes of the dissent a system is built to hear. None of that gets built by one person working alone, and none of it becomes the norm because it is a good idea: a well-designed system built inside institutions with no reason to demand one is a design nobody has to use.

Three chapters take up what sits outside the system. This one is about the conditions under which anyone builds it at all — who has to be in the room, whose preferences count, and what the money rewards. Chapter~\ref{sec:9} is about what obliges anyone to: policy, privacy law, and who answers when a system harms someone or is harmed itself. Chapter~\ref{sec:10} is about jurisdictions, which agree on none of it.

It starts with interdisciplinary collaboration, because neuroscience, psychology, philosophy and political science each catch failure modes the others are structurally blind to, and a lab staffed only by machine-learning researchers is blind the way its field is. It then turns to the public — what engagement looks like as against the appearance of it — and closes on the limit its mechanisms share: all of them make public preference more legible, and not one of them protects anybody from it. It closes on the economics, where the reward structure settles what gets built long before an ethics review sees it.
