8.3.3. A Jobs Guarantee, and the Objection It Has to Answer
As AI systems automate tasks once performed by people, demand for advanced technical skills — AI development, data analysis — grows alongside a shrinking need for the job types most exposed to automation. Left unmanaged, that combination widens income inequality and strains the social fabric across whichever sector it hits hardest.

Manufacturing shows what this looks like at scale. An estimated 5.7 million factory jobs disappeared in the United States between 2000 and 2010, and while economists still dispute how much of that loss traces to automation rather than trade with China, automation and changed production methods account for a substantial share of it [[cite:C0383]]. Retail is following the same path: Amazon opened its first cashier-less Go store to the public in 2018, and stores built on that model displace the specific human roles — cashier, floor sales — that retail automation targets first [[cite:C0384]].

The response has to be more direct than encouraging retraining and hoping employers fund it. Employers will invest in continuous education for their workers at the scale this requires once they cannot afford not to, and the way to force that scenario is a federal jobs guarantee: government as employer of last resort, hiring anyone who applies, where they live, at a living wage, with paid on-the-job training built into the position instead of offered in place of one. Section 8.3.4 develops the rest of the policy toolkit for workforce disruption. Since this is one of the few proposals in this book ambitious enough that a reader is entitled to ask what it costs and what is wrong with it, the rest of this section answers both.

<<h>>The proposal does not rest on a forecast<</h>>
Almost every argument for a policy of this kind is staked on a prediction about how much work machines will take, which is a bad place to stake it. The predictions are contested, they have been wrong in both directions for two centuries, and a proposal that stands or falls with them inherits their fragility. I want to disown that framing before using any of the numbers, because the case I am making does not need it.

Both the maximal-displacement scenario and the standard argument for a universal basic income share an assumption that will not survive being stated: that there is a fixed quantity of work in the world, and that machines doing some of it leaves less for people. Set that assumption beside the two categories where labor is most obviously short. Care — of the old, the sick, the very young, the disabled — is not a bounded quantity that could be finished. Neither is the repair of a damaged environment: the seawalls, the remediated ground, the restored watersheds, the retrofits. In both, the number of hours a society could usefully absorb is limited by what it is willing to pay for, and not by how much needs doing.

The evidence for that is in the wage. Federal projections put direct care among the largest sources of job openings in the American economy — roughly 9.7 million openings across the occupation between 2024 and 2034, the bulk of them from workers leaving rather than from growth — against a median wage of $17.36 an hour in 2024 [[cite:C0721]]. An occupation cannot simultaneously be short of workers and unable to raise pay unless something other than scarcity is setting the price. What that number describes is a shortage of willingness, which is a political fact and not a technological one.

The disciplined version of the claim concedes what it should. Tasks inside those categories will be automated, some of them soon, and I am not predicting otherwise. The claim is about the binding constraint: what limits how many people are employed caring for other people is what society will pay, and that holds at any level of machine capability. A jobs guarantee justified this way needs no view about how fast the models improve.

<<h>>What it would cost<</h>>
The most developed costings come from the Levy Economics Institute, whose April 2018 simulation of a program employing between eleven and sixteen million people at fifteen dollars an hour plus benefits put the net budgetary impact at between 0.8 and 2 percent of GDP, with GDP itself rising and private-sector employment increasing by three to four million [[cite:C0716]]. The wage figure has to be read with its date attached: fifteen dollars in early 2018 is worth about twenty in 2026, and a program legislated at the nominal figure would be legislating a cut [[cite:C0722]]. Some critics reach higher gross figures — around 2.4 percent of GDP, which would make the program larger than Medicaid — and argue that it would be unmanageable at scale, disruptive to private labor markets, and inflationary [[cite:C0717]].

Now the part the arithmetic above does not cover, which I would rather put in the accounting than leave for a reviewer to find. The Levy simulation is calibrated against existing unemployment and underemployment. It models a residual employer: a program that picks up the people the private labor market has already put down. The version I am arguing for is not that. A floor that pays a living wage and builds training into the post competes for people who have alternatives, and it is supposed to. Take-up under a competitive guarantee exceeds anything a residual-employer simulation would produce, so the honest way to read the Levy range is as a floor on the cost and not an estimate of it.

That is a larger number and it is the mechanism working. Participation is not a symptom of a guarantee going wrong; it is the success condition of a floor that competes, because the entire theory of the thing is that private employers must match the offer to recruit against it. A version nobody joins has failed. This also disposes of the "unmanageable at scale" objection, which treats scale as evidence of malfunction when on this account scale is the instrument.

A reader is owed a bound rather than a shrug, and owed the truth about where the bound comes from. Suppose take-up runs at twice what the simulation modeled and the net impact doubles with it, to around 4 percent of GDP. The doubling is illustrative and I am not deriving it. Deriving it would mean estimating how many workers currently hold jobs paying less than the guarantee's wage-and-benefit package and would move, which requires a wage distribution matched against the package's full value, and nobody has done that for this design — the Levy simulation did not, because a residual employer does not need it. Nor would a doubling of take-up produce exactly a doubling of net cost: the offsets in the paragraphs below scale with participation too, and in opposite directions. So treat 4 percent as an illustrative upper bound chosen to be uncomfortable, and treat the argument that follows as one about whether a program of that size is thinkable, which does not depend on the multiplier being right. That is a very large program, and it is inside the range American government already operates in: federal spending on Medicare runs about 3.3 percent of GDP net of premiums and other offsetting receipts, and Social Security about 5.2 percent [[cite:C0723]]. A program at 4 percent would sit between the two on that measure. Measured gross, Medicare is nearer 4 percent and the program would sit alongside it rather than above it, which does not change the argument and is worth stating so that nobody has to go and check. It would be among the largest things the federal government does, which is an argument about priorities and political will, and not the reductio it is usually offered as.

Two things scale with participation that no simulation of this kind captures, and both belong on the page. The first is administrative capacity — placement, supervision, training, payroll — which is where a guarantee visibly fails first and which does not expand on the schedule a budget line does. The second is the private-sector wage response. Employers competing against a decent floor raise pay, which raises tax receipts and lowers spending on the transfer programs that top up low wages. That is not a rounding error to be waved at. It is the fiscal signature of the mechanism succeeding, and a costing that omits it is measuring the expense of the policy while ignoring its intended effect.

<<h>>The budget frame is this book's own signature<</h>>
There is a reason "does it fail on arithmetic" felt like the question, and this book has already named the pattern under another heading. A fixed pot of money converts interdependent public commitments into competitors, so that care, remediation, housing and education have to argue against one another over which is sufficiently basic to be included — and "sufficiently basic" is a category that can always be narrowed by whoever is doing the counting. That is section 2.1.4's second structural feature operating on public finance: a measure standing in for the thing measured, the substitution experienced as rigor, and everything outside the number going unnamed rather than being refuted.

An earlier draft of this section said I was not competent to adjudicate a macroeconomic dispute and would not pretend the estimates were settled. Chapter 0 commits this book to disclosing what I actually think, and by that standard the sentence was a false report: I have read the objections to the fiscal framing and I do not find most of them persuasive. So, plainly, the position. A government that issues its own currency is not revenue-constrained in the way a household is, and the real limits on a program of this size are productive capacity, available labor, and administrative competence rather than a financial ceiling that exists before any of those bind.

That is more than the argument needs, and I will take only the narrow premise: the constraint is real resources and coordination, so the question a jobs guarantee faces is whether the people and the organizing capacity exist to absorb it, and not whether the money can be found. On the wider claim there is a live disagreement worth naming precisely instead of dismissing. The strongest version concerns the interest-income channel — that sustained deficits at scale pay a growing stream of interest to bondholders, which is itself a fiscal injection with distributional consequences, and which complicates the tidy account of how the constraint binds [[cite:C0724]]. That objection is a real one and I do not have a settled answer to it. It also does not touch the narrow premise, since it is an argument about the consequences of the financing rather than about whether a currency issuer must find the money first.

What I want to avoid is the move chapter 9 is careful not to make elsewhere: sorting an argument by who is making it. The objections here get answered as objections, and characterizing the people raising them by motive would be the same substitution of the label for the thing that this section has just accused the budget frame of.

<<h>>Skill mismatch is not the objection<</h>>
The complaint that looks strongest is skill mismatch: a displaced radiologist, paralegal, or copywriter is not obviously served by a placement in elder care or infrastructure, and a guarantee that offers one is offering a floor, which is a smaller thing than a career. I treated that as decisive in an earlier version. It is not, and the reason it is not is a diagnosis this book has already made in another domain.

The objection collapses an occupation into its most automatable tasks. What gets automated first is the legible part — the countable, specifiable, gradeable part — and legibility is a poor proxy for what a job actually is. Document review was never the paralegal's work; it was the fraction of the paralegal's work that could be counted. Image classification was never the radiologist's. The mistake is the aestheticized metric again, this time applied to labor economics: the measurable component substitutes for the occupation, and what falls outside the measure goes unnamed. Once the substitution is undone, the distance the objection depends on shrinks. The paralegal's actual competence is advocacy inside an administrative system — benefits appeals, guardianship, elder-law casework — and those are not distant from the placements a guarantee would offer. They are adjacent to them.

I have also dropped a phrase that was doing damage. This section used to speak of offering someone a "public job," two paragraphs before rebutting the charge that the work would be make-work, and the phrase carries the connotation the rebuttal is trying to remove. The rebuttal itself stands: the categories in section 8.3.4's list are chronically understaffed at current wages, which is a fact about pricing and not about whether the work is needed.

<<h>>Training is the mechanism, and the evidence says so<</h>>
The natural next move is to disclaim the guarantee as a retraining program, and this book made it. That disclaimer contradicts the proposal's own definition, three paragraphs above, which specifies paid on-the-job training built into the position. Training is constitutive here and not adjacent, so disclaiming it gives away the design.

Worse, the disclaimer misreads evidence this book presents itself. Section 10.3.2 reports an evaluation of the main American channel for retraining displaced workers against twenty-three million participation records, and the finding is not that training fails. It is that classroom retraining, delivered outside a job, rarely moved a participant into work materially less exposed to automation — and that employer-led apprenticeship, in the same data, performed better [[cite:C0664]]. That is evidence for embedded, paid, in-post training. It is evidence for the mechanism a guarantee supplies and the evaluated program structurally could not, since a program that trains people and then returns them to a labor market to look for work is not able to place anyone by construction.

Reading it correctly changes what the proposal is. A guarantee that trains in post is not a bare floor; it is a competitive one. If public employment offers continual learning, a growth trajectory, and decent conditions, then private employers recruiting from that pool have to compete on the quality of the employment experience and not only on the wage. And it dissolves the skill-mismatch worry a second time, by a different route: a placement that carries training is an entry point to a trajectory rather than a terminus, which is what made "a floor is smaller than a career" sound like a concession that had to be made.

<<h>>Status, and against which baseline<</h>>
This section used to concede that a guarantee preserves nobody's earnings or status, and that I had no answer. The concession measures the guarantee against the job the person used to have, which is not among the options. The available alternative is unemployment, and against that baseline the claim inverts.

Unemployment destroys status, and the evidence is unusually good. A meta-analysis covering 237 cross-sectional and 87 longitudinal studies found unemployed people substantially more distressed than employed people — 34 percent showing psychological problems against 16 percent among the employed — with the longitudinal studies and natural experiments supporting the causal direction rather than only the correlation [[cite:C0725]]. The damage compounds through the labor market as well: field experiments sending matched applications to real vacancies find callback rates falling with the length of an employment gap, so time out of work is itself the disqualification [[cite:C0726]]. A guarantee that keeps someone employed, paid, and learning is producing exactly the standing that the counterfactual takes away.

That is not a side benefit. Section 5.1.4 identifies eroding trust, humiliation and social fracture as the conditions authoritarian movements recruit from, which means a guarantee operates directly on the mechanism this book says it cares about. The proposal's strongest argument is the one the earlier draft conceded away.

A residual survives, and it is narrow enough to state exactly. What a guarantee cannot preserve is relative standing within a profession, for people at the top of the wage distribution whose identity is bound to a specific expertise. That is a real loss and I have no answer to it. It is a much smaller claim than the one I was making.

<<h>>The objection this proposal actually has to answer<</h>>
Here is the hard one, and this book supplied it. A guarantee is only as good as the state administering it, and employer of last resort under a hostile administration is a placement lever with a compliance condition attached. Refuse the placement and lose the floor. Speak against the government that employs you and find the placement changes. Everything this book says about how a channel becomes an instrument of the thing it was built to check applies with full force to its own flagship proposal, and it is a far harder objection than skill mismatch.

The answer is a design specification and not a reassurance, because reassurance is what section 8.6.4 says cannot be audited. Three conditions:

<<list>>
- Placements are appealable, to a body that did not make them, on a record that survives the appeal. A placement decision that leaves no artifact is a placement decision that cannot be reviewed.
- Refusal rights are real and priced by the person refusing. Section 8.6.4's distinction governs here: the same written justification is dissent when the dissenter prices it and tribute when the gatekeeper does. A worker who can decline a placement without losing the guarantee has a refusal right; one who can decline only by exiting the program has none.
- The people who audit placements are not the people who make them. This is the same separation section 8.6.4 requires of anyone measuring the slope, and for the same reason: a reviewer that acquires the power to place becomes the thing it was built to watch.
<</list>>

The standard refrain for programs of this kind — federally funded, locally administered — survives, though its usual rationale is the weaker one. The reason is not local fit. It is redundancy. Many administrators, separately capturable, mean there is no single point at which a hostile executive flips the program, and capturing a thousand local authorities is a different and much more visible undertaking than capturing one federal one.

It cuts both ways, and pretending otherwise would be the kind of one-sided safeguard this chapter criticizes elsewhere. Local administration is exactly where a hostile local authority attaches its own conditions to a placement, and there the federal funder is the check. Neither layer is safe on its own. Two live layers are harder to capture than either alone, which is the whole of the claim.

One distinction has to be explicit, because the same words describe a very different policy. Devolution-to-shrink hands the obligation down without the money, so that a program can be starved locally while the center points at local administration. The test that separates them is checkable: whether funding is unconditional and centrally guaranteed while placement is local. If the funding can be withheld from a jurisdiction, the redundancy argument above is void and the structure has become the lever it was supposed to prevent.

That is the standard this book demands of everyone else's safeguards, applied here. The point it establishes is narrower than the section as a whole: a floor under wages and training is what makes the roof possible, and a floor that can be revoked by whoever is in office is not a floor.

Interdisciplinary research and training funding matters alongside the guarantee, for a more specific reason: bridging the divide between technical AI development and the fields that study its consequences produces people who can do both, which a labor market retraining displaced factory workers into six-month coding bootcamps mostly does not. I am skeptical that night classes serve this population as well as paid time during the working day would, which is the same argument as the one above — the point of a guarantee is to make the education part of the job.
