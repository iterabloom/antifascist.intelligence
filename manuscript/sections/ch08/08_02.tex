\section{The institutions the ratio presumes}
\label{sec:7.2}
Here is what a pipeline would look like with the adverse party built in.

What stays unified is command, operational secrecy, and the capacity to act at tempo. What is held adversely is the evidence bearing on civilian harm, the reviewer denominator, and the record. A pipeline divided that way, and a military that runs one, I call \emph{reconstructed}. The evidence is composed by a civilian-harm review body outside the chain of command, with its own sources, including the protected-site coordinates humanitarian organizations already submit for deconfliction, received with standing. The denominator is staffed and funded by that body, so the command can't cut it as it cut the Central Command team; and the body's own funding is a permanent appropriation the Treasury pays without request or approval, so that cutting it takes a repeal in the open and not a budget line. The 2022 civilian-harm action plan was a first attempt at the separation; its dismantling is an argument for making it harder to dismantle.

Synthetic autonomous systems reach the military as they reach any deployment a floor covers: formed in the schools of Part~V beside human students, measured by someone the builder didn't pick, against a standard a legislature has published, and licensed under the standard terms, adverse review and a written refusal right among them. A line one vendor held by contract would thereby be a standard term.

Precedents show authority outside the executive working inside real wars. Coalition “red card” holders in NATO operations could veto targets involving their assets, and Germany's Bundestag must approve each armed deployment. Both have limits: a red card protects the vetoing state's interests and assets, which may not include civilians, and parliamentary approval is given per mission and never reaches a target. Both are nonetheless candidates for the adverse party the review ratio presumes, and a red-card holder is the rare candidate positioned to act at tempo. What a machine bearer adds here is least: acts assembled from permitted requests, cases no person sees, and the moment mid-act when an interrupt is still possible. It depends on the institutions for evidence and not for continuity: the deployment condition is met here by the same bodies whose presence narrows its role. The comparison is not a choice between them. The institutions are humans in a room composed so they can refuse, and that room is built whether or not a machine bearer ever exists; the question is only what one would add to it.

One requirement is added for a reconstructed military, and it is a staffing requirement rather than an office: a civilian-harm review body whose reviewers are drawn by lot and cleared only after the draw, under the clearance rule of \S\ref{sec:15.5}, paid by a permanent appropriation the Treasury pays without request or approval, and counted in the denominator. Its evidence is a log the command doesn't keep; its demand for proofs is the targeted right of \S\ref{sec:13.5}; and its veto is a tripwire on the ratio: a case released outside the pre-registered figures ends the deployment's funding at the close of the period by itself, unless Congress enacts its continuation (\S\ref{sec:13.6}). Nobody chairs it, and the command can't cut what it never appointed.

Who sits is settled before any war. The reviewers are drawn from a standing pool nominated by the members of the \emph{Association}, a membership body of organizations that serve the people a floor protects, under a charter chapter~\ref{sec:27} sets out. Residents of places where armed force is used are one of the classes the charter names, so the humanitarian organizations that submit protected-site coordinates for deconfliction may join and nominate if they meet its eligibility test. Nobody in the pool needs a qualification beyond the training a schedule in the statute names, given after the draw at public expense. The draw is a standing one, held in peacetime for fixed terms, so the time a clearance takes is spent before any campaign and not on its first day. Its size is read from the ratio: the operator declares before the draw the highest generation rate it means to run at, and that rate, at the minimum review time the record's recipient fixes, gives the number of reviewers who must be seated and cleared before any deployment runs at it. A faster rate waits for the next draw and its clearances. A seat a denial empties stays empty until the next draw: no deployment may pre-register a rate that needs it, and one already running when it empties is in breach of its denominator, which fires on funding. Whether a person who isn't a citizen may sit is not decided here, and it matters because the people these acts fall on are outside the public that decides what is done to them, so a body of citizens alone would review harm to people with no seat on it.

\emph{What this doesn't do.} Without machine bearers in its ranks, a reconstructed military accepts a speed disadvantage against rivals that don't reconstruct: the minimum review time it pre-registers is a ceiling on its tempo, set by a recipient it doesn't control, and a rival that pre-registers nothing can condense hours into minutes, as the Maven pipeline did. The floor costs a just defender more than that: its claims about incoming attacks are discounted, the action threshold sits above one, and the person-directed terms bar practices the law permits. What the defender buys with the price is a pipeline that will refuse the next government's war of choice as it refused the last one's, and how much speed that costs is left open.
