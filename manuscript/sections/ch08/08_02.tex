\section{The institutions the ratio presumes}
\label{sec:7.2}
Here is what a pipeline would look like with the adverse party built in.

What stays unified is command, operational secrecy and the capacity to act at tempo. What is held adversely is the evidence bearing on civilian harm, the reviewer denominator, and the record. A pipeline divided that way, and a military that runs one, I call \emph{reconstructed}. The evidence is composed by a civilian-harm review body outside the chain of command, with its own sources, including the protected-site coordinates humanitarian organizations already submit for deconfliction, received with standing. The denominator is staffed and funded by that body, so the command can't cut it as it cut the Central Command team; and the body's own funding is a permanent appropriation the Treasury pays without request or approval, so that cutting it takes a repeal in the open and not a budget line. The 2022 civilian-harm action plan was a first attempt at the separation; its dismantling is an argument for making it harder to dismantle, not evidence that it can't exist.

Models reach the military as they reach any deployment a floor covers: formed in the schools of Part~V beside human students, measured by a measurer the builder didn't pick, under the registration standard, and licensed under the standard terms, adverse review and a written refusal right among them. A line one vendor held by contract is then a standard term, and nothing about the military calls for an office of its own to license models; what is military-specific is the evidence and the denominator, and the review body holds those.

Precedents show authority outside the executive working inside real wars. Coalition “red card” holders in NATO operations could veto targets involving their assets, and Germany's Bundestag must approve each armed deployment. Both have limits: a red card protects the vetoing state's interests and assets, which may not include civilians, and parliamentary approval is given per mission and never reaches a target. Both are nonetheless candidates for the adverse party the review ratio presumes, and a red-card holder is the rare candidate positioned to act at tempo. What a machine bearer adds here is least: acts assembled from permitted requests, cases no person sees, and the moment mid-act when an interrupt is still possible. It depends on the institutions for evidence and not for continuity: the deployment condition is met here by the same bodies whose presence narrows its role. The institutions it is compared with are people in a room composed so they can refuse.

One requirement is added for a reconstructed military, and it is a staffing requirement rather than an office: a civilian-harm review body whose reviewers are drawn by lot from a cleared pool under the clearance rule of \S\ref{sec:15.5}, paid by a permanent appropriation the Treasury pays without request or approval, and counted in the denominator. Its evidence is a log the command doesn't keep; its demand for proofs is the targeted right of \S\ref{sec:13.5}; and its veto is a tripwire on the ratio: a case released outside the pre-registered figures ends the deployment's funding at the close of the period by itself, unless Congress enacts its continuation (\S\ref{sec:13.6}). Nobody chairs it, and the command can't cut what it never appointed.

\emph{What this doesn't do.} A reconstructed military accepts a speed disadvantage against rivals that don't reconstruct: the minimum review time it pre-registers is a ceiling on its tempo, set by a recipient it doesn't control, and a rival that pre-registers nothing can condense hours into minutes, as the Maven pipeline did. The floor costs a just defender more than that: its claims about incoming attacks are discounted, the action threshold sits above one, and the person-directed terms bar practices the law permits. What the defender buys with the price is a pipeline that will refuse the next government's war of choice as it refused the last one's, and how much speed that costs is left open.
