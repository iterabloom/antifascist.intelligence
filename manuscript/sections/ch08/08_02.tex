\section{Stakeholder Involvement and Public Engagement}
\label{sec:8.2}
Democratizing AI development means building the people affected by a system into how it gets built, not just consulting them once it already exists. Regulators set the legal boundaries around data, privacy, and accountability. Developers and researchers understand a system's actual capabilities and failure modes better than anyone outside the lab. End users find the places a design breaks down in practice that no design team anticipated. Communities already carrying the costs of the digital divide have the clearest view of who a system leaves out. Civil-society organizations raise the political questions a technologist is prone to missing. Leave any one of these groups out, and a decision that affects all of them gets made by whoever is left in the room.

One structural way to build that involvement is the citizen jury: a panel of randomly selected, non-expert citizens deliberates on a specific question and produces recommendations that feed into policy. Section~\ref{sec:5.4.3} covers Taiwan's vTaiwan process, the clearest working example of that approach applied specifically to AI.

Three pieces of what this looks like in practice follow: bringing diverse stakeholders directly into development work, building the structures that let the public deliberate on AI governance, and building the literacy that makes public input worth having in the first place.
