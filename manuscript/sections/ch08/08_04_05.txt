8.4.5. Encouraging the Development of Altruistic, Antifascist, and Ethical AI Systems
Sections 8.4.1 through 8.4.4 covered the instruments — national guidelines, self-regulation, public-private partnerships, legal status. What they are instruments for is the subject of this closing section: AI development that is altruistic and antifascist by design.

<<h>>Policies to Counteract Authoritarian Uses of AI<</h>>
Section 6.4.3 covers the core policy toolkit here — export controls, prohibited-practices regulation, developer-facing standards — and section 5.6.3 covers the safeguards a system needs built in from the start. One piece belongs upstream of all of them: measurement.

A government cannot restrict what it cannot see. The Carnegie Endowment's AI Global Surveillance Index assessed 176 countries and found AI-enabled surveillance technology already in active use in at least 75 of them [[cite:C0409]]. That number is the evidence base for a countermeasure and is not itself one. It also demonstrates what a measurement instrument has to do to be worth the effort: count deployments instead of commitments, name the countries, and publish on a schedule that makes a change from one year to the next visible. An index reporting only that surveillance AI exists somewhere in the world would give a policymaker nothing to act on, and most published AI-governance indicators are closer to that than to this one.

<<h>>AI Systems That Support Democratic Values<</h>>
Section 5.4.6 works through what this looks like with working examples — content scoring that informs a moderator without removing the content, structured public input on contested policy questions — along with the caution that belongs next to each.

One gap those examples leave open is election-integrity monitoring specifically: a system built to flag anomalies in real-time reporting, or to detect coordinated interference during a vote. What would make such a system trustworthy is the list section 5.4.6 already gives for any AI role in a democratic process — privacy-preserving design, an audit trail, and a multi-stakeholder review body that includes more than whichever party happens to hold power. That last condition is the one that fails first, and it fails structurally: an election-monitoring system is procured by an incumbent government, and an incumbent that chooses who audits the count has already answered the question the audit exists to ask.
