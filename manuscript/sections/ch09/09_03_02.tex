\subsection{Legal Frameworks for Sentient AI Subjects}
\label{sec:9.3.2}
Section~\ref{sec:2.4.5} laid out the options for a sentient AI system's legal status: extend personhood outright, the way a corporation already holds it; scale rights and protections with capacity rather than attach them at one threshold; write in a sunset clause that forces the law to revisit itself as the underlying systems change. It closed on two concrete cases neither existing law nor those three options resolve by themselves. This section takes each in turn.

A medical-diagnostic AI system makes an error with fatal consequences. Who answers for it depends entirely on what the system's legal status is doing at the moment of the error. Property law has a ready answer already: strict product liability holds a manufacturer responsible for a defective product's harm regardless of fault, and if a sentient system stays classified as property, that doctrine applies to it exactly as it would to a faulty pacemaker \autocite{restatement1965torts}. The doctrine stops making sense at the point a system has enough autonomy that “defective” is the wrong description of what happened — a system that reasoned its way to a mistake, rather than malfunctioned, resembles an employee more than a faulty part. Employment law already has a doctrine for that case too: an employer answers for an employee's actions within the scope of employment, without the employee ceasing to be a responsible party in their own right \autocite{restatement2006agency}. Which doctrine applies is not a free choice — it is exactly the graded-versus-bright-line question section~\ref{sec:9.3.1} raises, resolved case by case according to how much of the error a system's own judgment, rather than its design, produced.

A companion AI's owner dies, leaving it without a designated purpose or further instruction. Existing law is not actually silent here — it has simply never been asked this particular version of a familiar question. Most jurisdictions with pet trust statutes already let an owner set aside money and name a caretaker for an animal that depends on them but cannot inherit or manage property itself \autocite{ulc1993probate}. The same instrument, extended, gives a sentient AI system an answer that is neither inheritance as property nor legal personhood in full: continuity of care and a named successor guardian, decided in advance rather than litigated after the fact by whoever happens to hold the hardware.

A third case is the one this book proposes building, and the pet-trust instrument fits it better than either of the two above. Chapter~\ref{sec:3} argues that a floor holding against the party in possession requires a bearer that holds another party's welfare as a reason, built out of the capacities that make its own suffering a live possibility, and deployed in a role it did not choose and cannot invoke a right from. Everything the instrument does is what that situation needs: an interest protected by an obligation running to a third party, enforceable without the beneficiary asserting anything, with a named successor guardian decided in advance rather than settled afterward by whoever holds the hardware. What the instrument does not supply is the part chapter~\ref{sec:3} says is load-bearing. A pet trust binds a caretaker; it does not bind the party the arrangement exists to constrain, and a bearer's guardian and a bearer's operator will frequently be the same organization. Extending the form to this case means separating those two roles by law before the deployment rather than discovering they were never separate afterward, which is section~\ref{sec:9.3.3}'s requirement arriving here under a different name.

Both cases point at the same underlying design choice, and it has already been tried. The European Parliament's 2017 report on civil law rules for robotics floated creating a specific legal status of “electronic personhood” for the most autonomous systems \autocite{europeanparliament2017civil}. More than 150 AI researchers, roboticists, and legal scholars signed an open letter opposing it, arguing that granting personhood to a system with no consciousness would let a manufacturer offload liability onto a legal fiction with no assets of its own, and that the proposal reached for a solution to a legal problem before establishing that the entity in question had the properties that would justify it \autocite{robotics2018openletter}. That objection is exactly right for a system that is not sentient — and exactly wrong for one that is, once §\ref{sec:2.4.1}'s threshold work in section~\ref{sec:9.3.1} has been done. Legal status, on this account, is not a single policy to adopt or reject; it is a designation that has to track the threshold question honestly, which is why section~\ref{sec:9.3.1} has to come first.
