\subsection{Interpretability, Self-Reports, and Resistance to Tampering}
\label{sec:9.2.2}
“AI self-awareness” names at least two different questions, and collapsing them into one is where the term goes wrong. Whether a system has anything like subjective experience is not a question current methods can answer for a human, let alone a machine, and nothing here resolves it. Whether a system's own report about what is happening inside it — its stated reasoning, its stated confidence, its stated intentions — tracks what its internal computations are actually doing is a narrower question, and an empirically tractable one, that mechanistic-interpretability research has started to test directly.

Anthropic's interpretability team tested that narrower question with a technique called concept injection: altering a model's internal activations mid-task to represent a specific concept, then asking the model whether it notices anything unusual and what it is \autocite{lindsey2025introspective}. The models tested could sometimes detect the injected concept and name it correctly without it ever appearing in the visible conversation — a real, if partial and unreliable, correspondence between what a system reports about its own state and what its activations are actually doing. The same work found that correspondence collapses often enough, and shifts enough with how the question is phrased, that no tested model's self-report can be trusted as an accurate readout of its internal state. That gap, not metacognition as evidence of inner life, is what “AI self-awareness” can mean as something a system is tested against rather than assumed to have.

“Emotional regulation” — cognitive reappraisal, attentional control, the vocabulary of human clinical psychology — assumes a system already has stable, emotion-like internal states to regulate, which section~\ref{sec:9.2.1} gives no reason to grant even for reading emotion off a human face, let alone locating one inside a model. The testable question next door is not regulation but robustness: does a system's trained-in behavior survive deliberate pressure to change it?

\begin{itemize}
  \item Prompt-level attacks try to talk a deployed model out of its training within a single conversation. Anthropic's Constitutional Classifiers, trained on synthetic data generated from written safety rules rather than human-labeled examples, held through more than 3,000 hours of professional red-teaming without a universal jailbreak found, at a cost: a 0.38-percentage-point rise in unnecessary refusals and a 23.7 percent increase in computation per query \autocite{sharma2025constitutional}.
  \item Weight-level attacks retrain the model itself. Researchers stripped GPT-3.5 Turbo's safety training using ten adversarially chosen fine-tuning examples, for under twenty cents through OpenAI's own API \autocite{qi2024finetuning}. Tamper-Resistant Safeguards, a training method from researchers spanning the Center for AI Safety, UC Berkeley, and the University of Illinois, is built to survive hundreds of steps of that kind of adversarial fine-tuning rather than a handful \autocite{tamirisa2024tamper}.
\end{itemize}
Both are adversarial research areas in the literal sense: each published defense is followed by an attack built against it, and neither problem is closed. That is a narrower and more honest description of “resistance to manipulation” than a system trained to sense hostile intent the way a person senses a threat. Section~\ref{sec:9.1} takes up what an antifascist AI system's commitments should be; this is the separate question of whether any commitment, once trained in, survives someone trying to remove it.
