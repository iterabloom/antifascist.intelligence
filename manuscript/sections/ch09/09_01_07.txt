9.1.7. What an Antifascist Detector Would Actually Have to Detect
Section 2.1.4 gives fascism a structural definition and calls the four features detection targets. Chapters 3, 4 and 5 then assume, in several places, that a system can be built to recognize authoritarian tactics and flag democratic backsliding early enough to matter. That assumption is doing more work in this book than the evidence behind it supports, and this section is where I say so.

<<h>>Why the general framing yields no targets<</h>>
Most of the difficulty is not technical. A specification that says a system should oppose concentrated, unaccountable power cannot be turned into a classifier because it names a property of an entire political arrangement. Nothing observable in a document, a transaction, or a feed corresponds to it. The structural signature in section 2.1.4 is a real improvement, since each of its four features points at an artifact somebody could go and look at. But look closely at what those artifacts are. Whether a dissent mechanism has ever changed an outcome requires knowing the counterfactual. Whether a metric admits an unmeasured remainder requires knowing what the remainder contains. Whether the exception-filer was punished requires access to what happened to a particular person inside an institution that has every reason not to disclose it. These are not features you extract; they are findings you reach, usually after somebody has spent months inside the organization.

<<h>>What would actually have to be measured<</h>>
A detector worth building would need at least three things this book cannot currently tell an engineer how to obtain.

It would need longitudinal access rather than a snapshot. Every one of the four features is defined by a change over time — dissent metabolized, a metric substituted, an exception recoded, a model drifting from what it tracks. A single observation of an institution cannot distinguish any of them from an institution that has always been mediocre. Section 7.4's account of the slope, not the level, is the right instrument here, and its data requirements are severe: the same measurement, taken the same way, over years, by someone the institution cannot fire.

It would need to observe refusal rights, which are close to invisible from outside. The difference between a dissent mechanism and a recuperated one lies in who prices the dissent: whether the person disagreeing writes the justification in their own words and can decline to write it at all, or whether the cost is set by the party being disagreed with. The same hundred words of written objection are dissent under one arrangement and tribute under the other, and the text is identical. There is no feature of the document that separates them.

It would need a base rate, and none exists. Nobody has assembled a labelled corpus of institutions annotated for the four features by people who agree on what they mean, and it is not obvious the annotation task is even well posed. Section 9.1.6's argument for learning from disagreement applies here with unusual force: on this question, disagreement between annotators is likely to be the most informative signal available, and treating it as noise would discard exactly what a researcher needs.

<<h>>The false-positive problem, which is the hard one<</h>>
Suppose all of that were solved. The remaining problem is discrimination, and it is the reason I do not think this capability is close.

Ordinary politics contains a great deal of behavior that resembles the signature. Governments run misleading communications campaigns. Parties demonize opponents. Bureaucracies substitute metrics for judgment continuously, and mostly out of laziness. Coalitions mobilize mass support, which is what coalitions are for. A detector calibrated to catch fascism early — while intervention is still cheap, which is the entire argument for building it — is calibrated to fire on precursors, and the precursors are common. A detector calibrated to avoid that fires only once the case is unmistakable, at which point it is telling people what they already know and the early-warning rationale has evaporated.

Both failure directions are dangerous, and they are not symmetric. A tool that over-flags supplies whoever holds it with a technical-sounding case that opposition is proto-fascist, which is a weapon handed to the exact actor the tool was built to constrain — the dual-use problem of section 4.4.6 in its sharpest form, because here the output is an accusation. A tool that under-flags provides false assurance that the instruments are working, which is worse than no tool, since the absence of a warning gets read as evidence of health.

<<h>>What I am claiming and what I am not<</h>>
I am not claiming the project is impossible. The molecular scale looks more tractable than the molar one: whether a particular exception mechanism inside a particular organization is being recuperated is a bounded question, answerable from records that organization already keeps, and a system that helped a regulator or an ombudsman ask it consistently across many institutions would be genuinely useful without ever pronouncing on a regime.

What I am claiming is that this book asserts a capability it has not specified, and that the honest place for it is here, in the chapter built for what is unfinished. A researcher who wanted to work on it would start with the narrow molecular case, with longitudinal data, with the slope rather than the level, and with an explicit account of what the tool would be permitted to output — a finding for a body with standing to act on it, never a public accusation. The gap between that program and the early-warning system section 4.1.4 gestures at is the honest measure of how far this is from done.
