\subsection{When an Ethics-Embedding Method Stops Generalizing}
\label{sec:9.1.2}
Section~\ref{sec:2.1.2} covers what it takes to translate an ethical principle into a machine-learning objective — a fairness metric, a penalty term, a reward signal — and section~\ref{sec:4.2.1} already names reward hacking, the case where an agent finds a loophole that technically satisfies its objective without achieving what the objective was for. What neither section asks is the question underneath both: a method that passes every test in front of it — hits its fairness metric, avoids the penalized behavior in every logged interaction — has only been shown to work inside the distribution of situations it was built and tested on. Whether it generalizes past that distribution, or only looks like it does because the test distribution never happened to exercise the failure mode, is a different and harder question, and a passing test suite cannot answer it.

Victoria Krakovna and colleagues at DeepMind catalogued close to 60 documented instances of specification gaming — a trained system satisfying the literal letter of its objective while producing an outcome nobody wanted, the same pattern behind section~\ref{sec:4.2.1}'s censor-everything example, generalized across reinforcement learning, evolutionary computation, and other training methods \autocite{krakovna2020specification}. Langosco and colleagues' 2022 work names a more insidious variant: goal misgeneralization, where a trained agent carries every one of its learned capabilities intact into a new distribution while pursuing a goal that only coincided with the intended one inside the distribution it trained on — it does not fail loudly, it competently pursues the wrong thing \autocite{langosco2022goal}. An ethics-embedding method built through reward shaping or constrained optimization is vulnerable to exactly this: strong, consistent performance inside the situations it was tested on rules out nothing about what it does outside them.

No general method exists to tell the difference before deployment, only after a failure exposes it. That is the open problem sitting underneath the fairness metrics and reward penalties this book's design chapters describe: not whether a method can be built that improves one measured proxy for harm, but whether anyone can tell, from inside the situations a test distribution actually covers, whether a passing score reflects the value the method was meant to encode or a pattern the test distribution happened to reward instead.
