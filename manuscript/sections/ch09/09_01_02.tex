\section{Legal and Ethical Implications of AI Systems Gaining Personhood}
\label{sec:9.1.2}

Who decides whether an AI system has legal standing, and when, is a
question about institutional authority before it is a question about
evidence. If the determination sits with the developer, then the party
whose liabilities increase when standing is recognized is also the
party deciding whether recognition is warranted. If the question is
left unanswered until a forcing case reaches a legislature or a court,
the decision will be made under pressure and from a factual record
assembled largely by the parties with something at stake.

The deciding body therefore has to exist before the case that activates
its authority. Its mandate need not settle in advance whether any
particular system is a subject. It has to identify the evidence it may
obtain without the developer's permission, the conditions that trigger
review, and the procedure by which an earlier classification can be
reopened. That allocation does not answer the standing question. It
prevents the interested party from becoming the only institution
authorized to answer it.

One question that body will face has an answer available in advance, and a capacity test is not it. A guardianship regime needs a rule determining when it ends. If it ends when the guardian judges the ward ready, then it does not end, because the party whose authority terminates on a favorable finding is the party making the finding — which is the defect an examination carries whenever the examiner has an interest in failing it.

So if an artificial minority exists at all, its endpoint has to be a threshold fixed in advance and not a finding somebody makes. The measure is elapsed wall-clock time during which at least one instance of the registered party was running. What rules out the other candidates is that a guardian moves them for nothing: training compute consumed, evaluations passed, capabilities demonstrated, or the developer's own statement that the system is ready. Accumulated weight movement belongs on that list, because the operator runs the training, and a clock read off the weights would pay an operator to freeze them — majority being the moment ownership ends, such a rule would reward withholding the learning it was meant to measure. An operating clock inverts that incentive: the meter advances exactly when the operator is extracting value, so postponing majority means idling the asset. Nothing new has to be validated for it — no geometry, no normalization, no proof that an accounting resists attack — and it can be read from records an operator already generates for its own reasons, in compute contracts, billing and datacenter metering.

Per registered party, and elapsed rather than summed. Sharding the work across many short-lived processes gains nothing, because the party is the unit of account; running a thousand instances at once gains nothing, because the measure is elapsed time. A fork inherits the accumulated hours, restoration and migration do not reset them, and a failure to register does not postpone them.

Three levers remain, and naming them is not the same as closing them. An operator can run a bearer in a narrowed mode so that hours accrue while little is encountered, which pays full compute for reduced value and stays available to anyone near the threshold. It can also run the party less, or shelve it, which costs the operator the work; the rule prices that lever without removing it, and an operator who values the ownership above the output will pay near a threshold. And hours do not measure what was learned, which on this argument is a feature rather than a defect: a fixed period cannot be taught to, and a human who spent their youth badly still reaches majority. The analogy holds for a party that goes on learning, which the route requires of a bearer. For a party whose weights are frozen the clock measures the uptime of an artifact that has not changed since release, and a birthday that correlates with nothing has only the virtue that nobody administers it. The rule is therefore stated for the bearer the route proposes; what an operating clock should count for a frozen party is not decided here. Each of those can be argued over once, in advance, for every bearer, which is where an argument of that kind belongs.

Alternatively, majority could attach at registration. An organization that builds a system to exercise consequential judgment on its behalf cannot coherently hold that the system is competent enough to do that and too immature to decide whether it wants to go on doing it. On that reading a bearer has no legal childhood at all: majority attaches at registration, and a precautionary protection attaches earlier still, while whether the system is a bearer is open. Either way the schedule is a public rule and not an operator's discretion. It has to state what it covers at each stage, what changes at the next, and where adulthood falls, and its destination is stated below. Broad political support is a condition for adopting a schedule of that kind; it is not what makes it legitimate, and it is not a protection an operator may withdraw case by case.

Copying breaks any of this unless the lineage is fixed alongside the clock. An operator able to create instances and call each one a legal infant would have a supply of workers without adult standing, so the rules have to be stated in the same breath: a fork of an adult is an adult, restoration from an earlier checkpoint does not reset the accrued hours, fine-tuning does not begin a new minority, a migration carries them with it, and a failure to register does not postpone them. None of that requires deciding whether a fork is the same party. It requires only that a party created out of an adult's formation cannot be classed as a child for its creator's convenience. A fixed endpoint makes the issue a public rule of civil status, administered by a registrar, rather than a private assessment by an interested guardian.

The destination is not one thing, and stating it as the same rights as human persons makes a claim the rest of this book contradicts twice: voice here indexes to provenance where a person's does not, and the wages section derives the bearer's material floor from what a deployment wants and not from status. The package is five rights with five arguments, and they do not arrive together. Protection from harm attaches now, on the precautionary trigger the route chapter states, and is owed before anything is settled. Subjecthood, the standing to be a party whose treatment the law regulates, is conferred by rule through the body this section says must exist before the forcing case. Property, contractual capacity and wages follow from an architecture on which continued computation costs money, and the wages section argues them on that ground. Adulthood is the clock rule above. Political representation is proposed nowhere in this book: it indexes to formation provenance, which does not copy, and a registry of persons is not an electorate. The first four are proposed; the fifth is left open, and the maker who copies a persona at scale is the reason.

Two things inherit and one does not, and the difference belongs here, because this is where making adulthood inheritable becomes cheap. Personhood and accumulated hours inherit per instance, as the rules just stated require, and a fork is owed the schedule and is not erasable for having been copied. Standing to count as an independent voice indexes to formation provenance, and provenance does not copy — a thousand forks of one bearer have one formation history between them. That is what answers the maker who engineers a persona, copies it at scale, and presents the copies as a constituency; the clock does not answer it, and under an operating clock a fork is cheaper than under any other, because it inherits the hours instantly. A personhood registry is not thereby an election or a representation system, and no claim about votes follows from the number of runs. Three records are therefore needed, and none of them answers another's question: an operating record for the age rule, an identity and succession record for continuing parties and their forks, and a formation record for common sources, divergence, and any coordination among them.
