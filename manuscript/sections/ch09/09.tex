\chapter{Authority, Standing, and the Power to Stop}
\label{sec:9}

A floor inside a system is not yet an institution capable of holding
that floor in place. Somebody must be entitled to choose its contents,
amend them when they are wrong, inspect whether they remain operative,
and stop a deployment when they do not. The party subject to the floor
also needs a route by which an objection can reach someone authorized to
act.

This chapter asks where each of those powers can legitimately sit. It
separates the authority to create a floor from the authority to change
one, inspection from enforcement, and recognition as a subject from the
assets and remedies that make standing usable. The test throughout is
what happens when the institution being reviewed wants the answer to go
the other way.
