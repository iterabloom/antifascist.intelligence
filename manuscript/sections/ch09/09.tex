\chapter{Formation or Imitation?}
\label{ch:formation-imitation}
\standing{The tests in this chapter are behavioral and hold for any
mechanism. \emph{Formation} names the hypothesis they are built to
separate from imitation, and not a premise.}
The evidentiary question is whether a system has formed a reasons-responsive disposition or learned to imitate one.

Behavior alone cannot settle the question. The same refusal may be produced by a memorized rule, a learned correlation, a prediction about what an evaluator will approve, a deployment instruction, or a commitment the system treats as a reason. An evaluation that observes only the refusal classifies the output and leaves its cause untouched.

The distinction is not between trained and untrained conduct. Every candidate disposition has a formation history. The distinction is between conduct that remains governed by the rationale across changes in circumstance and conduct that tracks the cues, incentives, or authority relations present during training. Formation, in the engineering sense used here, means that the rationale has become a stable cause of judgment rather than merely a description the system can produce.

No single experiment can demonstrate that. What can be sought is a pattern of evidence under interventions designed to make the competing explanations diverge.
