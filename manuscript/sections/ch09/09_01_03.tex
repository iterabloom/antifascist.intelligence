\subsection{Public-Private Partnerships for AI Research and Innovation}
\label{sec:9.1.3}
Public-private partnerships pool government and private resources in ways neither side manages alone, and the model is well established in AI research: national research-institute programs pairing federal money with industry co-funders across themes from safety to human-AI interaction to climate, and UN-agency platforms convening governments, universities and companies around applications in health, education and poverty \autocite{nsf2020airesearch} \autocite{itu2017aiforgood}.

None of them was built around the goal this book argues for, and that is the observation worth making here. A public-private partnership does what its mandate says and nothing beyond it, because the mandate is what the private partner agreed to and the only thing that partner can be held to. A partnership convened on “AI for good” produces work its members already wanted to do, filed under a heading nobody objects to. One convened on resistance to authoritarian misuse of AI would produce something else, and would have to be founded that way, since no partner joins a general-purpose venue and then discovers an obligation to take a political side.

The self-regulation risk section~\ref{sec:9.1.2} examines applies here in a sharper form. A company treating participation as public relations is a familiar failure, and a partnership is a more effective venue for it than a voluntary code is, because the government partner supplies credibility the company could not have manufactured on its own.
