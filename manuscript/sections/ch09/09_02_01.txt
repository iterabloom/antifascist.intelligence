9.2.1. Is Emotion Legible From a Face at All?

Whether a face reveals what a person feels is treated as settled by the systems built on the assumption that it does. It is not settled. Facial-expression-recognition AI is built almost entirely on Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen's claim that a small set of emotions — anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise — is universally expressed and universally readable, based on the Fore study section 2.3.1 introduces: roughly 85 percent cross-cultural agreement on which face matched which emotion story, among people with almost no prior exposure to Western media [[cite:C0561]]. Section 2.3.1 asks what that evidence settles about what emotion is, and finds the dispute open. The question here is narrower and has moved further, because a deployed product does not need emotion to be a natural kind. It needs a face to be readable.

A meta-analysis spanning both face and voice found real above-chance recognition across cultures, but also a consistent "in-group advantage": people read emotion more accurately from members of their own national, ethnic, or linguistic group than from anyone else's, an asymmetry that shrinks, but does not vanish, the more contact the groups have with each other [[cite:C0562]]. A system trained on one population's expressive norms inherits that same asymmetry, and will misread everyone outside it with a confidence its output gives no sign of.

In 2019 the underlying claim collapsed further. The Association for Psychological Science commissioned five researchers from competing theoretical camps to review the evidence; the assignment was expected to take a few months and took two years, working through more than a thousand studies before the panel reached a unanimous conclusion: a person's emotional state cannot be reliably inferred from facial movement alone, in the strong sense the six-emotion model requires [[cite:C0563]]. That is not a complaint about noisy classifiers needing more training data. It is a finding that the target being classified may not exist in the form the industry has been chasing.

This has already reshaped a deployed product, not just an academic debate. HireVue, whose video-interview software screened candidates for employers including Unilever and Hilton, dropped facial-expression scoring from its assessments in January 2021, after an independent audit and years of criticism that the technique penalized candidates with autism, facial differences, or other atypical affect [[cite:C0564]]. The European Union's AI Act goes further: it prohibits inferring emotion from biometric signal in workplaces and schools outright, and its own recital gives the reason — limited reliability, lack of specificity, and expression that "varies considerably across cultures and situations, and even within a single individual." One of the few bans in the Act rests on the science failing, not on how the technology might be misused [[cite:C0565]].

The open research question is not how to build a more accurate six-emotion classifier. It is whether any face-to-feeling mapping survives once individual and cross-cultural variation are taken seriously, under what constraints, and against what error rate — a harder and more honest question than the one facial-expression-recognition products are sold as having already answered.
