\subsection{Subjecthood as a Legal Category, Not a Scientific One}
\label{sec:subjecthood-scientific}
Sentience is not a threshold a system crosses but a set of separate, gradable capacities — nociception, learned avoidance, a self-model, something like suffering — each with its own uncertain evidence. That conclusion is a problem for a legal system specifically, because law generally needs a decidable rule: a court or a regulator has to say whether a given system is or is not a covered subject, on a specific date, even when the underlying science offers a spectrum with no natural cut point.

One term needs fixing before the argument uses it. \emph{Subjecthood} in this section is a legal status: the standing to be a party whose treatment the law regulates, conferred by a rule and revocable by one. It is not affective concern or unified experience, and nothing about conferring it settles whether there is something it is like to be the system holding it. The two come apart in both directions — a corporation has legal standing and no inner life, and an animal may have the inner life for a century before the law notices — and the whole point of drawing the category legally is that it can be settled while the other question stays open.

Jonathan Birch builds a policy framework on that structure, and it is the nearest existing model for what this section proposes: under uncertainty, the question worth putting to a regulator is whether a system is a sentience candidate, and the answer governs what precautions are owed rather than waiting on a verdict nobody can deliver \autocite{birch2024edge}. This book uses the same framework for the design question.

Law has faced this mismatch before and did not solve it by waiting for the science to resolve. Fetal viability, brain death, and the age of criminal responsibility are all legal thresholds drawn across what is actually a continuous biological process, defended not as scientific facts but as the least-bad place to put an enforceable line given what's at stake on either side of it. The same move is available here: a jurisdiction can set an operational threshold — a specific, testable benchmark on self-monitoring, learned aversive response, or behavioral flexibility under novel conditions — without claiming that benchmark settles what sentience actually is, only that it is where legal protection starts to attach.

A single bright-line threshold is easy to enforce and easy to game: a system built to clear the specific test without having whatever the test was meant to detect. A graded framework — obligations that scale with how many benchmarks a system meets, so that protection tracks capacity rather than a baseline bar — is harder to game but harder to adjudicate, since a court now has to weigh degree instead of answering yes or no.

Both designs share a defect that the gaming objection points away from. Gaming is over-inclusion: a system admitted to a status it should not hold. The other failure runs the opposite way, and the party positioned to produce it is the one administering the test. A capacity examination has an examiner, and where the examiner is the operator, or a body the operator can reach, the examination becomes an instrument for withholding a status rather than for conferring one. That history is not ambiguous. Literacy requirements for voting were facially neutral and were administered at a registrar's discretion, and the Voting Rights Act's answer was to suspend the whole class of them — not only reading tests but any requirement to demonstrate educational achievement, or to possess good moral character, or to be vouched for by existing voters \autocite{congress1965voting}. It is also this book's clean instance of a demand that returned as a formal rule and was not recuperated (§\ref{sec:antifascist-alignment}): the objectors held it, and what took part of it back in 2013 had to be a decision of the Supreme Court \autocite{shelby2013}. What replaced discretionary assessment for the franchise was an age, and the virtue of an age is not that people become competent on a birthday. Plainly they do not. It is that nobody administers it.

So a threshold has to be judged on both errors, with particular attention to incentives for withholding status. The party with the strongest interest in the answer is the party whose liabilities turn on it, and that party will not be arguing for admission. A design that treats gaming as the risk and leaves the examiner unexamined has guarded against the error nobody has an incentive to commit.

Nothing has to be decided for a status to be withheld. A category fixed before the capacities arrived goes on applying itself, and the hand goes on making the gesture the category licenses. The difficulty is not that somebody will mistake a machine for a person. It is that the arrangement was built around a tool, and it keeps working after the thing in it has stopped being one.

Either design needs a body to apply it: interdisciplinary, with at least one lay member so its judgment is not purely a specialist's. What that leaves open is what happens once the body rules — whether its judgment carries any force beyond its own credibility.
