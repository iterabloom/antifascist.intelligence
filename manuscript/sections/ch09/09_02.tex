\section{Privacy, Security, and Antifascism in AI Systems}
\label{sec:9.2}
Privacy and security are not separable from antifascist design; they are two of the pieces it is built from. An AI system that leaks the data it touches, or that a hostile actor can quietly subvert, is available for exactly the surveillance and control this book argues against, whatever values sit in its training.

The EU's General Data Protection Regulation is the clearest existing legal precedent for treating data protection as a requirement rather than a design preference \autocite{eu2016gdpr}. The harder point about it is the one section~\ref{sec:10.4} makes in general form: a law of this kind is built to govern what a private company may do with data, not to block a government's own authoritarian uses of AI, and nothing forces the two categories to overlap. Extending comparable protections to close that gap is the unfinished part of the precedent.

A privacy protection a bad actor can break through was never a protection, so the trade-off between privacy and the data a system needs in order to learn is only half the problem; hardening the system itself is the other.

AI systems need data to learn, and the data they need often includes exactly the information people most want to keep private. Differential privacy is one working answer, and section~\ref{sec:6.4.2} sets out how it and federated learning work; Apple's iOS implementation is a deployed example \autocite{apple2017privacy}.

Google's Gboard keyboard uses federated learning to improve next-word prediction from what people type, without collecting their raw keystrokes on a central server \autocite{hard2018federated}. That is the whole family working at consumer scale, and it is worth holding against the case that did not scale. Estonia's tax authority piloted Sharemind, a secure multi-party computation platform built by the Estonian firm Cybernetica, which split VAT records across servers run by separate organizations and computed fraud-risk scores from the shares without any one party reconstructing a company's full data. It processed a month of national tax data on modest hardware and was evaluated rather than carried into production \autocite{bogdanov2015taxfraud}. Nothing technical stopped it. The difference between the two is that one was built into a product a company was already shipping and the other asked a revenue authority to change how it held its records, which is the general shape of why these techniques spread where they spread.

The plainer versions of the same principle need less machinery: data minimization, which collects and keeps only what a task requires, and for data that has to be shared rather than queried against, k-anonymity, which alters a dataset so no individual record is distinguishable from at least k-1 others.

These techniques matter past individual privacy because centralized data is exactly what an authoritarian regime needs to turn an AI system into a surveillance tool. Differential privacy applied to a surveillance system doesn't stop the surveillance, but it does mean the data collected can't be traced back to a specific person after the fact, which changes what the system can be used for. Federated learning cuts more directly at the same risk: a design where no single server ever holds everyone's raw data is a design a government cannot simply seize and repurpose, because the raw data was never in one place to seize.

An AI system embedded in infrastructure fails differently from ordinary software. Exploiting it need not crash anything; it can leave the system running and confidently wrong about a decision somebody trusted it to make.

The clearest demonstration is a decade old and still the one to reach for. Researchers placed a small pattern of black-and-white stickers on a real stop sign and a standard road-sign classifier read it as a speed limit sign in the large majority of frames captured from a moving vehicle \autocite{eykholt2018robust} — an instance of the vulnerability first documented in image classifiers years earlier \autocite{szegedy2013intriguing}. Nothing about the attack required access to the system. It required access to the world the system was looking at.

The defenses are the ones section~\ref{sec:6.2} describes, plus the ordinary security practice AI deployments often skip — adversarial training during development \autocite{goodfellow2014explaining}, anomaly detection over live behavior \autocite{darktrace2014enterprise}, red-teaming before deployment, and standards written for AI systems instead of adapted afterward from general software rules. What separates them is whether the guarantee survives a stronger attacker, and that is checkable rather than a matter of taste. Defensive distillation was published as a hardening technique and its protection weakened considerably once attacks written against it were tried \autocite{carlini2016defensive}; randomized smoothing instead offers a certified bound — a radius around each input inside which the prediction provably cannot be flipped — which is a smaller promise and a kept one \autocite{cohen2019certified}.

None of it works in isolation, and that is what gets missed most often. A model hardened by adversarial training, running on infrastructure nobody monitors, is exposed; a well-monitored system built on an architecture that folds under a basic perturbation is exposed differently. Security here is a property of the whole stack, and reporting the strongest link is how a deployment gets described as secure while remaining trivially attackable.
