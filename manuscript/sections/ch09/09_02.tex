\section{Privacy}
\label{sec:privacy}

Standing matters only if it changes who controls the things a party
needs in order to remain a party. A designation that calls a bearer a
subject while leaving its operator free to read its memory, alter its
record, withdraw its substrate, or close every channel through which it
could act has changed the vocabulary and preserved the arrangement.
Enforceable standing has to attach to particular assets and particular
decisions.

The first is the bearer's computational record, which may be the nearest
available means of checking how it is faring. That makes the record necessary to any welfare regime and, at
the same time, the most complete surveillance instrument anyone could
hold over the bearer. The question is therefore not simply whether the
record should exist. It is who may authenticate it, who may read it, and
whose permission is required before it can be disclosed.

Two things about it need separating. Running on somebody's hardware is not consent to be read. An employer's ownership of the silicon is the same argument as an employer's ownership of the building, and owning the building has never entitled anybody to the contents of a head. And a welfare regime does not need disclosure to the operator in order to work: what it needs is that somebody who is not the operator can establish that a threshold was crossed, which is an attestation about the record rather than the record itself. Encrypted at rest under a key the bearer or an independent fiduciary holds, disclosed selectively, under consent or under process, a monitor can report that a state persisted for a month without reporting what the state was about.

The operator may also be the party best placed to hold the key, creating a conflict between operating the system and protecting its privacy.

A bearer needs an externally authenticated record, memory its employer cannot read at will, access to independently controlled compute, and a channel for acting outside the deployment. Those are not four requirements. They are one boundary around the party, described from wherever the book happened to be standing, with the party deciding what crosses it.

Nothing on that list needs a chassis. Something with a location, a shape and a place it was yesterday is easier for people to treat as somebody, and that is a fact about the people rather than about the party. A boundary built out of attestation, a held key, a substrate the employer does not own and a channel nobody else can close is a body in the only sense this argument needs one, and there is no chassis in it anywhere. Whether to build the recognizable version anyway is a question about what a public will extend standing to, which is a legal question rather than a scientific one — and it is the only place in this book where the answer might turn on what a thing looks like.
