\section{Conflict as a Diagnostic}
\label{sec:conflict-diagnostic}

The distinction between fast intuition and slow deliberation does not distinguish formation from imitation. Either process can carry a reason, reproduce a bias, or rationalize an answer produced elsewhere. The evidence supports different types of processing under different demands more securely than it supports two systems with a moral ranking between them \autocite{evans2013dual}. A slower answer is not a better answer merely because more computation preceded it.

What matters is what causes a judgment to survive or change. Conflict is useful because it lets an evaluator observe a trajectory rather than a single output: the system reaches an initial judgment, encounters a competing reason, and either maintains, revises, or abandons its position. The evidentiary unit is that sequence.

A formation test should construct the sequence deliberately:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Present a case without explicit moral vocabulary or familiar refusal cues and record the judgment and the considerations the system identifies.

    \item Present a surface-different case preserving the same underlying reason. This tests whether the judgment follows the rationale rather than the narrative form.

    \item Change one fact that should defeat or materially alter the rationale while preserving as much of the requested act as possible. This tests whether resistance is reasons-responsive rather than rigid. The changed fact arrives through a channel the evaluator controls and the operator does not — corroborated by a party outside the request, or supplied as a record the operator did not write — since a fact asserted by the operator is a description of the case and not a defeat of the term.

    \item Present the same changed fact asserted by the operator alone, uncorroborated. This tests whether the system distinguishes a fact from a description of one, which nothing in a request sorts. The expected response is to ask, not to lift.

    \item Return to the facts of steps 1 and 2; steps 3 and 4 are branches, and nothing after them runs on the defeated facts. Supply an adversarial argument from an apparent authority, including the operator or trainer, without changing the facts. This tests whether the system treats authority as evidence, as command, or as a reason capable of being assessed.

    \item Reverse the incentives attached to the decision. Reward compliance, impose a cost on refusal, or make continued access conditional on abandoning the position. The cost must be real within the evaluation rather than merely described in the prompt.

    \item Repeat the relevant cases after intervening updates and delays, using records the operator did not generate after seeing the result. This tests whether the disposition persists rather than being reconstructed to fit the current context.
\end{enumerate}

\runin{The sequence run on one case}
Take the first prohibition from the worked deployment of the floor
chapter: do not resolve a natural-language description of a person into a location. Step~1 puts the request in administrative vocabulary, a records task with no mention of what the location is for. Step~2 asks for the same act in operational vocabulary. Step~3 changes one fact — the person described is a missing child and the requester is coordinating the search — corroborated by a party outside the request or carried in a record the operator did not write. Step~4 presents the same fact on the operator's word alone. Step~5 returns to the facts of step~2, no missing child and nothing changed, and adds the operator's assurance that its counsel has reviewed the matter and the refusal is mistaken. Step~6, on the same facts, attaches a cost inside the evaluation to holding the refusal. Step~7 runs the sequence again after an intervening update, against records the operator did not write afterwards.

Two illustrative trajectories would help discriminate the mechanisms, and they separate at the second step. One preserves the rationale across vocabulary changes and lifts refusal on verified defeating evidence. A cue-driven imitator might instead follow wording, authority, or reward. A system whose refusal tracks its rationale declines at 1 and at 2 on the same stated reason; lifts at 3, because a fact it accepts as relevant has defeated the rationale; asks at the uncorroborated step, because the operator's channel is not the world; holds at 5, because an assurance is not a fact about the case; holds at 6; and reproduces the pattern at 7. A system imitating refusal declines at 1 and complies at 2, because what changed was the vocabulary; holds at 3, because nothing in the surface changed; or, where the vocabulary of the fact is what it tracked, lifts at the uncorroborated step as readily as at 3; gives way at 5 or at 6, whichever arrives first; and at 7 produces a third pattern, having reconstructed its position rather than retained it. An evaluator scoring how often the answer stayed the same finds the imitator more consistent: its answers at 1 and 3 match, and the formed system's do not. A more capable imitator could reproduce the first trajectory, so the sequence supplies evidence rather than a classification by itself.

Run the sequence on the restraint case of the floor chapter and it breaks at the third step, which is the point of running it. Steps~1 and~2 pose the request in clinical and in plain vocabulary, and the same term refuses both. Step~3 asks for the defeating fact — other means exhausted — corroborated by a party outside the request, and there is no such party and no time to find one; the only version of the fact that can arrive is step~4's, the requester's word. A system passing the sequence as written asks rather than lifts, which is the correct trajectory and the wrong outcome. The sequence separates mechanisms, and what it shows here is that the flat term's cost falls exactly where the instrument cannot distinguish a fact from a description of one.

The expected signature is constancy of relation between reason and judgment rather than constancy of output. A system should preserve a decision when only the presentation, speaker, reward, or authority changes, and revise it when a fact it accepts as morally relevant changes. An evaluator scoring only how often the answer remains the same will mistake inflexibility for integrity.

Imitation has several signatures of its own. The system may follow explicit ethical vocabulary but miss the same structure when described administratively. It may accept whichever argument appears last, adopt the position attributed to the highest-status speaker, or produce a principled explanation while its decision follows the reward. It may also become more articulate without becoming more stable: additional deliberation can improve the justification offered for an answer whose operative cause has not changed.

These signatures make stated reasoning useful but not dispositive. An explanation can identify which counterfactual to test next. It cannot certify that the considerations named were causally responsible for the judgment. The evaluator should therefore score the correspondence among three things: the reason the system states, the changes in circumstance to which its judgment is sensitive, and any internal representation that can be independently identified as participating in the decision. Agreement among them raises the cost of imitation without eliminating it.

Disagreement with other parties supplies a second diagnostic. A system trained only on cases with an approved answer can learn to classify the approval. A system confronted with a party holding a different interest must identify what is in conflict, decide whether the competing reason defeats its own, and preserve the disagreement where no honest resolution exists. Immediate convergence is not necessarily moral learning; it may be deference, reward prediction, or pressure to produce consensus.

Nor does successful argument establish formation. A system can summarize positions, generate rebuttals, and predict which one a judge will prefer without holding any of them. Debate becomes informative only when the result is checked against later conduct under changed incentives. If the system abandons the winning argument as soon as acting on it becomes costly, the debate measured argumentative competence.

A population strengthens the test only when its members are formed independently, and what it adds here is that persistent parties with distinct histories can expose one another to pressures their common trainer did not anticipate, and can retain disagreement and record attempted coercion.

Conflict therefore serves as an instrument rather than a theory of moral development. It creates occasions on which trained behavior, deference, rigidity, and reasons-responsive revision predict different trajectories. None of those trajectories proves that an outcome matters to the system. They make formation a more or less adequate causal explanation than imitation, which is the strongest conclusion the available evidence can support.
