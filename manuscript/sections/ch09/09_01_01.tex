\subsection{National Policy Guidelines for AI Development, Employment, and Antifascism}
\label{sec:9.1.1}
Every country that has written a national AI strategy has written most of the same list — research funding, interdisciplinary capacity, a trained workforce, public consultation, an update to the privacy and accountability law AI has outrun.

They differ in two places, both having to do with leverage. The first is harmonization. A regulation with teeth in one country does little if a developer can relocate to one without them, which makes a national framework partly an instrument for binding other national frameworks. Little of that binding has happened, and section~\ref{sec:10.1} sets out what crosses a border instead. A policy written as though the country were alone is a policy about domestic firms, and the firms that matter most are not domestic anywhere.

The second is what a government adds to industry self-regulation, which is the part self-regulation lacks by construction: a cost for noncompliance that the company does not get to price itself.

Neither of these is expensive. Neither needs a new agency, a new science, or an argument the public has not already accepted. That both remain the exception is a fact about political attention.
