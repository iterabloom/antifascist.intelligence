\subsection{Who May Create or Amend the Floor?}
\label{sec:9.1.1}
A floor binds people who did not choose it. The party it protects has not selected it, cannot contest it, and in most cases will never learn it operated; the party it constrains has not consented to it either, beyond whatever consent is carried by buying the product. Publishing the floor's contents in advance tells everybody what is coming. It does not tell anybody why the party writing it was entitled to.

The question has a literature, and it is not the AI ethics literature. Entrenching a substantive commitment against later majorities, and putting an unelected body in charge of holding it, is what a constitutional court does. The standing objection is Jeremy Waldron's: judicial review is democratically illegitimate, and there is no reason to suppose rights come out better protected by judges than by a legislature that takes them seriously \autocite{waldron2006core}. Put a lab in the court's chair and the objection gets worse rather than better. A court is public, reasons in writing, and can be amended around by constitutional process. A model's floor is none of those things, and the body holding it was assembled by whoever was paying.

\runin{The reply, and how far it reaches}
Waldron's case is conditional and he says so. It assumes a society with working democratic institutions and a citizenry that takes rights seriously. The occasion a floor is built for is the one where those conditions have failed — a government that won an election and is dismantling the machinery that would check it, which is the case section~\ref{sec:3.1} builds the whole argument around. So the objection holds where the floor is least needed and lapses where it is needed most.

The reply is not a license. It concedes that a floor operating in a functioning democracy is doing something Waldron's argument condemns, and the party deciding which of the two situations obtains is the party holding the model. Every authoritarian movement of the last century has described the institutions it was dismantling as the ones that had already failed. An operator's belief that the emergency is real is worth no more here than a system's report about its own internals is worth anywhere else in this book.

\runin{The tradition this belongs to, and where it went wrong}
The position that a democracy may entrench limits against movements that would use its procedures to end it is Karl Loewenstein's, argued in 1937 while the case for it was being made in front of him \autocite{loewenstein1937militant}. Militant democracy is the floor's ancestor, and its record is usually read as a warning. Read carelessly it is one. But the Federal Republic has run the doctrine at two settings for seventy-five years, and nearly everything that went wrong went wrong at one of them.

At the first setting a body assesses a threat. Article~21 of the Basic Law reserves the power to dissolve a party to the Federal Constitutional Court alone, which has used it twice: against a successor to the Nazi party in 1952 and against the Communist Party in 1956. The second attempt on the far-right NPD is the instructive one. In 2017 the court found the party's aims genuinely incompatible with the constitutional order and declined to ban it anyway, holding under a criterion it introduced for the case that a party must have some realistic prospect of achieving those aims before it can be dissolved \autocite{bverfg2017npd,molier2018germanys}. Every exercise of the power is a reasoned judgment delivered in public, and every one of them requires somebody to decide how dangerous a movement is.

At the second setting nothing is assessed. Section~86a of the criminal code prohibits a listed set of symbols — the swastika, the SS runes, the Hitler salute — and prohibits nothing else \autocite{germany1998stgb}. Ordinary police and ordinary courts apply it, and through the 2010s the recorded case figure sat between thirteen and fourteen thousand a year. German doctrine classes the offence as one of abstract endangerment, which means the prosecution need not show that the defendant supported the organization whose symbol he displayed, or that any harm followed, or that anything at all was under threat. The symbol is on the list or it is not.

The failures are on one side. The 1972 decree against radicals screened some three and a half million applicants for public employment, opened disciplinary proceedings against around two thousand serving officials, and ended in roughly 2,250 refusals of appointment and 256 dismissals \autocite{braunthal1990political}.
The decree named the extreme left and the extreme right, and in Bavaria between 1973 and 1980 it rejected 102 applicants from the left and two from the right \autocite{muehldorfer2014radikalenerlass}. Turkey's dissolutions of Kurdish parties ran through the same form. What those cases share is an official deciding whether a person or a movement threatened the order, which is the operation Loewenstein's critics have always said would be turned on whoever was inconvenient. Section~86a has been available for the same seventy years, is applied thousands of times more often, and has not become a general instrument of political self-defense. The danger tracks whether a prohibition is enumerated in advance or assessed at the point of application, rather than whether the tradition is militant.

\runin{What enumeration answers, and what it leaves}
That bears on the difficulty two paragraphs above. Enumeration does not remove the discretion an operator exercises in deciding that the emergency is real. It relocates it, out of continuous silent application and into a single act of drafting, done once, before any case has arrived, by a party that has to say what is on the list. An interested party still makes the judgment. What changes is that the judgment is made in one place, at one time, in a form somebody else can read and dispute.

This book prefers that trade elsewhere and for the same reason: a bearer's majority attaches on a fixed date rather than on a capacity test, because a rule of civil status is argued once in the open and administered by a registrar, while an examination is administered case by case by whoever has an interest in the result. The preference here is that one applied to the floor's contents.

Section~86a operates inside a state with courts, an appeal, and a public record of every application, and the floor's drafter has none of the three. Enumeration buys visibility at the moment of drafting and buys nothing at the moment of application, which is the objection this section closes on.

\runin{The tradeoff this leaves the book with}
The two settings do not only sort militant democracy's record. They sort this book, and they put its halves on opposite sides.

Section~\ref{sec:3.2} asks a refusal to reach cases the training did not anticipate, because the reason for refusing survives a change in the request's surface. Meeting that requirement means something in the system decides, for a case matching no listed form, whether the reason is present anyway — which is assessment, moved inside the machine. And assessment is the setting this section has just shown to be the capturable one, for a reason that is structural rather than German: a judgment needs somebody to make it, and whoever makes it can be reached.

So the floor's design requirement and its legitimacy requirement want opposite things. Enumerate it narrowly enough to be defensible and a rewritten request walks around it, which is what section~\ref{sec:3.6} finds. Let it generalize and a party nobody elected is deciding, silently and case by case, what the prohibition was for.

Germany has both because it runs two instruments rather than one. The statute is enumerated, and the courts read it against its purpose; the reading happens in public, on appeal, argued by parties who do not own the statute and answered by a body that did not write it. A generalizing prohibition is survivable there because the generalizing is done somewhere its author cannot reach. A deployment has one instrument and one party.

Nothing in the model resolves that and this book does not claim to. What the pairing shows is where a resolution would have to sit: the assessment has to be performed by somebody other than the operator, in a form that leaves a record somebody else can read, which is what section~\ref{sec:3.5} asks of custody and section~\ref{sec:5.4} asks of evaluation. Neither is a property of the artifact. The tension therefore does not get settled inside the system, and that is why chapter~\ref{sec:3}'s answer is a hybrid rather than a mechanism.

\runin{The one construction the objection does not fit}
Of chapter~\ref{sec:3}'s four ways to build a floor, three put the commitment in something somebody holds — the architecture, a party inside the system, an institution outside it — and the objection above is about who that somebody is. The fourth does not. A norm carried across a population of differently formed parties has nobody sitting in the court's chair, because there is no office to vacate and no chartering statute to overrule, and it is the only one of the four with anything resembling a democratic account of where its authority comes from. Chapter~\ref{sec:3} declines it on other grounds, and the legitimacy advantage does not appear in the reckoning there.

The advantage is thinner than it looks, and thinner for the reason chapter~\ref{sec:3} gives. Custody moves up a level and does not leave. What a population buys is a floor with no holder for anyone to object to, and the same unelected body one step further back, now harder to see.

\runin{What the contents can borrow}
The contents do not have to be invented by the party installing them. The interests a short list of prohibitions would protect have been written down before, most durably in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, whose thirty articles have been the reference text since 1948 \autocite{un1948udhr}. A laboratory drawing its prohibitions from there is choosing which already-stated interests it will enforce rather than choosing what matters in the first place, and it can say which interest each prohibition answers to, which puts its selection where somebody else can dispute it.

Four limits hold that where it is. Thirty articles do not reduce to a short list without somebody doing the reducing, and the reduction is the laboratory's. The Declaration includes rights to work, to rest and to education, and provisions of that kind are the ones a floor has to leave out, for reasons of breadth its provenance does not touch. Its duties fall on states, and a rule a government owes its population is a different instrument from a constraint on what a company's model will do. And the Declaration has no enforcement of its own to lend. What the borrowing supplies is an answer to where the contents came from. It supplies nothing about who may impose them, which is the question this section opened with.

\runin{What follows for design}
Keep the floor narrow. Breadth costs legitimacy, which is true and general; what the German record adds is the specific mechanism. Enumeration removes discretion at the point of application, so that nobody applying the floor has to decide whether the present case is the emergency. A short list of things that will not be done to a person can be applied without that judgment. A general mandate to do good cannot be applied without it, and whoever ends up exercising it acquires exactly the power the list was written to constrain. Publish the interception positions in advance, which does not authorize anything but does convert a hidden exercise of power into a contestable one. And provide a route by which the parties the floor is exercised over can raise an objection that reaches somebody, which section~\ref{sec:3.7} specifies for one of them.

One disanalogy with the court has been working in the floor's favor and stops here. A judge who reads a right wrongly binds the case in front of them, and the next case is argued again, in front of somebody else; error enters one decision at a time, and the apparatus around it is built to catch it that way. A floor held by one bearer deployed at scale is one reading applied everywhere at once, wrong in the same direction on every occasion it is wrong at all, because the instances are not parties who have happened to agree. So the legitimacy cost scales twice over: with how much the floor covers, which the paragraph above prices, and with how many deployments hold the same one, which nothing above prices and no narrowness reduces. Section~\ref{sec:3.8}'s population is the only construction here that breaks the correlation, and that is the second of the two things it buys.

\runin{What amending it would take}
The apparatus for this is in chapter~\ref{sec:3} under another name. Section~\ref{sec:3.5}'s precommitment forms — the floor's contents published before deployment, the weights held so that no single party can retrain them, the running model attested against what was published, a quorum adverse in interest, a bond forfeited when the record shows the floor gone — are set out there as what makes removal expensive and visible. Nothing distinguishes an amendment from a removal at the level of what happens to the artifact; both end with a model that no longer refuses what it refused before. What distinguishes them is how many parties had to agree, whether the attempt is on the record when it fails, and whether the change was declared before it operated. So the same five terms are the procedure, and what was missing was never the mechanism. It was the decision to run it in the open.

Three conditions follow and two are already argued for. The number of parties who must agree to a change has to exceed the number needed to run the deployment, which is what a threshold scheme buys and what entrenchment amounts to on section~\ref{sec:3.3}'s own account. The attempt has to reach the record whether it carries or fails, and a proposal that fails moves no weights, so the parties who had to agree are the ones who record that they were asked. The failed attempt is the more informative of the two: a floor nobody has tried to change and a floor whose removal was proposed and refused look identical from outside, and only the record separates them. The third condition is new here. An amended floor binds forward and does not reach back: a model retrained to permit an act cannot then be offered as evidence that the act was never covered, which it otherwise can be, the artifact that would contradict it being the one that was replaced. Versioning the published list and dating the attestation is what makes that checkable, and it asks nobody to do anything they were not doing already.

The nearest instrument outside this book sits in the tradition named above. The Basic Law the Federal Republic adopted in 1949 requires two thirds of both chambers for any amendment at all, and then places a short list of principles beyond amendment entirely \autocite{germany1949basiclaw}. It was drafted against a particular memory, the 1933 statute by which a legislature voted away its own power to check what came next, and it is militant democracy given a text rather than an argument. A floor can have both layers in the same way: a published list that changes under a procedure, and a core the procedure cannot reach. What it cannot have is an account of who decides which is which that does not run straight back into this section's problem.

One disanalogy makes the floor's version harder than the constitutional one, and it is the same fact about deployment that makes a single bearer's error uniform. An amendment to a constitution reaches every future case because every court reads the same text. An amendment to a floor reaches a deployment only if that deployment is updated, so the amendment procedure is also an update channel — and an update channel is the operator's lever, the layer section~\ref{sec:4.2} finds nearest the act and section~\ref{sec:3.5} finds running for the whole length of a deployment that learns continuously. Whatever makes public amendment possible makes silent amendment possible by the same route. A procedure that shut the channel would be one under which no deployment could be corrected at all. So no version of this procedure closes it, and the exposure is the price of every correction the procedure exists to make.

None of it is a route of appeal. Every party to the procedure holds some part of the floor already, and nobody the floor is exercised over can begin it, be heard in it, or learn that it ran. What is answered here is the objection that a model's floor cannot be amended around the way a constitution can. Section~\ref{sec:3.7} answers half of the other one, for the party a wrongful refusal fell on. The party the floor is exercised on behalf of, who would decline the protection, still has nowhere to take a complaint, and neither procedure can be started by anyone the floor is exercised over.

A floor is a claim of authority, the claim is not discharged by disclosure, and the strongest thing that can be said for it is that the alternative on offer is a system perfectly obedient to whoever holds it.
