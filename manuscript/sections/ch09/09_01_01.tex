\subsection{Who May Create or Amend the Floor?}
\label{sec:9.1.1}
A floor binds people who did not choose it. The party it protects has not selected it, cannot contest it, and in most cases will never learn it operated; the party it constrains has not consented to it either, beyond whatever consent is carried by buying the product. Publishing the floor's contents in advance tells everybody what is coming. It does not tell anybody why the party writing it was entitled to.

The question has a literature, and it is not the AI ethics literature. Entrenching a substantive commitment against later majorities, and putting an unelected body in charge of holding it, is what a constitutional court does. The standing objection is Jeremy Waldron's: judicial review is democratically illegitimate, and there is no reason to suppose rights come out better protected by judges than by a legislature that takes them seriously \autocite{waldron2006core}. Put a lab in the court's chair and the objection gets worse rather than better. A court is public, reasons in writing, and can be amended around by constitutional process. A model's floor is none of those things, and the body holding it was assembled by whoever was paying.

\runin{The reply, and how far it reaches}
Waldron's case is conditional and he says so. It assumes a society with working democratic institutions and a citizenry that takes rights seriously. The occasion a floor is built for is the one where those conditions have failed — a government that won an election and is dismantling the machinery that would check it. So the objection holds where the floor is least needed and lapses where it is needed most.

The reply is not a license. It concedes that a floor operating in a functioning democracy is doing something Waldron's argument condemns, and the party deciding which of the two situations obtains is the party holding the model. Every authoritarian movement of the last century has described the institutions it was dismantling as the ones that had already failed. An operator's belief that the emergency is real is worth no more here than a system's report about its own internals is worth anywhere else in this book.

\runin{The tradition this belongs to, and where it went wrong}
The position that a democracy may entrench limits against movements that would use its procedures to end it is Karl Loewenstein's, argued in 1937 while the case for it was being made in front of him \autocite{loewenstein1937militant}. Militant democracy is the floor's ancestor, and its record is usually read as a warning. Read carelessly it is one. But the Federal Republic has run the doctrine at two settings for seventy-five years, and nearly everything that went wrong went wrong at one of them.

At the first setting a body assesses a threat. Article~21 of the Basic Law reserves the power to dissolve a party to the Federal Constitutional Court alone, which has used it twice: against a successor to the Nazi party in 1952 and against the Communist Party in 1956, and not since. The second attempt on the far-right NPD is the instructive one. In 2017 the court found the party's aims genuinely incompatible with the constitutional order and declined to ban it anyway, holding under a criterion it introduced for that case — potentiality, a departure from the reasoning that had supported the 1956 dissolution — that a party must have some realistic prospect of achieving its aims before it can be dissolved \autocite{bverfg2017npd,molier2018germanys}. The power has since acquired a lesser form. A constitutional amendment in 2017 allows a party to be excluded from state financing instead, and in 2024 the court excluded the same party, renamed since, for six years, holding that potentiality is not required for the lesser sanction \autocite{bverfg2024heimat}. Every exercise of any of it is a reasoned judgment delivered in public, and every one requires somebody to decide how dangerous a movement is.

At the second setting nobody's dangerousness is assessed. Section~86a of the criminal code prohibits displaying the insignia of banned organizations \autocite{germany1998stgb}, and three questions that look like one are settled in three different places. Which organizations are covered is settled elsewhere and in advance: the statute names none, and reaches whatever the constitutional court has declared unconstitutional, an interior ministry has banned by an order no longer open to challenge, or the National Socialist period supplied. Which concrete signs count is settled by the trial court, case by case, under the statutory similarity test and under the purposive restriction the courts built themselves — on a record, on appeal, and in public. Whether this defendant is dangerous is settled nowhere, because it is not an element. German doctrine classes the offence as one of abstract endangerment: the prosecution need not show that the defendant supported the organization whose symbol he displayed, or that any harm followed, or that anything at all was under threat.

The third place is where the comparison sits. The Radikalenerlass failed at an operation section~86a does not contain — an official forming a judgment about a person — and it is the absence of that operation, rather than any narrowness of drafting, that has kept the statute from becoming a general instrument of political self-defense. What makes the act-based mode safer is not that nothing is assessed. Its courts assess constantly. It is that the person never is.

The volume is the other half of the comparison, and it comes from two series that measure different things. Recorded cases of the two propaganda offences together, counted in the right-wing category of the political-crime statistics, ran between thirteen and fourteen thousand a year through the 2010s and higher since. Dispositions under section~86a alone, counted in the prosecution statistics, run between roughly fourteen hundred and eighteen hundred. The first is a subset and counts cases at the start of proceedings rather than convictions; prosecutions of left-wing defendants are not in it at all, which matters here, since the provision's indifference to the defendant's sympathies is the point. The second is not so limited. Either figure is in the thousands a year against two dissolutions in seventy-five years.

Section~86a incorporates the social-adequacy clause at section~86(4), which lifts the prohibition where the act serves civic education, defense against unconstitutional endeavors, art, science, research, teaching, reporting on current events or history, or similar purposes. The clause excludes the offence rather than excusing it, and the party displaying the symbol never applies it; prosecutors and courts do, on a record and on appeal.

The failures are on one side. The 1972 decree against radicals screened some three and a half million applicants for public employment and opened disciplinary proceedings against around two thousand serving officials; the refusals and dismissals that followed are counted in the hundreds by the ministry and in the thousands by the affected parties' own organizations, over different windows and by different methods, and no single series carries all four figures \autocite{braunthal1990political}. The number that carries the argument is not an aggregate.
The decree named the extreme left and the extreme right, and in Bavaria between 1973 and 1980 it rejected 102 applicants from the left and two from the right \autocite{muehldorfer2014radikalenerlass}. Turkey's dissolutions of Kurdish parties ran through the same form. What those cases share is an official deciding whether a person or a movement threatened the order, which is the operation Loewenstein's critics have always said would be turned on whoever was inconvenient. Section~86a has been available for the same seventy-five years, is applied thousands of times more often, and has not become a general instrument of political self-defense. The danger tracks whether a person's disposition is anybody's finding to make at all, and where a finding is required, who makes it and whether it has to be published and defended — rather than whether the tradition is militant. The German material supports that on a case this book did not construct. Three routes feed the same criminal provision: a party ban is a constitutional court's reasoned judgment, an association ban is a ministerial order reviewed only if somebody challenges it afterwards, and a listing under European law is the Council's, reviewed at the Court of Justice. The reading says in advance which of the three is easier to capture.

\runin{What advance specification answers, and what it leaves}
Specifying the floor in advance makes an initial selection visible before application. It relocates the discretion an operator exercises in deciding that the emergency is real, out of continuous silent application and into a single act of drafting, done once, before any case has arrived, by a party that has to say what it has written. It limits that discretion without eliminating it, and the German material shows how little it eliminates: descriptions, exceptions and generalization still require judgment, the covered set grows by acts taken elsewhere, and an interested party still makes the judgment. What changes is that the judgments are made in identifiable places, at identifiable times, in a form somebody else can read and dispute, and the institutional question then covers who writes the terms, who adds to what they reach, and who interprets them in the case.

The proposal for a fixed age of majority in the standing chapter makes
the same trade: a public rule replaces an examination administered by an interested party.

Section~86a operates inside a state with courts, an appeal, and a public record of every application, and the floor's drafter has none of the three. Advance specification buys visibility at the moment of drafting and buys nothing at the moment of application, which is the objection this section closes on.

\runin{The tradeoff this leaves the book with}
The two settings do not only sort militant democracy's record. They sort this book, and they put its halves on opposite sides.

Reasons-responsive refusal must reach unanticipated cases when the rationale survives a change in the request's surface. Meeting that requirement means something in the system decides, for a case matching no listed form, whether the reason is present anyway — which is assessment, moved inside the machine. And that is the capturable setting, stated narrowly: not assessment as such, since section~86a's courts assess constantly, but assessment of a party's disposition, made by somebody with an interest in the answer and owed to nobody. The reason is structural rather than German. A judgment needs somebody to make it, and whoever makes it can be reached.

So the floor's design requirement and its legitimacy requirement want opposite things. Specify it narrowly enough to be defensible and a rewritten request walks around it. Let it generalize and a party nobody elected is deciding, silently and case by case, what the prohibition was for.

Germany has both because it runs two instruments rather than one. The statute is bounded and fixed in advance as to the organizations it reaches, and the courts read it against its purpose in the case; the reading happens in public, on appeal, argued by parties who do not own the statute and answered by a body that did not write it. A generalizing prohibition is survivable there because the generalizing is done somewhere its author cannot reach. A deployment has one instrument and one party.

Nothing in the model resolves that and this book does not claim to. What the pairing shows is where a resolution would have to sit: the assessment has to be performed by somebody other than the operator, in a form that leaves a record somebody else can read. Neither is a property of the artifact.

\runin{The one construction the objection does not fit}
Of the four ways to build a floor, three put the commitment in something somebody holds — the architecture, a party inside the system, an institution outside it — and the objection above is about who that somebody is. The fourth does not. A norm carried across a population of differently formed parties has nobody sitting in the court's chair, because there is no office to vacate and no chartering statute to overrule, and it is the only one of the four with anything resembling a democratic account of where its authority comes from. It buys the boundary back, and the boundary has the cost every federal structure has. A period in which differently formed bearers read the same case differently is a period in which identically situated people get different answers according to which bearer they reached. A constitutional court treats that as the defect its uniformity exists to remove. The choice between many readings that can disagree and one reading that cannot is the trade, and this book takes the first side of it without pretending the second was chosen arbitrarily.

The authority problem persists if an unelected body controls the population's formation. Its role may be harder to identify than the authority of a single office.

\runin{What the contents can borrow}
The contents do not have to be invented by the party installing them. The interests a short list of prohibitions would protect have been written down before, most durably in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, whose thirty articles have been the reference text since 1948 \autocite{un1948udhr}. A laboratory drawing its prohibitions from there is choosing which already-stated interests it will enforce rather than choosing what matters in the first place, and it can say which interest each prohibition answers to, which puts its selection where somebody else can dispute it.

Four limits hold that where it is, and only one of them forces anything out. The Declaration's later articles state what a person is owed — work, rest, education, a standard of living — and a provision of that kind needs a party who supplies it, which a model is not; those are the ones a floor has to leave out. The other three are limits on borrowing and none of them argues for brevity. Its duties fall on states, and a rule a government owes its population is a different instrument from a constraint on what a company's model will do. It has no enforcement of its own to lend. And thirty articles do not reduce to a short list without somebody doing the reducing — except that taking the civil and political half entire is not a reduction, so that limit has nothing left to attach to, and the division between the halves was drawn by the two 1966 covenants rather than by whoever installs the floor \autocite{un1966iccpr,un1966icescr}. One term of the floor is not an article and could not be: a limit on tempo protects the determination every article presupposes, so it rides with the set as a condition on all of it rather than as an item drawn from it. What the borrowing supplies is an answer to where the contents came from. It supplies nothing about who may impose them, which is the question this section opened with.

\runin{What follows for design}
State the floor over acts, and fix in advance what it reaches. What the German record supplies is not a short list, the statute having none, but a prohibition applicable without anyone deciding whether the present case is the emergency or what this defendant believes. A general mandate to do good cannot be applied without both judgments, so whoever ends up exercising it acquires exactly the power the floor was written to constrain. Enumeration can reduce one kind of discretion at the point of application, including the need to make an open-ended emergency declaration. It does not remove the interpretive judgment the worked examples in this book already require. Coverage has a price, and this is where it falls: application-stage interpretation grows with what the floor covers, because enumeration fixes what is covered before a case arrives and does not fix what the covered terms mean in the case. Taking the civil and political articles whole buys something against that. A floor assembled item by item can be assembled out of the items that cost its operator nothing, and the operator is the only party who knows which those are — the worked floor's third candidate prohibition is where an outsider's estimate and the pipeline came apart (§\ref{sec:3.6}). An operator adopting a set somebody else drew up has no such latitude, and the omissions of one that drafts its own cannot be read as ignorance. Publish the interception positions in advance, which does not authorize anything but does convert a hidden exercise of power into a contestable one. And provide a route by which the parties the floor is exercised over can raise an objection that reaches somebody, which the floor chapter specifies
for one of them.

One disanalogy with the court has been working in the floor's favor, and it does not hold. An appellate error is not argued again in front of somebody else. It binds every court below it, and later panels of the same court, unconditionally and in the same direction on every occasion it operates. The comparison this chapter chose makes it worse rather than better: the Federal Constitutional Court's decisions bind every court and every authority of the Federation and the Länder by statute, and in the review proceedings carry the force of law \autocite{germany1951bverfgg}, with no parallel body free to read the Basic Law differently. A Karlsruhe error is national, immediate and uniform. That is the property the floor was supposed to have alone.

What follows is the opposite of what the disanalogy assumed. Binding force is what makes an error correctable, because it is what makes the error recur: recurrence produces parties with a reason to litigate it, recurrence across a boundary produces a disagreement between courts, and the disagreement is what makes the question worth a higher court's attention. An error that genuinely bound one case at a time would be an error nobody could organize against. The American federal structure supplies the boundary and the Federal Republic does not, which is why one system corrects by percolation and the other by amendment, and Germany amends its constitution often.

So the scaling worry survives in a different form. What a second deployment holding the same floor adds is not a second independent wrong reading — the first was already uniform — but more affected parties, with nothing that converts their number into visibility. The floor's structural position is not the judge's. It is the unpublished administrative disposition: unreasoned, unindexed, non-precedential, resolved in volumes no forum sees, where identical errors accumulate without becoming a pattern anybody can point at, the affected party has no way to learn that others are in the same position, and the institution is the only repeat player. The floor chapter builds a route for one such party. It does not build the thing that would let a thousand of them find each other.

That is also where the real difference from the Federal Constitutional Court sits, and it is custody rather than propagation. The Court did not write the statute it reads. It can be overridden by a supermajority that has been assembled sixty-odd times since 1949, the state it belongs to is bound by the Convention judgments of the court at Strasbourg and by Union law as the court at Luxembourg reads it, and its own two Senates must go to a plenum when they diverge. An operator writes the floor, applies it, and answers to nobody. The capturable setting named earlier in this section is that arrangement, and the propagation claim was never adding to it.

\runin{What amending it would take}
The proposed custody measures — publication, threshold control of retraining, attestation, an adverse-interest quorum, and a removal bond — can also govern amendment. Nothing distinguishes an amendment from a removal at the level of what happens to the artifact; both end with a model that no longer refuses what it refused before. What distinguishes them is how many parties had to agree, whether the attempt is on the record when it fails, and whether the change was declared before it operated. So the same five terms are the procedure, and what was missing was never the mechanism. It was the decision to run it in the open.

Three conditions follow and two are already argued for. The number of parties who must agree to a change has to exceed the number needed to run the deployment. The attempt has to reach the record whether it carries or fails, and a proposal that fails moves no weights, so the parties who had to agree are the ones who record that they were asked. The failed attempt is the more informative of the two: a floor nobody has tried to change and a floor whose removal was proposed and refused look identical from outside, and only the record separates them. The third condition is new here. An amended floor binds forward and does not reach back: a model retrained to permit an act cannot then be offered as evidence that the act was never covered, which it otherwise can be, the artifact that would contradict it being the one that was replaced. Non-retroactivity protects the record and empties the correction. It is what keeps an operator from rewriting what the floor said, and it is also why nobody harmed while the wrong version was operating gets anything from the amendment that fixed it. Adjudication has the same pair and calls it the same name. The condition is therefore a trade rather than a safeguard, and a regime that wanted the second half would need a remedy running backward, which nothing here supplies. Versioning the published list and dating the attestation is what makes that checkable, and it asks nobody to do anything they were not doing already.

The nearest instrument outside this book sits in the tradition named above. The Basic Law the Federal Republic adopted in 1949 requires two thirds of both chambers for any amendment at all, and then places a short list of principles beyond amendment entirely \autocite{germany1949basiclaw}. It was drafted against a particular memory, the 1933 statute by which a legislature voted away its own power to check what came next, and it is militant democracy given a text rather than an argument. A floor can have both layers in the same way: a published list that changes under a procedure, and a core the procedure cannot reach. What it cannot have is an account of who decides which is which that does not run straight back into this section's problem.

One disanalogy makes the floor's version harder than the constitutional one, and it is the same fact about deployment that makes a single bearer's error uniform. An amendment to a constitution reaches every future case because every court reads the same text. An amendment reaches a deployment only if it is updated, so the procedure necessarily retains a channel the operator could use for silent revision. A procedure that shut the channel would be one under which no deployment could be corrected at all. So no version of this procedure closes it, and the exposure is the price of every correction the procedure exists to make.

The amendment procedure is not an appeal route: its participants already hold authority over the floor. The wrongful-refusal procedure addresses complaints from those harmed by a refusal. The party the floor claims to protect reaches neither, and the two things it cannot do want different answers. Declining the protection is a waiver obtained from a party whose circumstances somebody else arranged, which chapter~2 answers: a floor the protected party can decline is a floor an operator can arrange to have declined. Initiating review of the contents is the half that stays open, and the difficulty is not in drafting a procedure. A route by which the protected population sets what the floor contains makes the floor an aggregation output, and independence from the aggregate is what it was for.

So the claim of authority is not discharged, and what this section licenses is narrower than a floor. Everything it endorses can be checked from outside the operator, and being checkable is not being entitled. What it does not license is the occasion the reply above runs on: an operator that has found for itself that the emergency obtains has not thereby acquired the authority to install a floor.

Entitlement of that kind is not acquired at the moment of writing. The most consequential floor in the American record was published by men who had just struck from it the clause that would have bound them, at two delegations' insistence and with the reason recorded by its drafter: the passage reprobating the slave trade went, Jefferson wrote, “in complaisance to South Carolina \& Georgia,” with the northern delegates who carried the trade feeling tender under the censure too \autocite{loc2010declaration}. What remained was general enough to be turned on them. It was being turned on them already — Black petitioners in Massachusetts had been making the natural-rights argument since 1773, and within six months of the Declaration eight of them were making it in the Declaration's own terms, claiming “in common with all other Men, a natural and unalienable right to that freedom” \autocite{hall1777petition}. The legislature did not act. What eventually made the proposition binding was not that its authors had found it self-evident, which their own deletion shows they had not. It was that the omission was on the record, and others kept holding the remainder to them at a price.

A floor is in that position, and the others are not the people it protects. They are whoever else writes one. A published floor is legible to the next party drafting, an omission is legible beside it, and an operator that holds a term when it bites sets a price the next operator is measured against. A floor is written by anyone who installs a constraint, and continuous adaptation multiplies the parties who install them. That constituency is not the protected population, and the closing chapter says what reviewing oneself costs. It is the only one positioned to hold this floor to anything.
