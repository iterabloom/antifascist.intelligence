\section{Authority Over the Floor}
\label{sec:9.1}

A floor is not only a list of acts a system will refuse. It is an
allocation of authority. Somebody chooses its contents, somebody decides
when those contents may be amended, somebody determines whether the
deployed system still holds them, and somebody has the power to stop or
alter the deployment when it does not. A further question precedes all
four: who has standing to initiate any of those decisions.

Those powers need not belong to the same party, and ordinarily should
not. A laboratory may have the technical access needed to inspect the
system while lacking legitimate authority to decide what the floor
protects. An outside body may have a stronger claim to determine its
contents while lacking the access or leverage needed to enforce them.
A reviewer may be able to publish a finding without being able to make
anyone act on it. Calling all of these arrangements ``oversight''
conceals the differences that matter.

The custody problem begins when one institution holds the powers
together. A party that can state the commitment, interpret it, inspect
its own compliance, and decide what follows from a violation has not
bound itself. It has described what it currently intends to do. The
sections that follow separate those powers and ask what could make each
one legitimate, visible, and enforceable against the institution that
operates the system.


\runin{The custody of review and the dangers of corporate doublespeak}

Corporate doublespeak is not merely a gap between what an institution
says and what it does. It is an arrangement in which the party making a
commitment also decides what counts as compliance, controls the evidence
by which compliance could be judged, and determines whether an adverse
finding has consequences. The language can be sincere when it is
published. What makes it doublespeak is that nothing outside the
institution holds the commitment in place when keeping it becomes
costly.

Google's AI Principles were published in 2018 after employee opposition
to Project Maven, a Pentagon program that used machine learning to
analyze drone footage. Thousands of employees signed a letter opposing
the company's involvement and roughly a dozen resigned
\autocite{pichai2018principles}. The episode shows that internal dissent can
impose a cost large enough to change an organization's stated policy. It
does not show that the resulting policy can bind the organization after
the pressure recedes. A commitment extracted by a temporary balance of
forces lasts only as long as something preserves that balance.

OpenAI's history makes a related point from a different angle: founded on a public commitment to open publication, it withheld the full version of GPT-2 in 2019 over misuse concerns — a safety-motivated retreat from its own transparency norm that was itself controversial — and in the same year restructured from a nonprofit into a capped-profit company to raise the capital its research increasingly required \autocite{openai2019lp}. Neither move was dishonest on its face. Both show how a stated commitment to transparency erodes the moment an organization has a competing incentive strong enough to test it.

That is the authority problem in its ordinary form. An institution may
have good reasons to amend a commitment, and an unamendable rule can
become dangerous or obsolete. But if the institution bound by the rule
is also the only party entitled to revise it, then the commitment
describes a present intention rather than a constraint. Disclosure
allows the public to observe the change after it occurs. It does not
give anyone standing to contest the change before it operates or to
require the institution to justify it.

The removal of Timnit Gebru and Margaret Mitchell supplies the harder
test. Google employed both researchers to examine the risks of its own
systems and retained the authority to dismiss them when that examination
became institutionally costly \autocite{dickey2021mitchell}. The defect was
not a lack of expertise or a failure to create an ethics function. It
was that the institution being inspected also controlled the
inspectors' employment, their access to evidence, and the consequences
of their findings. Review existed, but custody never moved. The
institution could point to the existence of internal criticism as
evidence of openness while retaining the power to remove the critics
whose work tested that claim.

These cases separate four powers that corporate ethics language tends
to collapse: the power to state a commitment, the power to interpret or
amend it, the power to inspect whether it is being kept, and the power
to impose a consequence when it is not. Concentrating all four in the
organization being constrained turns a rule into a description of what
that organization currently prefers. For a floor meant to bind its
operator, amendment and inspection must therefore involve parties whose
access, tenure, and authority do not depend on the operator's continuing
consent. Otherwise the institution announces a commitment while
preserving custody of every mechanism that could compel it to keep one.
