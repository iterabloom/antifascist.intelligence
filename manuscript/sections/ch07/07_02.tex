\section{Recuperation at the Reward: RLHF}
\label{sec:7.2}
Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the technique behind most deployed systems' behavioral tuning, and it works by collecting pairwise comparisons from human raters — which of two responses is better — and training a reward model on the aggregate. Stephen Casper and thirty-one coauthors published a 2023 survey of the method's open problems, and one of them is structural, and more data does not reach it: RLHF trains toward whatever a population of raters agrees on, which means real, persistent disagreement among the raters is treated as noise to average out rather than as information the system might need to represent \autocite{casper2023open}. A model that has learned to produce the response most raters rank highest has not learned what to do when the humans it serves are split. It has learned to hide the split.

Every rating moves something. A comparison a rater makes enters the fit, shifts the reward model by however much one comparison shifts it, and is not thrown away.

What the operation removes is what made the disagreement a disagreement. Three properties go in the compression. The first is provenance: a comparison arrives without the standpoint that produced it, so nothing downstream can represent that the people who would live with an answer split from the people who would administer it. The second is persistence: a disagreement recurring in the same shape across many raters over months enters the fit as random rater noise does, and leaves it as the same quantity, variance around a mean. The third is structure: a coherent position held by a fifth of raters, internally consistent and stable, becomes a small displacement of the majority direction — arithmetically faithful, and carrying nothing about the position.

A fourth property was never collected. A rater judges which of two responses is better. No output is available by which they can record that neither is acceptable, that the pair is malformed, that the dimension along which better is being judged is the wrong dimension, or that the question presupposes something false. The same output was missing one stage upstream, where an annotator can apply a category and cannot contest it. The agenda arrives as a given at both stages, and is set at neither.

The mechanism combines distribution collapse with agenda control. Recuperation was never the claim that a mechanism does nothing. Debord's point is that dissatisfaction is sold back as a commodity of opposition: the dissent is put to work, and what it works for is the arrangement it was aimed at. The split is solicited, at scale, deliberately; it moves the system; and where it moves the system is toward the center of the very distribution whose disagreement it was. Multi-agent systems that learn norms from each other, and deliberation inside open-ended play, are different cases; neither reaches this case, which is the ordinary one.

One alternative treats disagreement as the thing to be modeled rather than compressed. Jury learning, developed by Mitchell Gordon and coauthors at Stanford, trains a model to predict how each individual annotator in a dataset would label an item, then lets a practitioner assemble a “jury” — a chosen composition of annotators, in a chosen proportion — and see how the verdict shifts depending on who is empaneled. In their tests on toxicity labeling, letting eighteen community moderators build their own juries changed the classification outcome on 14 percent of items \autocite{gordon2022jury}. The method was built for content moderation, not for the more open-ended ethical judgment an antifascist AI system would need to make, and has not been extended there — but it demonstrates that a system can be built to represent whose disagreement is being resolved and how.

There is a way of failing in the other direction and it is not obviously better. A scheme that models each rater exactly, and answers each of them in their own terms, has stopped averaging and has also stopped producing what a shared answer produces, which is the discovery that somebody else got the same one. Collapse the distribution and nobody sees the split; resolve it person by person and nobody sees the others. A workforce on individually negotiated wages against individually set targets has the same problem, and accuracy does not solve it. Divided finely enough a population stops being one, which is what Deleuze coined the \emph{dividual} to name \autocite{deleuze1992postscript}.

Neither averaging the disagreement nor modeling it answers the harder version. When the people a system serves disagree, and one side's position is itself the product of coercion or a captured information environment, treating all disagreement as equally worth representing is not obviously the right response either. Modeling disagreement honestly and recognizing when a disagreement is the product of unfree conditions are different problems. Only the first one has an active research program behind it.
