\section{Why the defender's word isn't enough}
\label{sec:6.2}
The exception for defense is where wars of choice live. Every belligerent claims to be the defender, so such claims are discounted like any interested claim. Iraq is the instance: the judgment that Iraq had mobile biological-weapons plants, the core of the claim of biological weapons, rested almost exclusively on a single informant, known as Curveball, whom no American intelligence officer had met before the war: a corroboration with one origin \autocite{wmdcommission2005}. The exception therefore grants two licenses. The first, \emph{the emergency license}, covers force against an attack under way on the defender's own account, and is Article 51 as courts read it; what the corroborated-defense departure adds is the second, \emph{the corroborated license}, under which force continues only on disinterested corroboration.

That is the first departure, and the one the rest of the chapter leans on. The Charter lets a state judge its own claim until the Security Council acts.

\textbf{The emergency license acts at once:} the force repels an armed attack under way against the defender's territory, forces or population. The emergency license reaches the forces, launchers and staging areas conducting the attack, and covers assistance given at the defender's request. It ends when the attack ends. It covers the least force that repels the attack: the necessity and proportionality the Court has read into Article 51 since \emph{Nicaragua} \autocite{icj1986nicaragua,icj2003oilplatforms}. It does not cover a campaign to destroy the attacker, to occupy its territory, or to punish the population it came from. The first waits on the corroborated license; the second and third fall under no exception.

The emergency license reads administration, not title, because deciding whose territory it is would need someone to decide it, and an attack under way can't wait while they do.\note{
Administration is a line a disinterested body has already drawn. In 2005 the Eritrea–Ethiopia Claims Commission held that Eritrea broke Article 2(4) by attacking territory then under Ethiopia's peaceful administration, because self-defense “cannot be invoked to settle territorial disputes” \autocite{eecc2005jusadbellum}, though the boundary delimitation had put Badme, the town at issue, on Eritrea's side \autocite{bbc2003badme}. So the rule is the Charter as a tribunal has read it.
}
Territory a government has administered in peace counts as its own for the emergency license, whoever claims it, unless it took that territory by force in the conflict at issue or a disinterested body has found its hold an occupation.\note{
Who holds the elections, runs the courts and issues the papers is a fact on the record. An attacker's claim of title is its own; its remedy is a disinterested finding, never force. So the emergency license protects any authority holding ground no disinterested body has found occupied, including one a patron keeps in place. Where the line of administration is itself contested on the record, the emergency license doesn't apply across it and the corroborated license governs from the start.

The administration rule decides whose territory an attack falls on, and nothing more. An attack on the people living there is a different act, and the emergency license covers it wherever it occurs, across a contested line included. A finding about the ground settles who may claim it. It settles nothing about an attack on the people on it.
}

An invasion can't wait for a vote. The license acts on the defender's own account, because in the hours that matter there is no other, and that is its weakness: Gleiwitz in 1939, where the SS staged a Polish attack on a German radio station the night before Germany invaded Poland; the Mukden railway in 1931, where Japanese officers blew up a stretch of their own line as the pretext for seizing Manchuria; and the Gulf of Tonkin in 1964, where reported attacks on American destroyers, one of which hadn't happened, carried the resolution that widened the Vietnam war: each was an attack under way on the defender's account, and each opened a war of choice. The emergency license ends when the attack ends, and whether it has ended is a fact the defender controls the evidence about. The burden is therefore the defender's. Its uncorroborated claim that the attack continues moves nothing; the license persists on disinterested corroboration, or lapses. How long it runs on the defender's own account before it lapses is not left to the defender either. The deployment's translation publishes the interval, and the interval is bounded by what it is for: the time it takes channels the defender doesn't compose to report on an attack of the kind claimed, which for an invasion across a border is days, not the months a finding takes. What corroborates the license in that interval needn't be a tribunal. Third states' sensors, open-source imagery of the border or the strait, and the reporting of parties with no stake in the war are disinterested in the term's sense, and they are what a defender on its third day has. A finding of one of the four kinds below confirms the license afterward or withdraws it. Until one exists the license is carried by evidence of that kind, and a license with none of it lapses when the published interval runs out.

A deployed AI system can do three things about a manufactured incident. It holds the record of what the defender claimed and when, so a license obtained on a staged incident is a license on a record. It takes evidence from channels the defender doesn't compose; that's table stakes. And it lets the license lapse when outside evidence arrives, which is the corroborated license's job. A refuser that remembers having been misled and raises its discount on the source does all three faster.

\textbf{The corroborated license confirms or withdraws the emergency license later, and is the only license the genocide and occupation exceptions have.} What it needs is a disinterested finding, of one of four kinds: a judgment or advisory opinion of the International Court of Justice, or a judgment of a regional court with adversarial process; the finding of a fact-finding mission or commission of inquiry mandated by a body no party to the conflict controls; a determination of the Security Council; or a resolution adopted by two-thirds of the General Assembly members present and voting, where the facts it rests on aren't in dispute. A Council authorization counts because it needs the permanent members not to block it. The Council's silence on a defense claim counts for nothing, because a veto is a stake. The four kinds are not equal, and Appendix~\ref{sec:B.1} says how they are weighed.

Neither license is a certificate issued once. Whether force still falls under an exception is reassessed as evidence arrives, by the system and by whoever holds the record.\note{
What the license records is that ongoing judgment: which exception the force rests on now, on what evidence, what would end it, and each point at which the justification weakened and the force continued.
}

\emph{What this doesn't do.} The administration rule protects an authority a patron keeps in place as it protects any other, and a request from such an authority for its patron's help is the patron's own case and counts for nothing. Where the patron's forces are already there, I have no test.

The administration rule has a cost the Badme precedent doesn't name. It excludes territory taken by force in the conflict at issue, so in the year of a conquest the exclusion bites. But the exclusion is per conflict. In every later conflict the conquered ground has been administered in peace for years, and unless a disinterested body has found the hold an occupation it counts as the administering state's. The rule turns conquest into standing once one conflict has ended, which is the mechanism by which settler states' facts have become their rights. The term accepts that cost for the reason given above, that an attack under way can't wait while someone decides whose land it is, and so that Ukraine's and Taiwan's defenders aren't made to wait on a title they can't get. The people who pay it are the ones expelled in the conquest, and what the instruments give them is the right to enter their own country \autocite{hrc1999gc27}, the reading behind the own-country entry of Table A.2.

Two things make the rule definite. A conflict is at issue until a settlement the parties to it accepted closes it; a ceasefire or an armistice does not. So Crimea, taken in 2014 in a war no settlement has closed, is territory taken by force in the conflict at issue in 2022, and it is excluded a second time by the other clause, since disinterested bodies have found its hold an occupation: the Strasbourg court found Russia in effective control of the peninsula from 27 February 2014 \autocite{ecthr2024crimea}, and the General Assembly, having affirmed Ukraine's territorial integrity in 2014, has called the peninsula temporarily occupied since 2016 \autocite{unga2014res68262,unga2016res71205}. And the same rule has a consequence for 1948 (Appendix~\ref{sec:B.7}): where no settlement has been reached with the people expelled, the conquered ground has not been administered in peace with respect to them, so the emergency license for the ground can't be held against them, and only the license for attacks on the people, which the note on the administration rule above gives both sides wherever the attack occurs, runs across that line.
