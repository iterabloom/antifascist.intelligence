\section{Recuperation at the Reward: RLHF}
\label{sec:7.2}
Section~\ref{sec:7.1} found the pattern in how a training corpus is labelled. It appears again one stage downstream, in how a model is tuned on human feedback, and the second instance is the purer one.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is the technique behind most deployed systems' behavioral tuning, and it works by collecting pairwise comparisons from human raters — which of two responses is better — and training a reward model on the aggregate. Stephen Casper and thirty-one coauthors published a 2023 survey of the method's open problems, and one of them is structural rather than fixable with more data: RLHF trains toward whatever a population of raters agrees on, which means real, persistent disagreement among the raters is treated as noise to average out rather than as information the system might need to represent \autocite{casper2023open}. A model that has learned to produce the response most raters rank highest has not learned what to do when the humans it serves are genuinely split. It has learned to hide the split.

That arrangement has a name in this book. A channel that collects disagreement and is built so that the disagreement changes nothing is the first feature of section~\ref{sec:2.1.4}'s structural signature, and RLHF is its purest technical instance: the split is solicited, at scale, deliberately, and averaged away in the same operation that records it. Section~\ref{sec:5.5.1} covers multi-agent systems that learn norms from each other and section~\ref{sec:5.7.2} covers deliberation inside open-ended play; neither reaches this case, which is the ordinary one.

One alternative treats disagreement as the thing to be modeled, not eliminated. Jury learning, developed by Mitchell Gordon and coauthors at Stanford, trains a model to predict how each individual annotator in a dataset would label an item, then lets a practitioner assemble a "jury" — a chosen composition of annotators, in a chosen proportion — and see how the verdict shifts depending on who is empaneled, instead of collapsing every annotator into one fixed ground truth from the start. In their tests on toxicity labeling, letting practitioners build different juries changed the classification outcome for 14 percent of contested cases \autocite{gordon2022jury}. The method was built for content moderation, not for the more open-ended ethical judgment an antifascist AI system would need to make, and has not been extended there — but it demonstrates that a system can be built to represent whose disagreement is being resolved and how, instead of collapsing everyone into RLHF's single number.

Neither method answers the harder version of the question this book's own frame raises: when the people a system serves disagree, and one side's position is itself the product of coercion or a captured information environment, treating all disagreement as equally worth representing is not obviously the right response either. Modeling disagreement honestly and recognizing when a disagreement is the product of unfree conditions are different problems. Only the first one has an active research program behind it.
