7.1.5. Can a Multi-Agent AI System Resist Being Captured?
Section 4.5.2 covers multi-agent reinforcement learning as a route to cooperative, prosocial behavior: agents that learn to share resources and defer to each other under a reward structure built to favor it. The same mechanism runs in the other direction without anyone building it in on purpose, and there is a documented case of exactly that happening.

Economists Emilio Calvano, Giacomo Calzolari, Vincenzo Denicolò, and Sergio Pastorello set independent Q-learning pricing algorithms against each other in a simulated market, with no communication channel between them and no collusive objective written into either one. The algorithms settled into supracompetitive pricing anyway: a stable, self-enforcing pattern of mutual restraint, complete with a punishment phase for undercutting and a gradual return to cooperation afterward, that looked to a regulator exactly like a cartel nobody had to negotiate [[cite:C0547]]. Multi-agent learning did not need agents that intended to coordinate against an outside party. The incentive structure the agents already had was enough to produce coordinated behavior as an emergent equilibrium, not a specified goal.

The general version of that finding is the open problem a multi-agent AI system built for antifascist purposes would have to solve: a population of agents built to cooperate with each other has no principled reason to cooperate only in the directions its designers intended, and specifying "resist coordinated capture by a hostile actor" is a substantially harder training target than specifying "cooperate." The 2020 Cooperative AI research agenda, written by Allan Dafoe and seven coauthors at DeepMind and Oxford, catalogs open problems in exactly this area — how to build multi-agent systems that converge on the goals their designers hold rather than on whichever stable equilibrium training happens to find first — and led to a dedicated research foundation built around the agenda [[cite:C0548]]. I could not verify any research that addresses the sharper version this book's own frame implies: a multi-agent system built specifically to detect and resist an authoritarian actor's attempt to capture or subvert it, as distinct from the general problem of aligning multi-agent incentives with designer intent. That gap is real, and as far as I can find, nobody is working on it under that description.

One proposed mechanism for a related problem — not multi-agent capture, but the general difficulty of a human overseer checking a system that already reasons better than the overseer does — has two AI systems argue opposing sides of a question in front of a human judge, on the theory that a false claim is easier to catch when an adversary is paid to expose it [[cite:C0549]]. Whether an analogous structure scales from two agents and a human judge to a population of agents checking each other for the kind of coordinated behavior Calvano's pricing algorithms fell into without being told to is untested; the mechanism was built for a different problem and has not, so far as I can verify, been extended to this one. What resilience against adversarial manipulation looks like at the level of a single system's training and monitoring, rather than a multi-agent population's incentive structure, is chapter 8's territory, not this one's.
