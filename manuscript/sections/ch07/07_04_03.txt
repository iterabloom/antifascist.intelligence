7.4.3. Making Review Binding
Section 2.4.4 worked out what an oversight board for sentient-AI research needs to look like — interdisciplinary, empowered to require a less invasive substitute for a risky protocol, able to trigger re-review when a system under study develops capacities its original consent didn't cover — and ended on the question this section answers: what makes that board's ruling something a researcher has to obey, rather than a recommendation a determined lab can route around.

Human-subjects research already solved a version of this problem, and the solution is institutional rather than criminal. An institutional review board's authority does not come from any power to fine or arrest a noncompliant researcher directly. It comes from a federalwide assurance: an institution promises, as a condition of eligibility for federal research funding, that all of its human-subjects research will go through review, and a federal office can suspend that assurance if the promise is broken — cutting off an entire institution's federal funding, not just the offending lab's [[cite:C0423]]. The leverage is economic, not punitive, and it works precisely because almost no research institution can function without the funding at stake.

The same structure transfers to sentient-AI research directly. A funding body, a journal's editorial standards, or a jurisdiction's research-licensing regime can condition eligibility on review-board clearance the same way federal grants already condition eligibility on IRB clearance — making noncompliance expensive to the institution rather than merely improper for the researcher. What section 7.4.1 leaves open is which cases the board needs to see at all: a graded framework means low-capacity systems can be exempted from full review the way minimal-risk human studies already qualify for expedited review, freeing the board's scrutiny for the cases where a system's status is genuinely contested.

<<h>>What the board applies<</h>>
Section 2.4.6 already named the substantive standard: animal-research ethics' Three Rs — replace an experiment with a non-sentient alternative wherever one exists, reduce the number of sentient subjects and the duration of exposure to the minimum an experiment actually needs, and refine the protocol to minimize whatever harm remains. A binding board is what makes those three questions something a protocol has to answer before it runs, not language a paper cites afterward to justify what already happened. In practice, applying the Three Rs to a candidate sentient system means asking, in order: can a simulation or a non-sentient model answer the same question; if not, what is the minimum exposure that still produces a valid result; and given that minimum, what escalation triggers stop the study before harm compounds. A board with real teeth can require a negative answer to the first question be documented, not merely asserted, before it will consider the second.

The Three Rs' own logic — defaulting toward the least invasive option available — argues for a specific evidentiary rule at every stage of this apparatus: a system's default experimental status stays the more protective one until the case for the less protective status is actually made, not the reverse. That default has a real cost, and section 2.4.7 is explicit that neither error it produces is free: it will sometimes extend protection to a system that turns out not to have needed it, and sometimes, before a threshold is met, withhold protection from one that did. The Three Rs answer only what a board does once a subject is inside its scope. Where the line sits at all is section 7.4.1's question, not this one.
