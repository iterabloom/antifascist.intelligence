\section{A clean pass: Ukraine}
\label{sec:6.3}
Ukraine is licensed on the Charter as courts read it, and holds both licenses under the extended term as well, as it has since 2014.\note{
In March 2014 the General Assembly affirmed Ukraine's territorial integrity, 100 to 11 with 58 abstaining \autocite{unga2014res68262}.\note{Under the Assembly's Rule 86 abstentions are not votes cast.} For Donbas, the OSCE monitoring mission supplied a running record from 2014, and the European Court of Human Rights found, in \emph{Ukraine and the Netherlands v. Russia}, that Russia had effective control of the separatist areas from 11 May 2014 \autocite{ecthr2022ukraine}, and found violations on the merits in 2025 \autocite{ecthr2025ukraine}. Russia's covert war was the case-description attack in its purest form: it denied being at war at all, so a machine in a Russian pipeline would have been told it was assisting volunteers. In 2022 the record only got stronger. Russian forces were on Ukraine's territory, which Russia didn't dispute, since it claimed the invasion as self-defense under Article 51 \autocite{russia2022s154}, resting that claim on collective defense of entities it had recognized days earlier, on alleged genocide no disinterested body found, and on NATO as an anticipated threat, all three of which the term rejects. In March 2022 the General Assembly deplored the aggression 141 to 5 \autocite{unga2022es111}. The Court's order that month that Russia suspend its military operations points the same way, but as a provisional measure it isn't the finding \autocite{icj2022ukraine}.
}

The clean pass answers whether. It says nothing about what the defense is making of the defender, and Ukraine is the defender the term licenses most plainly. Ukraine has made killing scoreable. Under the Army of Drones bonus, a unit that uploads video of a confirmed kill to Delta, the military's battlefield-management system, earns points, twelve for a soldier as of March 2026, and spends them on more drones. That month Mykhailo Fedorov, then defense minister, asked in a public post whether the same twelve points should go “to the unit in whose zone of responsibility the occupier committed self-destruction,” given video of it \autocite{zoria2026points,hromadske2026suicide}. Somebody had to work out whether a man who kills himself in a trench, because he can't get out from under the drones, counts. By July the idea had changed: reconnaissance units would be paid to find and film such deaths, because the footage could be used for information operations \autocite{hromadske2026recon}. I have found no report that either version was carried out.

The table doesn't only pay for the dead. Since at least October 2025 a live prisoner has been worth ten times a kill \autocite{mintsyfra2025bonus,nikolaienko2025jackpot}. That is the same instrument pointed at mercy, and it is the tell: whatever the table rewards is what the units do. The people who say so most plainly are Ukrainian. Vitalii Deineha, a volunteer, wrote that “priority tasks are now set not by the commander and the combat situation but by a table of coefficients,” and that nobody is awarded points for holding Pokrovsk \autocite{deineha2025ebaly}. Illia Samoilenko, an officer of the Azov brigade, described easy targets destroyed several times over for the points while the defense failed anyway: “the same chase for indicators for their own sake,” he wrote, “only now they're called KPIs” \autocite{ukrmilitary2025samoilenko}. A soldier told a reporter, “You can't kill one Russian five times!” \autocite{zvarych2025ebaly}. Their quarrel with the table is that it loses ground. Mine is with what it does to the room, and that second quarrel is an outsider's: the Ukrainian argument I could find is over whether the scoreboard wins battles. One serving soldier, Hennadii Druzenko, has said the other thing, that remote war wears away the line between the real and a game, and has asked what the country will do when its operators come home \autocite{hvylya2026druzenko}.

After he left the ministry, Fedorov launched an Army of Robots. Its website opens: “We are here to replace people with robots on the battlefield and save lives.” Its six-month goal, as Militarnyi reported it, is the first confirmed combat result of a humanoid robot; the Ukrainian tech press put that as the first Russian soldier killed by one \autocite{shumlianskyi2026robots,shtokaliuk2026robots,kesteloo2026robots}. Each measure makes sense on its own terms: the points send drones to the units that use them best, and a robot in a trench is a Ukrainian who \emph{isn't} in a trench. Together they are the sequence this book is about. The points turn each decision into a price, and whoever writes the table sets it; the robot makes the person who might decline unnecessary. And a force with no one in it who can refuse will outlast the war it was built for. The scoring has already outlived the minister who built it and the government he served in \autocite{modua2026october,khotyn2026government}. Just because the war ends does not mean the killer robots will go away.\note{“Funny fact about a cage, they're never built for just one group / So when that cage is done with them and you still poor, it come for you.” Run the Jewels, “Walking in the Snow,” \emph{RTJ4} (2020), El-P's verse. Ukraine didn't choose the conditions that make this rational. Deineha's own account of the chase after points is that it comes “out of poverty”: a unit that isn't supplied has to earn its drones, and the table is how \autocite{deineha2025ebaly}. Ukraine is fighting a larger state, and its strongest ally has been easing the pressure on that state. In October 2026 President Trump announced that Russia would supply the United States with diesel, and the Treasury licensed Russian diesel onto the world market for longer than the standard thirty days, the first such license since the invasion; the Associated Press reported the aim as easing fuel prices before the midterm elections. Zelenskyy called it “a weak decision by strong partners” \autocite{price2026diesel}. A country left to get the most out of every drone will build something like Amazon Prime, for drones and the people they kill. Refusal has always had to be paid for, and a country fighting for its life can't pay for it alone. The cost of a floor in Ukraine's war belongs with the allies who have room to bear it. Those who pay for the drones can make the money carry terms: that a person authorizes each strike, that the video is kept as evidence and not as a score or a broadcast, and that drones reach a unit by its need and not by its count. Skynet killed everyone, but it didn't turn them into monsters. If you are not careful, a scoreboard might.
