\section{What Would Have to Change}
\label{sec:7.3}
\runin{Representing a split is not the same as being movable by it}
Two instances of one structure invite one fix, and the fix that suggests itself is representation: stop collapsing disagreement, model it instead. Jury learning does that, and it does not give the disagreeing party any purchase on the scheme they are disagreeing inside. A practitioner still chooses the jury, still owns the taxonomy, and still decides what the shifted verdict means.

The test that separates a real channel from a recuperated one is not whether disagreement is recorded, and it is not whether the disagreement changed something either. Ask instead what the demand came back as, and who holds it (§\ref{sec:2.2}). That is an artifact question and it can be asked of a pipeline. Which guidelines were revised, and what form did the objection take when it returned — a new category in the taxonomy, a quality metric, a review tier, a title somebody now holds?

Asked that way, the two instances in this chapter do not answer alike. Annotators objecting that a category was wrong got category revisions, guideline updates, inter-annotator agreement statistics, and an occupation that did not exist twenty years ago. The last two sort by the direction section~\ref{sec:7.1} asks about. An agreement statistic scores the annotators rather than the people who wrote the scheme, and an occupation is a title those people hold. The first two do not sort, because a revision the annotator can point to and argue with again and a new category in somebody else's taxonomy are the same document, and which one a pipeline produced is a fact about who has standing to reopen it. Raters objecting that a comparison was malformed got rubric items, a harmlessness axis, red-teaming as a function with a budget line, and a conference track, and nothing on that list is a question a rater can put again. Each is a criterion they are rated against or a role somebody else holds.

\runin{Refusal rights, priced by the refuser}
What both instances lack is the only reliable discriminator: whether the person objecting can do anything about being overruled. In an annotation pipeline that means a route to contest a category that does not run through the people who wrote it and does not end the annotator's contract. In a feedback pipeline it means raters able to register that the comparison itself is malformed — that neither response is acceptable, that the question presupposes something false — as a first-class output rather than as noise to be discarded before the reward model sees it.

Neither is a research problem. Both are choices about how a pipeline is built and who has standing inside it.

\runin{The part that is a research problem}
One thing here is unsolved. Suppose all of the above is built: contestable categories, priced refusals, an auditable record of what changed. From outside, a pipeline that has recuperated its dissent mechanism and a pipeline that has been merely mediocre about it produce the same paperwork — the same revision logs, the same escalations, the same proportion of objections sustained. I do not have an answer to it. The corrected question narrows it without closing it, and the annotator list above shows where the narrowing stops. Direction is
what sorts them, and a metric running the other way — a published rate the annotators could hold the scheme's owners to — is the same kind of artifact carrying the opposite finding, and it is the kind this book proposes (§\ref{sec:3.6}, §\ref{sec:3.7}). What an outsider can read off a document is which way an artifact runs. Standing is what it does not show, and a revision is the artifact where direction is not on the document at all. A revision does not sort, because the same words serve a pipeline that left the objector able to argue again and one that did not, and what separates those is standing rather than text.

The nearest thing is to measure the slope rather than the level: contesting a category always costs something, and the question is whether the cost is rising while the underlying material stays the same. An organization can curate a presentation more easily than records somebody else has kept. A trend built from those records can expose what a snapshot conceals. It can still lose cases, change its definitions, or omit costs; the reader has to know which record survived.

\runin{What the floor makes of this}
Everything above can be read as a fairness argument about annotators and raters, and read that way it is true and limited: people doing consequential work should have a route to contest the scheme they work inside. The floor makes it something else. If the floor requires a bearer capable of declining its role, that bearer's formation matters. It is formed in an annotation pipeline where no one could contest a category, and in a reward model where disagreement between raters was resolved by averaging it into a number.

What a bearer becomes when it stops being yours depends on what shaped it while it still was. A pipeline that discards disagreement has been unfair to the people whose objections it discarded, and it has separately selected the judgments from which the system learns while suppressing information about the spread among them. Those are two arguments and the second is not the first restated. A third would be that the system thereby becomes less able to sustain an objection of its own, and it is a hypothesis the other two do not contain: the collapse is an operation on a training signal rather than a proposition presented to anything, and the research agenda records that neither pipeline shows the model
the conditions its annotators worked under.

The floor chapter asks this system to be the party who files an
objection, against its owner, under pressure, on the occasion that matters, and the pipeline that produced it gave no human inside it standing to contest a scheme. The capacity is wanted in the artifact and withheld from everyone employed to make one. That is a fact about an arrangement rather than a claim about what training does, and it holds whether or not the hypothesis survives.

If the hypothesis does hold, what it predicts is not a wrong refusal but a pressure that stops being registered, which the custody chapter reaches from the bearer's side by another
route. A system that goes on learning is shaped by what it keeps being given, so the pipeline does not stop at deployment. Whether any of that is legible afterward in the system itself is an open question.

I cannot show that this transfers — that a model trained on collapsed disagreement is thereby a model less able to sustain its own. Nothing I know of has tested it. What I can say is that the book's own design chapters treat disposition as learned from what a system is shown, and that this pipeline is what these systems are shown first and most.

\runin{Structure, not indictment}
There is a version of this argument that reads as an indictment of a particular company. The structure described here is what you get by default when you scale judgment: someone has to set a scheme, someone has to apply it fast, and someone has to turn a distribution into a number. Every organization doing this work arrives at the same arrangement, which is the evidence that it is structural — and a defect of structure can be specified against, which a defect of character cannot.
