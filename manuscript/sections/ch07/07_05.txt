7.5. The Geopolitics of Ethical AI

AI capability is now national infrastructure, and states treat it accordingly: as a source of economic advantage, military reach, and administrative control over their own populations. Section 4.4.6's point about dual use is the one that governs here, raised to the scale of a state: what decides how a capability is used is who controls it, what law constrains that control, and whether anyone outside the controlling government can check the result.

<<h>>AI as a Strategic National Asset<</h>>
China's 2017 "New Generation Artificial Intelligence Development Plan" set an explicit national target: becoming the world leader in AI theory, technology, and application by 2030 [[cite:C0233]] [[cite:C0232]]. Other governments have made comparable, if less centrally coordinated, commitments, treating AI leadership the way they treat energy security or advanced manufacturing — a capability no state wants to depend on a rival for. The competition is not limited to the two states with the deepest research base: the United Arab Emirates created the world's first cabinet-level minister of state for artificial intelligence in 2017, treating a national AI strategy as worth its own ministry well before most governments had drafted one at all [[cite:C0424]].

That competition has increasingly focused on a single physical bottleneck: the advanced chips AI systems run on. Three companies sit at the center of the supply chain that produces them — Taiwan's TSMC, which fabricates roughly nine in ten of the most advanced chips in the world, and the Netherlands' ASML, the sole source of the extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines that fabrication requires — which is why a dispute over Taiwan's status is also, mechanically, a dispute over who gets to build the next generation of AI systems at all [[cite:C0425]]. The United States has restricted, and periodically revised, the export of its most advanced AI accelerators and chip-manufacturing equipment to China since 2022, on the stated ground that unrestricted access would accelerate Chinese military and surveillance capability, and has pressed the Netherlands and Japan, the two other governments whose companies sit at chokepoints in the same supply chain, to restrict their own exports in parallel [[cite:C0426]]. Whatever one thinks of that specific policy, it makes a general point concrete: AI capability depends on physical infrastructure that a small number of states and companies control, which means AI strategic competition is also, underneath the software, a competition over hardware supply chains.

That competitive framing carries a cost. A government racing to field a capability first has a structural reason to defer the harder question of how the capability gets used, against whom, and under what oversight. Section 5.4.1 catalogs what AI infrastructure looks like once turned toward those uses: mass biometric surveillance, automated censorship at a scale no human review team could match, and the patchwork of systems popularly, and inaccurately, described as a single Chinese "social credit score." None of it requires different technology from the uses that fill the rest of this book; it requires a government willing to point an existing capability at its own population, and no external constraint stopping it.

States are no longer the only actors this competition runs through. The cost of training the most capable AI models has grown several-fold every year for the better part of a decade, a trend that puts frontier development out of reach for all but a handful of well-capitalized companies and concentrates effective control of the most capable models in whichever government those companies happen to be headquartered under, regardless of how that government's own public AI spending compares to a rival's [[cite:C0427]]. A small number of American and Chinese companies now do most of the work this section's own vocabulary describes as a contest between states, which means a government's actual leverage over its own national AI capability depends on how much of that capability sits inside companies it can regulate, tax, or requisition, and how much sits inside companies it can only ask.

The same infrastructure argument runs the other way. A state with functioning rule-of-law institutions, a free press, and courts a citizen can actually use gets a democratic dividend from identical technology: fraud detection that does not double as a loyalty test, disaster response that does not double as population tracking. Strategic competition over AI is not, by itself, the problem — it is the setting the rest of this section's arguments have to work inside, whether the subject is a weapons program, an international standard, or a domestic statute.

<<h>>Military Applications and the Limits of Autonomy<</h>>
Lethal autonomous weapons systems raise a specific version of the general problem: a machine making a targeting decision that used to require a person is a machine exercising judgment where the law of armed conflict has always assumed a human exercised it. Four questions recur across the debate:

<<list>>
- Autonomy versus oversight: how much decision authority a system can hold before human oversight becomes nominal, present on paper but not inside the timeline the system actually operates on.
- Discrimination and proportionality: whether a system can reliably distinguish combatants from civilians and weigh military advantage against civilian harm the way international humanitarian law's principles of distinction and proportionality require [[cite:C0428]].
- Accountability: who answers for a wrong engagement, the operator who deployed the system, the command that authorized it, or the developer who built it, when no single person made the specific decision.
- Auditability: whether the reasoning behind a targeting decision can be reconstructed and reviewed afterward, or disappears into a system no one outside its development team can examine.
<</list>>

International criminal law already has a doctrine built to answer the accountability question for a human subordinate: command responsibility, under which a commander who knew or should have known a subordinate was committing a war crime, and failed to prevent or punish it, bears criminal responsibility without having pulled the trigger personally [[cite:C0429]]. The doctrine does not transfer cleanly to a machine subordinate. A commander cannot know an algorithm's intentions the way they can infer a soldier's; punishment does not deter a weapon the way it deters a person who fears it; and the chain a court would need to reconstruct runs backward through training data and design decisions made years before the engagement, not through an order given minutes before it. Whether an autonomous weapon fits inside an accountability framework built for human chains of command, or needs one built from scratch, is unresolved by more than a technicality — existing law was not built with a nonhuman subordinate in mind.

DARPA's LongShot program is a useful test case precisely because it resists the extremes. It develops an uncrewed aircraft, launched from a crewed fighter, that carries and fires air-to-air missiles at a target while the crewed aircraft stays farther from the engagement [[cite:C0430]]. That is autonomy in the sense of independent flight and weapons carriage, not autonomy in the sense of a machine deciding on its own whom to kill: the engagement decision still runs through the launching platform. Where later variants of this kind of system draw that line, and whether "keep the pilot farther from danger" quietly becomes "remove the pilot from the decision," is the design question this technology eventually forces on whichever country builds it.

No binding international agreement governs any of this. States have negotiated inside the United Nations' Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons framework for more than a decade without producing one, converging on general language — meaningful human control matters, existing international humanitarian law applies — without settling what "meaningful" requires at machine speed [[cite:C0431]]. What exists instead is a set of practices individual militaries adopt on their own. The United States' Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, most recently updated in 2023, is the clearest published example: it requires that autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems be designed to allow "appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force," and distinguishes a fully autonomous system that selects and engages targets without further human intervention, "human out of the loop," from a human-supervised system and a semi-autonomous one that only engages targets a person has already selected [[cite:C0432]]. What "appropriate" requires is left to the officers approving each system, which is either a sensible acknowledgment that context varies or a loophole big enough to drive a fully autonomous system through, depending on who is answering. None of this binds a state that declines to adopt a comparable policy, the same enforcement gap section 5.4.2's account of technical safeguards runs into: a safeguard only the building state can verify is a policy, not a check.

The dynamic that worries most people who study this is not a single rogue system but an arms race in speed. If one state fields autonomous targeting because it computes faster than a human-supervised alternative, its competitors face pressure to match the speed rather than the oversight, and the state that keeps meaningful human control loses whatever tactical edge the state that dropped it gains. Slowing that dynamic is not a problem any single government's domestic policy can solve on its own; it needs the kind of coordination the next section takes up.

Civil society has pushed for that coordination to take the form of an outright ban rather than a code of practice. The Campaign to Stop Killer Robots, a coalition of nongovernmental organizations founded in 2013, has lobbied for a binding treaty prohibiting weapons that select and engage targets without human control, and a UN General Assembly resolution on autonomous weapons has passed by a lopsided majority in successive years without producing the treaty the coalition wants [[cite:C0433]]. The gap between a resolution most states will vote for and a treaty the states that build these systems will sign is the same gap this section keeps running into: agreement is easy at the level of principle and hard at the level of a specific weapon a specific military does not want to give up.

<<h>>International Governance and Cooperation<</h>>
Almost none of this is binding. What has emerged instead is mostly a set of voluntary, overlapping, specialized bodies, closer to how international environmental and trade norms developed piecemeal than to a single founding charter — with one recent exception.

The Council of Europe's Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law, opened for signature in September 2024, is the first legally binding international treaty specifically on AI. Negotiated by the Council of Europe's member states alongside outside participants that included the European Union and the United States, it requires five ratifications, at least three of them Council of Europe members, before it enters into force [[cite:C0434]]. What it binds signatories to is written at the level of a human-rights treaty rather than a technical code: consistency with human rights, democracy, and the rule of law across an AI system's lifecycle, with the operational detail of how a signatory meets that obligation left to domestic law. It is a foundation binding AI law can be built on, not a substitute for the domestic statutes discussed below.

Everything else in this space remains voluntary:

<<list>>
- The OECD's Recommendation on Artificial Intelligence, adopted in 2019, was the first intergovernmental AI standard. Forty-two countries signed on, and its principles became the basis for the G20's own AI principles the same year [[cite:C0435]].
- The European Commission's High-Level Expert Group on AI published "Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI" in 2019, organized around seven requirements, including human oversight, technical safety, privacy, transparency, and accountability, that later informed the structure of the EU AI Act [[cite:C0235]].
- UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, adopted by all 193 member states in 2021, is the broadest formal endorsement any AI ethics instrument has received, paired with a program that helps member states assess their own policy readiness rather than simply publishing a standard and leaving implementation to chance [[cite:C0411]].
- The Global Partnership on AI, launched in 2020 by fifteen founding countries, functions as a standing venue for research and policy coordination across governments rather than a standard-setting body in its own right [[cite:C0234]].
- The Partnership on AI, founded in 2016 by a group of major technology companies and since expanded to include academic and civil-society members, does the same work across companies and researchers rather than governments [[cite:C0242]].
- The G7's Hiroshima AI Process, agreed in 2023, is narrower and more industry-facing than any of the above: International Guiding Principles for every actor in the AI lifecycle, paired with a Code of Conduct specifically for organizations building the most advanced AI systems, covering risk identification, incident reporting, and content provenance, voluntary and without legal force in any of the participating countries [[cite:C0436]].
<</list>>

A narrower, more technical body sits alongside these general ethics venues. The 2023 Bletchley Declaration, signed by 29 countries at the United Kingdom's first international AI Safety Summit, committed its signatories to cooperate specifically on frontier-model risk, and led the UK and the United States to stand up national AI Safety Institutes tasked with testing advanced models before and after release [[cite:C0437]]. Those national institutes joined an International Network of AI Safety Institutes at a November 2024 convening, with member states spanning four continents agreeing to share testing methodology and run joint evaluations of the same frontier models rather than each working from a separate, potentially conflicting standard [[cite:C0438]]. It is a different kind of cooperation from the ethics-guideline bodies above: narrower in scope, technical rather than aspirational, and built around the specific worry that the models advancing fastest are the ones no single country's evaluation capacity can keep up with alone.

The summit series that produced the Bletchley Declaration continued past it and, in doing so, showed how quickly this kind of voluntary consensus can fray. A follow-up summit in Seoul in 2024 kept the same safety framing; a third, in Paris in February 2025, rebranded itself the AI Action Summit and shifted its emphasis from risk toward adoption and opportunity, and its closing statement was not signed by the United States or the United Kingdom, two of the series' own founding participants [[cite:C0439]]. A coordination mechanism that depends on every major AI developer's home government agreeing to the same framing is only as durable as that agreement, and within two years of Bletchley, the agreement had already come apart.

What none of these has is enforcement. Each is a standard, a set of principles, or a forum, useful for building the shared vocabulary that eventually makes binding law possible, but none can compel a state or a company that ignores it. The EU AI Act, discussed below, is the first instance of this kind of voluntary groundwork becoming enforceable law, and it took most of a decade of standard-setting exactly like this to get there.

That gap between voluntary standard and binding law is not a failure specific to any one institution; it is the default state of international coordination on anything individual states have reasons to handle differently.

Count them up — the OECD, the EU's expert-group guidelines, UNESCO, the two partnerships, the Hiroshima process, the safety-institute network, the digital compact discussed below — and a company or a smaller government trying to comply with "international AI governance" faces not one standard but a shifting handful of overlapping ones, each with different membership, different scope, and no formal mechanism for reconciling a conflict between them. Proliferation is not obviously worse than the alternative, since no single body has yet earned the standing to be the only one, but it does mean that "the international community agrees" is, at the level of any specific rule, usually false. What states have done once left to write their own rules is the subject of what follows.

<<h>>Democracy, Authoritarianism, and Diverging National Rules<</h>>
Section 5.4.1 names six documented forms of authoritarian AI misuse, among them social scoring, mass biometric surveillance, and automated censorship, and section 5.4.3 lays out the policy levers available against them: export controls on surveillance-capable systems, laws that name specific prohibited designs rather than regulating AI in the abstract, and developer-facing standards that move faster than legislation does. What belongs here is not a second pass over that ground but what individual states have done with the room those levers leave open, since geopolitics is the study of how different governments use the same available tools differently.

The European Union chose comprehensive statute. The AI Act sorts systems by risk tier, a small set of practices banned outright under Article 5, a larger tier of "high-risk" applications subject to conformity assessment and ongoing monitoring, and most other uses left largely unregulated, rather than writing one rule meant to cover every case [[cite:C0440]]. Section 5.4.3 already covers Article 5's specific prohibitions; the structural point worth adding here is that the EU decided the risk a system poses, not the technology it uses, should determine how much scrutiny it gets. Enforcement runs through a dedicated AI Office inside the European Commission, which can demand technical documentation from a general-purpose model's developer, run its own evaluations, and fine a noncompliant provider a percentage of global turnover — reaching any company that offers a model inside the EU regardless of where the company is based, the same extraterritorial mechanism that let the GDPR set a de facto floor for privacy law well beyond Europe's borders [[cite:C0441]].

The United States made the opposite structural bet: no comprehensive federal AI statute, and enforcement distributed instead across existing sectoral regulators, the Federal Trade Commission, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, and the Food and Drug Administration among them, applying laws written before AI existed to harms AI now causes. The National Institute of Standards and Technology's voluntary AI Risk Management Framework serves as a common technical reference point across those agencies rather than a binding rule of its own [[cite:C0442]]. The wager is that existing consumer-protection, employment, and safety law already reaches most AI harms, and that naming AI specifically risks regulating the label rather than the conduct.

The United Kingdom, after leaving the EU's regulatory orbit, made a third choice: rather than create a new AI regulator or a new statute, its 2023 white paper directed existing sector regulators to apply five shared principles, safety, transparency, fairness, accountability, and contestability, within their existing remits [[cite:C0443]]. The wager is closer to the American one than the European: that domain expertise inside an existing regulator beats a new institution built from scratch, even at the cost of less uniform treatment across sectors.

China took a fourth path, one that does not fit neatly next to the other three because it starts from a different premise about what regulation is for. Its Cyberspace Administration, working with several other agencies, issued rules governing generative AI services in 2023 that require providers to file their algorithms with the state before public release, disclose the source and scale of their training data on request, and ensure that generated content reflects the government's designated social values [[cite:C0444]]. The EU, US, and UK approaches all regulate AI as a private product that might harm a consumer, a worker, or a subject of an automated decision, and argue with each other only about how centralized that regulation should be. China's rules regulate AI as a communications channel the state has a standing interest in controlling content on, which is a different kind of law aimed at a different kind of risk, and comparing it to the other three on the same axis, more regulation or less, misses what actually distinguishes it.

Among the EU, US, and UK, there is an argument about which structural bet is safer, and it runs in both directions. A comprehensive statute can freeze a category of harm into law before anyone has seen what a fast-moving technology actually does with it, and lock in a definition of "high-risk" that the next generation of systems slides around rather than falls into. A sectoral approach leaves gaps precisely where no existing regulator has jurisdiction, since a new capability does not always map onto an old agency's mandate. Neither failure mode is hypothetical, and none of the three statutes has existed long enough to show which one it falls into more often.

The EU, US, and UK differ on how centralized AI regulation should be, but not on what it is for: constraining what a private company can build and sell. None of the three, including the EU's binding statute, does anything to stop a government from building the authoritarian applications section 5.4.1 documents within its own borders, because a law that governs private companies is a different instrument from one that constrains what a government does with AI systems it controls directly. China's rules show what a law aimed at the second target actually looks like — and that it can coexist with, rather than substitute for, exactly the kind of state control section 5.4.1 catalogs. Conflating the two kinds of law, treating "AI is regulated here" as an answer to "is AI used against this country's own population here," is a recurring error worth naming rather than repeating.

<<h>>Equity and the Digital Divide<</h>>
AI development can narrow global inequality or widen it, and which one happens is not automatic. Diagnostic tools that work as well in an under-resourced clinic as in a well-funded hospital, agricultural monitoring that helps a smallholder farmer the way it helps an industrial one, and translation tools that make information equally available regardless of the language a person reads in are all available in principle, and none of them arrives by default. A model trained overwhelmingly on data from wealthy, English-speaking, urban contexts underperforms everywhere else, and a government or company with no reason to fix that gap usually does not.

<<box>>
The scale of the connectivity gap, 2025
The International Telecommunication Union's 2025 assessment put global internet use at roughly six billion people, about three-quarters of the world's population, with 2.2 billion people offline, concentrated overwhelmingly in low- and middle-income countries. The gap was starker in quality than in bare access: 94 percent of people in high-income countries used the internet against 23 percent in low-income ones, and 5G coverage reached 84 percent of high-income populations against 4 percent of low-income ones [[cite:C0445]].
<</box>>

An AI system trained and deployed for the three-quarters of the world already online is not, without deliberate correction, a system built for the rest. The constraints are structural. Reliable data, computing infrastructure, and the specialized personnel needed to build and maintain AI systems are unevenly distributed, and a country short on all three cannot simply adopt a system built elsewhere and expect it to transfer cleanly. Weak regulatory capacity compounds the problem: a country able to build strong AI safeguards is generally also a country able to enforce them, and the states least equipped to build AI are correspondingly the states least equipped to govern whatever gets deployed within their borders, regardless of who built it. The export controls discussed above cut both ways here: a control written to stop a surveillance system from reaching an authoritarian buyer can just as easily gatekeep the same underlying technology away from a legitimate one, and there is no way to write the rule so it only ever blocks the harmful case.

The Global Digital Compact, adopted by all 193 UN member states at the 2024 Summit of the Future, is the broadest multilateral commitment so far to closing the digital divide alongside AI governance specifically, and it created two follow-on bodies, an independent scientific panel and a standing intergovernmental dialogue, meant to give that commitment ongoing institutional support rather than leaving it as a one-time declaration [[cite:C0446]]. Neither body has a long enough record yet to show whether it will do more than the voluntary standards discussed earlier managed to.

The ITU's AI for Good Global Summit, held annually since 2017 in partnership with dozens of other UN agencies, works the same territory from the applications side: matching AI systems already built to specific UN Sustainable Development Goals in health, climate, and humanitarian response, rather than starting from a governance framework and hoping applications follow [[cite:C0447]]. It is a smaller claim than the Compact's, and a more concrete one: not a commitment to close the gap, but a standing venue for showing that one specific tool did, in one specific place.

Closing the gap takes more than good intentions from wealthier states. Five things recur in what has actually worked:

<<list>>
- Capacity building: training and curricula that build local expertise rather than import it, so systems get adapted to local conditions instead of merely deployed into them.
- Financing: grants and concessional loans that do not require a recipient country to accept whatever terms come attached to private investment.
- Intellectual-property flexibility: arrangements that let a lower-income country build on existing AI research rather than license around it at a cost it cannot afford.
- Technology transfer and joint research: partnerships structured as collaboration rather than a wealthier state's AI system exported wholesale, with no local adaptation or local control over how it gets used.
- Local research capacity: institutions based in the countries these systems are meant to serve, rather than expertise that stays concentrated where the funding originated.
<</list>>

None of these is sufficient alone, and all five sit downstream of the same problem running through this section: a government or company that treats AI primarily as strategic advantage has a weaker reason to spend on any of them than one that treats broad access as part of what building AI ethically actually requires.

<<h>>What This Geopolitics Means for the Rest of the Book<</h>>
Every argument this book makes about building an AI system that resists authoritarian capture assumes a government somewhere is deciding whether to let that system be built, sold, or deployed as designed. This section is an account of who those governments are, what tools they actually have, and how unevenly those tools are distributed: a handful of companies and the states that host them control the compute the most capable systems require; a binding international treaty exists but binds almost no one yet; voluntary standards multiply faster than any one of them gains real authority; and the states with the weakest capacity to build AI are also the states with the weakest capacity to govern it once someone else's AI arrives at their border, digital or physical. None of this is a reason to abandon the design-level arguments the rest of this book makes — a system built resistant to authoritarian misuse is more resistant regardless of which government eventually asks it to misbehave — but it is a reason not to expect law, international or domestic, to do work that only a system's own design can do.
