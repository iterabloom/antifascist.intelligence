7.1.3. AI's Impact on Labor Markets and Income Distribution
As AI systems progressively automate tasks once performed by people, demand for advanced technical skills — AI development, data analysis — grows alongside a shrinking need for the job types most exposed to automation. Left unmanaged, that combination widens income inequality and strains the social fabric across whichever sector it hits hardest.

Manufacturing shows what this looks like at scale. An estimated 5.7 million factory jobs disappeared in the United States between 2000 and 2010, and while economists still dispute how much of that loss traces to automation rather than trade with China, automation and changed production methods account for a substantial share of it [[cite:C0383]]. Retail is following the same path: Amazon opened its first cashier-less Go store to the public in 2018, and stores built on that model displace the specific human roles — cashier, floor sales — that retail automation targets first [[cite:C0384]].

AI development also creates work it did not destroy — research and development, data analysis, AI ethics, human-AI interaction design — but these roles typically demand training that workers displaced from low-skill positions do not have and cannot quickly acquire. The result is a widening split between high-skill and low-skill labor markets, with the low-skill side absorbing most of the displacement and most of the resulting instability.

Meeting that split takes something more direct than encouraging retraining and hoping employers fund it. Employers will only invest in continuous education for their workers at the scale this requires once they cannot afford not to, and the way to force that scenario is a federal job guarantee: government as employer of last resort, hiring anyone who applies, where they live, at a living wage, with paid on-the-job training built into the position rather than offered instead of one. Section 7.1.4 develops job-guarantee programs and the rest of the policy toolkit for workforce disruption in full; the point to establish here is narrower — that a floor under wages and training is what makes the roof possible, not a byproduct of it.

Interdisciplinary research and training funding matters alongside the job guarantee, for a more specific reason: bridging the divide between technical AI development and the fields that study its consequences produces people who can do both, which a labor market retraining displaced factory workers into six-month coding bootcamps mostly does not. I am skeptical that night classes serve this population as well as paid time during the working day would; the point of a job guarantee is to make the education part of the job, not something squeezed around it.
