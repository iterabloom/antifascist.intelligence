7.2.4. Legal and ethical implications of AI systems gaining personhood
Whether an AI system should ever hold personhood is worked out in depth elsewhere in this chapter: section 7.4.1 explains why the underlying science of sentience offers no clean threshold for law to seize on, and how a jurisdiction can set an operational one anyway; section 7.4.2 works through what personhood would actually mean case by case — who answers for a diagnostic system's fatal error, what happens to a companion system when its owner dies — and shows this is not a hypothetical debate, citing a real legislative attempt at exactly this move and the researchers who explained why it was premature. That is the substance, and this section does not re-argue it.

What belongs here, in the policy-frameworks part of the book, is narrower: who decides, and when. Every version of this question that has actually reached a legislature so far has arrived as a response to pressure — a liability dispute, a public controversy — rather than from a standing body asking it in advance. Section 7.4.1's interdisciplinary review body only does the work it is designed for if some jurisdiction creates it before a forcing case shows up. That is a policy choice, not a doctrinal one: a national AI strategy that names an agency, gives it a mandate, and sets a trigger for revisiting the question — one of the options section 7.4.2 already lays out — decides this in advance rather than under the pressure of whichever incident forces the issue first.
