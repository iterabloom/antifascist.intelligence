\chapter{The Recuperation of Dissent}
\label{sec:7}
The structure this book asks a machine to detect in institutions is already built into the process that makes the machine.

Michel Foucault, prefacing the first volume of the work whose second volume supplies this book's micro-fascisms, called that book an introduction to the non-fascist life, and what he said it offered was an art of living counter to all forms of fascism, whether already present or impending \autocite{foucault1977preface}. An art of living is not a position somebody takes. It is practiced, daily, on oneself, and Foucault put the adversary inside the practitioner: the fascism in us all, in our heads and in our everyday behavior, the fascism that causes us to love power and to desire the very thing that dominates and exploits us. His question was how one keeps from being fascist, especially when one believes oneself to be on the other side.

An instrument its holder never turns around is not an instrument. The reading is an accusation waiting to happen, available to anyone who wants a technical-sounding case that an opponent is operating fascistically. The discipline against that is not a procedure and cannot be one. It is running the reading on yourself first, on the machinery you are standing on, before running it on anybody else — and this chapter is where the book does it.

\runin{Where the reading is run}
This book defines fascism as a structure rather than a costume, and the first of its four features is recuperation: an institution metabolizes disagreement rather than suppressing it, and the tell is where what the mechanism carries stops. The artifact that feature asks for is not whether the channel moved anything. It is what the demand came back as, and this chapter's own instances are re-read against that question, having first been measured against the one a recuperating institution passes.

The same structure is in the machinery that produces the systems themselves — twice in the pipeline that trains them, and a third time in the discourse that examines them — and none of the three was designed by anyone who thought they were building a political arrangement.

The first is at the label. A training corpus is annotated by people working at speed under a quota, with no channel for saying that the category they have been given is the wrong category. The second is at the reward. A model is tuned on human feedback collected as pairwise comparisons, and the compression that turns those comparisons into a training signal keeps their direction while discarding whose they were, whether they persisted, and whether the minority among them held a position or made a mistake. In both, disagreement is solicited at scale, deliberately, and put to use. What neither mechanism can carry is a judgment about itself: the annotator cannot contest the category, and the rater cannot contest the question. The consultation is real, the participation is real, and the frame is fixed with respect to what the participants say.

Three things follow.

The first is that this is not a criticism of anyone's motives. Nobody built either arrangement in order to neutralize dissent. Both are ordinary engineering, arrived at because averaging is what you do with a distribution and a quota is what you do with a budget. That is the point about micro-fascism: the structure does its work whether or not anyone intends it, which is why looking for intent will not find it.

The second is that the structure is invisible from inside any single instance. Each looks like ordinary aggregation. The annotator sees a queue, the rater sees a pair of responses, and the mechanism is functioning as specified in both cases. Seeing it requires holding the two stages against a definition written for something else entirely.

Third, the same criteria can be applied to the systems' provenance, to the field, and to this book. The five discriminators are applied below as well.
