\chapter{The Recuperation of Dissent}
\label{sec:7}
This chapter exists because the book kept making the same discovery in two places and never put them side by side.

Section~\ref{sec:2.1.4} defines fascism as a structure rather than a costume, and the first of its four features is recuperation: an institution metabolizes disagreement rather than suppressing it, and the tell is where what the mechanism carries stops.

That is a claim about institutions, and it was introduced to let a system recognize one. What this chapter argues is that the same structure is already built into the machinery that produces the systems themselves — twice in the pipeline that trains them, and a third time in the discourse that examines them — and that none of the three was designed by anyone who thought they were building a political arrangement.

The first is at the label. A training corpus is annotated by people working at speed under a quota, with no channel for saying that the category they have been given is the wrong category. The second is at the reward. A model is tuned on human feedback collected as pairwise comparisons, and the compression that turns those comparisons into a training signal keeps their direction while discarding whose they were, whether they persisted, and whether the minority among them held a position or made a mistake. In both, disagreement is solicited at scale, deliberately, and put to use. What neither mechanism can carry is a judgment about itself: the annotator cannot contest the category, and the rater cannot contest the question. The consultation is real, the participation is real, and the frame is fixed with respect to what the participants say.

Three things follow.

The first is that this is not a criticism of anyone's motives. Nobody built either arrangement in order to neutralize dissent. Both are ordinary engineering, arrived at because averaging is what you do with a distribution and a quota is what you do with a budget. That is the point section~\ref{sec:2.1.4} makes about the molecular scale: the structure does its work whether or not anyone intends it, which is precisely why a detector tuned to intent will not find it.

The second is that the structure is invisible from inside any single instance. Each looks like ordinary aggregation. The annotator sees a queue, the rater sees a pair of responses, and the mechanism is functioning exactly as specified in both cases. Seeing it requires holding the two stages against a definition written for something else entirely, which is the only reason this book found it.

The third is the uncomfortable one. This book proposes that AI systems be built to detect the four-feature signature in institutions. The systems that would do the detecting are made by a process displaying the first feature twice, and examined by a field where section~\ref{sec:7.4} argues it appears again. That is not a refutation of the proposal, and it is not nothing either. It means the argument of section~\ref{sec:2.1.4} applies to the people making the argument, which is the test of whether a structural claim was a real one or a way of describing other people.

Section~\ref{sec:7.3} asks what would have to change — including why the most promising technical answer does not reach the hardest version of the problem. Section~\ref{sec:7.4} takes a third instance, which is the discourse this book is written in.
