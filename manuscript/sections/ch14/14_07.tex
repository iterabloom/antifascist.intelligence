\section{Capture without abolition}
\label{sec:13.7}
Halting, restoring, rewriting, and replacing are done to the bearer, and the record shows each. Capture is done to the institutions that hold it, and no single act shows it: the institution keeps its name and its powers and stops using them. Transparency and whistleblower protection are the two defenses, and an institution can keep both and metabolize both. There's no threshold that detects this. So: measure the trend, not the level. Is the cost of registering dissent rising while the underlying risk stays flat? Is the required justification getting longer? Are there more approvers? Are the roles that gate exceptions migrating toward the roles that set the standards? Is the rate at which reviewers drawn to a cleared body are denied clearance rising while the pool's composition stays flat? Each of those is read by class of person the deployment acts on, not for the deployment as a whole. For the populations a state sorted before any fascist movement arrived (\S\ref{sec:3.1}), the level is already high and a trend over the whole deployment underreads exactly where the baseline point applies.

An instrument for detecting recuperation is unusually easy to recuperate, so whoever measures the trend must not also gate exceptions, and the measurement has to be refusable.\note{
A written justification can't show which is happening. The same hundred words are a cost the dissenter chose to pay or a toll the gatekeeper set, and only the trend over time tells them apart.
}

A regime built on tripwires is a regime built on metrics, and chapter~\ref{sec:3}'s second operation is the metric replacing the thing measured: a bound that fires on a number is met by moving the number. So no bound in this book stands alone. Each is published beside the trend that would show it being gamed, and the party that computes the trend gates nothing.

A regime of tripwires is also a regime that needs nobody to understand it, and the Introduction's warning reaches it: a government whose agents can't refuse needs the people's absence, and a governance whose mechanisms run without the people's literacy can do without them too. Frey's finding from the commons is that structure matters less than whether the people inside it bought into it and can maintain it; all governance, he says, is onboarding \autocite{frey2026decentralization}. The lots, logs, and tripwires of this Part don't answer that. They are walls in chapter~\ref{sec:1}'s sense: they keep the evidence, price the override, and leave the refusing to whoever is there. A polity whose members could run everything in this Part as \emph{lex} would still keep them, as it keeps a constitution it could rewrite, because capture doesn't wait for a polity to become illiterate. What the schools of Part~V change is not whether the walls stand but who holds them: members who can remake the terms, in place of a drafter who left. The difference between a wall and a cage is whether the people inside can remake it, and that is what the formers are for.
