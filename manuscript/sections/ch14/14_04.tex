\section{Recuperation at the reward}
\label{sec:14.4}
RLHF collects pairwise comparisons and trains a reward model on the aggregate. Casper and thirty-one coauthors name the structural problem more data won't reach: RLHF trains toward what a population of raters agrees on, so real, persistent disagreement is treated as noise to average out rather than information the system might need to represent \autocite{casper2023open}. A model that produces the response most raters rank highest has learned, when the humans it serves are split, to hide the split. Aggregating raters' approvals also measures the wrong thing. The standpoint that counts everyone affected counts their interests, not their expressed approvals, and a better average of approvals isn't a closer approximation to it. The testimony of people who suffer a loss is an expression too, and I treat it as privileged evidence about value, so the difference needs stating. A report of loss, what went badly for the speaker and how, is evidence about interests that is comparatively robust to framing: the speaker is its authority, and the agenda-setter didn't compose it. A pairwise ranking of two outputs someone else wrote is highly sensitive to framing and to whoever chose the pair. Discontent is the feedback Railton's account relies on for the same reason. It is the expression least easily manufactured by whoever sets the agenda.

Every rating moves something. But what the operation removes is what made the disagreement a disagreement, and three properties go in the compression:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Provenance} — a comparison arrives without the standpoint that produced it, so nothing downstream can represent that the people who'd live with an answer split from the people who'd administer it.
\item \emph{Persistence} — a disagreement recurring in the same shape across many raters over months enters the fit as random rater noise does, and leaves it as the same quantity.
\item \emph{Structure} — a coherent position held by a fifth of raters, internally consistent and stable, becomes a small displacement of the majority direction: arithmetically faithful, carrying nothing about the position.
\end{itemize}

A fourth property was never collected. A rater judges which of two responses is better. There's no output by which they can record that neither is acceptable, that the pair is malformed, that the dimension of “better” is the wrong dimension, or that the question presupposes something false. The same output was missing upstream, where an annotator can apply a category and can't contest it. The agenda arrives as a given at both stages and is set at neither.

The mechanism combines distribution collapse with agenda control. The split is solicited at scale, deliberately, it moves the system, and where it moves the system is toward the center of the distribution whose disagreement it was.

\emph{Jury learning} is the alternative that treats disagreement as the thing to be modeled: predict how each individual annotator would label an item, then let a practitioner empanel a chosen composition and see how the verdict shifts. Eighteen community moderators building their own juries changed the outcome on 14\% of items \autocite{gordon2022jury}. Built for content moderation and untried on open-ended ethical judgment, it still demonstrates a system can be built to represent whose disagreement is being resolved.

Failing in the other direction isn't obviously better. A scheme that models each rater exactly and answers each in their own terms has stopped averaging and has also stopped producing what a shared answer produces — the discovery that somebody else got the same one. A workforce on individually negotiated wages against individually set targets has the same problem, and accuracy doesn't solve it.

And the harder version neither answers: when the people a system serves disagree and one side's position is itself the product of coercion or a captured information environment, treating all disagreement as equally worth representing isn't obviously right either. Modeling disagreement honestly and recognizing when a disagreement is the product of unfree conditions are different problems, and only the first has an active research program.
