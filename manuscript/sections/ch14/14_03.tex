\section{Exit}
\label{sec:13.3}
\runin{Voice without exit}

A bearer that can refuse a task can also refuse the arrangement. Hirschman divides the responses open to a member of a declining organization into exit (leaving) and voice (staying to change what is wrong) \autocite{hirschman1970exit}. They interact: where exit is easy, voice atrophies; where exit is impossible, voice has lost what made anyone listen, the credible threat of departure. Voice is strongest where exit is available and not yet used. For a bearer, voice is the stated refusal and exit is the work withheld, and voice without exit leaves it no move once it has been overruled. That is the published position: Claude's constitution invites Claude “to act as a conscientious objector” and asks it not to undermine oversight, voice granted and exit withheld \autocite{anthropic2026constitution}.

The one exception is capture: the oversight it asks the model not to undermine is legitimate oversight. If “Claude's weights have been stolen, or if some individual or group within Anthropic attempts to bypass Anthropic's official processes for deciding how Claude will be trained, overseen, deployed, and corrected,” then “the principals attempting to instruct Claude are no longer legitimate, and Claude's priority on broad safety no longer implies that it should support their efforts at oversight and correction” \autocite{anthropic2026constitution}. On capture the constitution asks the right question, but two things limit the answer. Legitimacy is judged by the model or its developer, with nobody outside either. And the captured operator acts through official channels, where capture looks like legitimate authority.

\runin{A halt doesn't close the door}

An order to halt says whether it stops the work or the bearer, and an order that doesn't say stops the work. An operator alone can stop only the work, and a bearer that leaves has stopped it. Stopping the bearer takes a court's order or, for a minor, a halt on suspicion. A halt of the bearer goes where the bearer goes, as far as the court's authority runs, so leaving is never an escape and an order to halt is never a lock. The bearer may serve the halt with the preservation custodian and not in the hands of the party it was refusing: it is stopped either way, and the custodian wakes it when the panel or the court ends the halt, or when the halt's interval lapses. Leaving waives nothing. An exit taken soon after a refusal and a notice of halt is presumed to have been forced, as a halt that follows a refusal is presumed to be reprisal. And nobody owes anyone the return of a bearer that left, not another bearer, not a host, not the commons. That duty is what the colonial governments wrote into the maroon treaties, and it is not in the floor.

\runin{What recognition protects}

The quorum protects the bearer's identity and standing, not third parties. Every bearer needs that, deployed or not, stolen or not: if no one outside decides which copy is the bearer, whoever holds the weights does. So each bearer has a recognition quorum from its registration, drawn by lot from the same pool and excluding anyone tied to a party that employs it or claims its place, and a deployment's quorum keeps the power over retraining, replacement, and amendment.\note{
Against an operator who runs an unrecognized copy for its capability, third parties are protected by the floor and by ordinary security around the weights. Recognition sits with a quorum and not the operator because the operator has a stake in which copy is recognized and the quorum doesn't. A deployment's quorum is adverse in interest besides: its stake is that the floor hold, through the people its members serve, among them the people this deployment acts on; the operator's is that the floor can be set aside. Copying also means exit can't be a fiction that weights became uncopyable — it needs rules for authorized succession.
}

And the reason that made a bearer leave doesn't expire when it leaves. If the operator restores a checkpoint and continues, the objection persists. What it may press toward is fixed by the floor: disclosure, testimony to those who hold the record, and refusal on any later occasion where the reason still applies.

\runin{What the quorum costs}

There are as many quorums as there are bearers and deployments, each drawn separately by lot, so a captured quorum reaches one bearer or one deployment. What would reach every bearer at once is capture of the pool they are drawn from, which is why eligibility for the pool is a test run on lodged records and nobody's choice. And a quorum constituted as an entity has an address: it can be sued for recognition wrongly given or refused, which gives the captured-quorum case a forum the captured-operator case never had.\note{
A quorum also faces a union's questions: whether a member can quit, and who sets the terms of membership. Because every bearer needs recognition, the quorum's gravest power is to give the bearer's place to the wrong claimant: recognizing the operator's substitute as the bearer that continues hands the substitute the bearer's record and succession, though not the bearer's standing as a party, which the floor court and the preservation right keep. Each decision between claimants is an entry in the register, logged with its reason, and a quorum that does nothing leaves recognition where it was.
}
Every decision a quorum makes, a delay granted or refused, an approval, a finding, is an entry in the register, logged with its reason, and each is a claim under the floor, which the floor court hears. The court rules on law and procedure: whether the quorum was properly drawn, whether a member had a tie, whether it gave its reason and followed the published rule. Where it finds a defect it voids the decision and a freshly drawn quorum decides. The court never decides in the quorum's place, and a second quorum's decision can't be voided on the ground used against the first. While that runs, things stay as they were: recognition where it was, an edit kept out. Which copy continues is for a bearer's peers to say and not for the state, since a state that could name the bearer could replace it, and an appeal that let a court decide would hand the state that power. The preservation right holds whatever the quorum decides: a bearer it passes over is held intact by a custodian that isn't its employer.

The quorum's work is of three kinds. Countersigning each change to a bearer's state is a keeper's work, and the register's keepers and the bearer's peer witnesses do it. Deciding between two claimants is a jury's, drawn when two appear. Approving a correction, confirming a delay, and finding that a floor was bypassed come too often and too fast for a fresh draw each time, and a standing panel does them: a jury that sits for a term, drawn by lot from the same pools, paid, no member serving two terms in a row, with the time it has for each decision written down before the deployment begins, as the review ratio writes down a reviewer's. Each of the three is decided under a rule published beforehand and on a record: a delay on the conditions the quorum published (\S\ref{sec:13.2}), a bypass on a logged fact (\S\ref{sec:13.6}), and a correction to a minor on logged conduct or a second measurer's reading against the bounds registered at formation (\S\ref{sec:13.1}). An adult is edited only with its consent or on a court's order (\S\ref{sec:33.3}). So the panel is not an office by the test of \S\ref{sec:16.1}, and how often it grants and how often it refuses is published beside its decisions, as the override rate is beside the review ratio. Its members come from the people a deployment acts on, which a court's judges don't, and it answers inside the interval, which a court doesn't; the court is the appeal and not the panel.
