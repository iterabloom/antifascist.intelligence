\section{Where the State's Leverage Sits}
\label{sec:states-leverage}

Strategic competition over AI is exercised through physical and legal
chokepoints: advanced chips, fabrication equipment, electricity, water,
land-use permits, procurement contracts, and the jurisdiction in which
a developer is incorporated. These controls matter more to the present
argument than the contents of any national AI strategy. They determine
which institutions a state can license, subsidize, exclude, requisition,
or compel.

The same leverage that allows a government to enforce a floor can be
used to remove one. An inspection body may be independent of the
operator and still depend on a license, appropriation, or infrastructure
provider controlled by the state whose conduct it is meant to inspect.
A company may be able to resist a customer and unable to resist its home
government. The custody question therefore moves outward: not only who
controls the model, but who controls the conditions under which the
model, its operator, and its reviewer are allowed to continue operating.

That competition has increasingly focused on a single physical bottleneck: the advanced chips AI systems run on. Two companies sit at the center of the supply chain that produces them — Taiwan's TSMC, which fabricates roughly nine in ten of the most advanced chips in the world, and the Netherlands' ASML, the sole source of the extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines that fabrication requires — which is why a dispute over Taiwan's status is also, mechanically, a dispute over who gets to build the next generation of AI systems at all \autocite{miller2022chipwar}. The United States has restricted the export of its most advanced AI accelerators and chip-manufacturing equipment to China since 2022, and pressed the Netherlands and Japan, whose companies sit at the other chokepoints, to restrict theirs in parallel \autocite{bis2022implementation}.

That dependence has a second half the chip story hides. A fabricated chip has to be installed somewhere, powered, and cooled, and that requirement lands on grids, water systems, and zoning authorities inside particular jurisdictions, on a timescale measured in years. It hands a state a lever over AI capability that needs no export-control regime at all: an interconnection queue, a water allocation, a permit. A government's leverage over the systems built in its territory is wider than the export-control debate suggests, and much of it is exercised by permitting authorities that were not designed with AI in mind and do not think of themselves as regulators of it.

That competitive framing carries a cost. A government racing to field a capability first has a structural reason to defer the harder question of how the capability gets used, against whom, and under what oversight. Turned toward those uses, AI infrastructure looks like mass biometric surveillance, automated censorship at a scale no human review team could match, and the patchwork of systems popularly, and inaccurately, described as a single Chinese “social credit score.” None of it requires technology different from what is already commercially deployed; it requires a government willing to point an existing capability at its own population, and no external constraint stopping it.

States are no longer the only actors this competition runs through. The cost of training the most capable AI models has grown several-fold every year for the better part of a decade, a trend that puts frontier development out of reach for all but a handful of well-capitalized companies and concentrates effective control of the most capable models in whichever government those companies happen to be headquartered under, regardless of how that government's own public AI spending compares to a rival's \autocite{epoch2024costtrend}. A small number of American and Chinese companies now do most of the work that gets described as a contest between states, which means a government's actual leverage over its own national AI capability depends on how much of that capability sits inside companies it can regulate, tax, or requisition, and how much sits inside companies it can only ask.
