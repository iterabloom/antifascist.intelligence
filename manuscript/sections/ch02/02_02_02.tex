\subsection{Theory of Mind and Perspective-Taking in AI Systems}
\label{sec:2.2.2}
Theory of mind (ToM) is the capacity to attribute mental states — beliefs, intentions, desires, emotions, knowledge — to oneself and others, and to recognize that another's states can differ from one's own. It underlies social interaction and interpersonal understanding generally. For an AI system to approximate it, the system has to model another agent's likely beliefs and goals well enough to predict how they'll act on them, not just react to surface behavior.

Two approaches show promise: multi-agent learning, where a system builds a model of other agents' mental architecture through repeated interaction with them, and the direct integration of human social norms and values into the system's decision-making. Cognitive science, psychology, and neuroscience all supply raw material for this — datasets and theory for predicting emotional state from facial expression, vocal tone, and text, and, further out, systems that refine those inferences through ongoing social contact rather than a fixed training set.

An AI personal assistant with some working ToM might infer from vocal tone that a user is stressed and suggest a break rather than pushing forward with the task at hand. A more consequential and more contentious application is a synthetic companion for elderly users, which would need a working model of a person's emotional and physical needs sustained over months or years, not just a single interaction — and which raises the question section~\ref{sec:2.2.3} takes up directly: whether the felt-empathy register this application seems to call for is the right capacity to build, or whether it should be built for compassion instead, the way section~\ref{sec:2.2.1} argues.

None of this substitutes for the underlying social and material conditions a lonely or under-supported elderly population needs — public infrastructure, adequate caregiving capacity, family and community support. An AI companion is not a policy.
