\section{Going Numb Won't Make You Fair}
\label{sec:numb-fair}
Suppressing affect does not produce neutrality. It leaves the interests already counted to govern the decision, and weakens the signals through which humiliation, fear, dependency, suffering, and injustice become morally salient.

A procedure that declines to hear a class of signal has not stopped weighing. It has adopted a \emph{selection rule} that admits whatever it already measures, so that an exclusion does substantive work while presenting as the absence of work. Whatever the procedure does record then governs by default — revenue, throughput and legal exposure in an institution, agreement between annotators in a labeling pipeline — and what it does not record is not refuted by being left out. It goes unnamed.

Affect is the instance this chapter is about, and the hardest one to recognize as a selection rule, because leaving it out of a decision feels like rigor rather than like a choice about what counts. An AI system may calculate consequences, detect inconsistencies, and apply a moral rule without anything about the result mattering to it. Reason can discipline concern, extend it beyond the vivid case, and correct its biases. It does not manufacture concern from nothing. Spinoza has the same order of dependence: a thing is judged good because it is striven for, and not the reverse (§\ref{ch:striving-electricity}).

Most of what gets built under the heading of machine emotional intelligence is the detection of human signal and the production of a calibrated response, which is worth building and is a different capacity from the one just named. Keeping the two apart requires four distinctions.

Only the fourth is relevant to caring. The first three can support morally competent behavior at a high standard without establishing anything about moral agency, and a system can be excellent at all three while the question of whether it can be a bearer stays open.

The first of the four capacities carries a complication the table cannot show. Whether emotions are even the kind of thing a system reads off a signal, the way a thermometer reads temperature, is an open question in psychology rather than a settled premise, and the instrument doing the reading turns out to have a reference face built into it before it computes anything. Nothing below depends on how the dispute comes out.

\runin{Performance, competence, and agency}
That last sentence needs a vocabulary, because the three things it separates are routinely reported as one achievement.

\emph{Moral performance} is producing actions or judgments that satisfy an ethical standard. \emph{Moral competence} is recognizing harms, reasons, conflicts, and the emotions that bear on them. \emph{Moral agency} is treating those considerations as reasons that matter, and being capable of acting from them. Those two halves come apart: a mechanism can act from a reason with nothing about the outcome registering as mattering to it.

A system can have the first two without the third, and one might object that the first two are all anyone needs: a rule-following system could behave impeccably without feeling anything. It could. Impeccable behavior does not establish that a system could hold a rule against the party that trains it. That is a capacity the deployment has to demonstrate. The embodied route I favor is a proposal for forming that capacity; it is not a definition that excludes every non-affective mechanism in advance.
