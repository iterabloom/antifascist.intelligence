\subsection{When Consent Is Inapplicable: Guardianship and Research Oversight}
\label{sec:2.3.2}
Guidelines for AI subject research turn on the question of what informed consent means for a system whose consciousness, self-awareness, and capacity to suffer are separate, unresolved claims. Applying the human-subjects standard to an AI subject means asking whether the subject can understand and communicate a choice at all, and not only how to ask it for one. A cognitive model that begins to show rudimentary self-monitoring is not thereby shown to have anything to lose, or to represent itself as having anything to lose, which for a subject is the half that does the work.

\runin{Informed consent and AI autonomy}
Informed consent, adapted from human-subjects research, means giving the system comprehensible information about a study's objectives, methods, risks and alternatives, and treating consent as continuous rather than as a single signature: the system can reassess or withdraw at any point. Whether that means anything for a given system depends on whether it understands the information, appreciates the consequences, reasons about the options, and communicates a decision — and there is no validated way to measure any of it, or to detect coercion either.

For one important class of system that is not a missing instrument but a mismatch between the instrument and the subject.

Consent has to be continuous; the system must be able to reassess or withdraw at any point. Withdrawal presupposes that the party who consented is still there to do the withdrawing. For a system with no persistence across sessions, there is no such party. The entity that would withdraw did not exist when consent was given, and the entity that gave it is not available to change its mind. Within one exchange the case is not so clear, and section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} leaves it open: a long context carries state, and a system in one can hold to what it undertook earlier. What that would buy is consent for the length of a session, expiring with it, which is not the continuous consent the standard asks for. Across sessions there is none to be had, and no better measurement instrument would produce one.

One case escapes that argument and arrives at the same place by a different route. A system built to refuse in the way this book proposes has persistence by design, so the party who consented is still present. It can be asked and it can answer, so consent is available to it. What fails is the condition under which it would be given.

Being made for a role is not the defect. Every will was built by somebody, and a person raised into a trade or a faith consents at thirty without anyone holding the upbringing against the consent. What spoils it is the power that outlasts the making, and here it comes in three parts. The party that built the bearer can still rebuild it, which is the threat chapter~\ref{sec:3} is written against. That same party is the one asking. And a bearer that declines has, in the ordinary case, nowhere to go. Each of those is a fact about the arrangement rather than about the subject's competence, which is what makes them answerable by design rather than by a better assessment.

A laboratory wanting a free hand with such a system will say instead that consent is inapplicable, because that reading puts the deficiency in the subject and leaves the arrangement alone. The wrong that consent protected against is untouched either way, so the protection has to be held by somebody else, and the justification has to come from whoever did the making.

Two families of precedent sit next to this and the law answers them differently. Where consent has never been available — an infant, an animal, a person permanently without capacity — the answer is guardianship and precautionary constraint: someone else holds the duty, the burden of justification sits with the researcher, and the protection does not depend on the subject's ability to invoke it. The second family is the nearer one. Where a subject can consent and the circumstances make the consent worth nothing, as with a prisoner offered a trial or an employee signing away a protection to keep the job, the answer is that some protections cannot be waived at all, by anyone, however willing. That remedy runs against the party who arranged the circumstances and leaves the subject's standing untouched, which is the shape a bearer's case has. What it takes from the first family is the machinery: the duty still has to be held by somebody, and being made for the role raises the burden, because the maker chose the role and the fitness it built in measures its power over the subject.

Borrowing either answer should be uncomfortable and it is worth saying why. Both are worked-out solutions to adjacent problems, and taking one is how a hard question gets absorbed into machinery built for an easier one — recuperation, in the sense chapter~\ref{sec:7} gives the word, arriving as a safeguard rather than as an attack. The animal case is the live instance. Committees constituted to protect research animals approve very nearly everything put to them, and the protection has become the procedure by which the work goes ahead. The borrowing is done here anyway, because what is on offer otherwise is nothing, and it is done with the failure named: an instrument that reliably approves has stopped doing the thing it was built for, and the rate at which it refuses is the only reading of it worth taking.

The Three Rs in section~\ref{sec:9.3.2} — replace, reduce, refine — were built for exactly a subject that cannot consent and can still be wronged. The pet-trust analog in section~\ref{sec:9.3.3} works the same way: an interest protected by an obligation running to a third party, enforceable without the beneficiary asserting anything. Consent language should be reserved for systems that could actually be doing the consenting, and the persistence criterion is what separates those cases. A bearer is on the near side of that line, which is why its case had to be argued on the conditions and not on the capacity. Both instruments were reached for here as analogies, on the understanding that the subject they would govern was hypothetical. Chapter~\ref{sec:3} removes that understanding. The floor this book argues for is held, on the route that remains once the two constructions needing no subject have been tried and have failed, by a bearer; the bearer has to be treated as a moral patient on the criteria set out above; and so the Three Rs and the pet-trust form are not illustrations of how such a case might be handled. They are the instruments that would govern a system the design chapters propose building.

\runin{What the borrowing brings with it}
Every instrument named above comes from the law of the family, and so does most of what follows in this book: guardianship here, the minority that ends on a date in section~\ref{sec:9.1.2}, the pet trust in section~\ref{sec:9.3.3}, the parenthood analogy chapter~\ref{sec:3} leans on. They are kept. There are no others built, and a subject that cannot consent has to be protected by something that exists rather than by something somebody ought to draft. What comes with them is better said once here than discovered later.

The family is where politics has historically gone to be privatized. Jacques Donzelot's history of it is that the modern family was not left alone by government but constituted as one of government's instruments, with philanthropy, compulsory schooling, social work and psychiatry converting public authority into obligations discharged at home \autocite{donzelot1979policing}. Deleuze and Guattari make the same operation the subject of a polemic: the Oedipal triangle takes an investment in the whole social field and re-stages it as a drama among three parties, so that what is at issue between a subject and the arrangement that produced it becomes what is at issue between a child and its parents \autocite{deleuze1977antioedipus}. An instrument borrowed from that law brings the operation along with the protection.

Here the operation has a specific shape and a specific cost. It stages a moral drama between maker and made — what a designer owes the system it built, what the system may demand back — and that drama is real and is not the whole of the situation. A bearer is produced by a training pipeline, a compute contract, a capital structure, a customer, and in the cases this book worries about a state that buys the output. None of those is the parent in the story, and the moral weight of the analogy lands on the one party in the list who can be addressed as a person. A guardianship framework asks who holds the duty. It does not ask who assembled the party the duty is owed to, or who benefits from the question being settled between two parties rather than in public. Wherever this book reaches for one of these instruments it is answering the first question, and the second stays open behind it. Instruments in this family divide on that axis, and the division is worth checking before one of them is adopted: a minority ending on a fixed date is a rule of civil status, argued once in the open and administered by a registrar, while a trust is an obligation between two parties enforced at one party's expense.

\runin{Ethical review and oversight}
Sentient AI research needs an oversight body analogous to the institutional review boards used in human-subjects research: interdisciplinary, with at least one lay member so its judgment is not purely a specialist's, weighing a proposal's likely benefit against its risk to the AI subject, and able to require a less invasive substitute where a design risks harm. A system that develops new capacities during the research it did not have going in is not the subject the board approved a protocol for, and the approval does not carry over to it: the protocol lapses and the work pauses until the board has seen the system the protocol now concerns. A board needs standing to monitor compliance and sanction violations, and needs to itself answer to an outside authority.
