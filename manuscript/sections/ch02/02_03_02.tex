\subsection{Affective Computing: AI Systems that Comprehend and Respond to Emotions}
\label{sec:2.3.2}
Affective computing is the applied half of what section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} leaves theoretically unsettled: systems that read and respond to emotional signal, useful or not regardless of which account of what an emotion is turns out to be right. The signal is taken from three places — the face, the voice, and the body. Facial expression analysis is a harder problem than the face verification deployed at scale, which only decides whether two images are the same person, and accuracy claims that borrow the reputation of the second for the first should be read with that in mind \autocite{taigman2014deepface}. Physiological sensing, which infers from heart rate and skin conductance, is the only one of the three the subject cannot pose for.

Four difficulties travel with the whole field, and section~\ref{sec:9.2.1} argues that the first is worse than a difficulty. Emotional expression varies by culture and by person, so a system trained on one population misreads another confidently. Whatever bias is in the training data is in the classifier. Consent to emotional inference is not obtained by a term of service. And a system that models emotion well enough to respond to it models it well enough to exploit it, which is the case where the capability and the misuse are one capability.
