\subsection{Affective Computing: AI Systems that Comprehend and Respond to Emotions}
\label{sec:2.3.2}
Affective computing is the applied half of what section 2.3.1 leaves theoretically unsettled: systems that read and respond to emotional signal, useful or not regardless of which account of what an emotion is turns out to be right. The signal is taken from three places — the face, the voice, and the body. Facial expression analysis is a harder problem than the face verification deployed at scale, which only decides whether two images are the same person, and accuracy claims that borrow the reputation of the second for the first should be read with that in mind \autocite{taigman2014deepface}. Voice analysis works from pitch, tone, pace and volume. Physiological sensing infers from heart rate and skin conductance, which is the only one of the three the subject cannot pose for.

\begin{esbox}
\boxtitle{A snapshot, early 2020s}
The deployed examples that shaped this literature were commercial products of a particular moment: real-time coaching for call-center agents from voice analysis \autocite{matheson2016watchyourtone}, wrist-worn bio-sensing for research and clinical use \autocite{empatica2015e4}, engagement scoring from facial signal in classrooms \autocite{affectiva2015emotionservice}, and cognitive-behavioral chatbots for anxiety and low mood \autocite{fitzpatrick2017delivering}. They are named here as the record of what was built and tried, not as a description of a current market.

\end{esbox}
Four difficulties travel with the whole field, and section 9.2.1 argues that the first of them is worse than a difficulty. Emotional expression varies by culture and by person, so a system trained on one population misreads another confidently. Whatever bias is in the training data is in the classifier, with the ordinary downstream consequences for whoever is being scored. Consent to emotional inference is not obtained by a term of service. And a system that models emotion well enough to respond to it models it well enough to exploit it, which is the case where the capability and the misuse are one capability.
