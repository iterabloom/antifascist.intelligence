\subsection{What Governs an Agent, and What Matters to It}
\label{sec:2.3.2}
The typical evidence that concern is not a natural consequence of rationality: the footbridge case, where saving five requires pushing a man off a bridge, engages emotional processing that the switch case — diverting a trolley by pulling a lever — leaves quiet, and patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage endorse the push at a higher rate than people with intact emotional response \autocite{greene2001fmri,koenigs2007damage}. Those results establish that affect changes particular moral judgments, but they do not establish that affect is what makes anything matter.

Two further lines of evidence bear on whether affect enables things to matter. Neither shows what happens when affect is taken away, which is what would settle it, so what follows argues about where the burden sits rather than claiming the question closed.

\runin{The philosophical position is empirical and has been argued as such}
Moral sentimentalism — the claim that moral judgment is constitutively tied to affective response — is usually met as Hume's, and its modern form is a psychological hypothesis with evidence attached. Shaun Nichols argues for what he calls sentimental rules: normative rules and affective mechanisms make separate contributions to moral judgment, and the rules that carry the distinctive force of moral prohibition are the ones bound to an emotional response to the harm they prohibit \autocite{nichols2004sentimental}. The interesting part for a designer is the separability. Rules without the affective component do not stop being rules; they stop being the kind of thing that carries the force.

\runin{The prosocial emotions are motivational states, not readouts}
Compassion has been characterized as a distinct emotion with a specific functional profile: it arises at the perception of another's suffering and moves the agent toward the sufferer, rather than reporting the suffering to some further faculty that decides what to do \autocite{goetz2010compassion}. A training study is the mechanistic version of the same finding, with compassion practice recruiting reward and approach circuitry where empathy practice recruited shared distress \autocite{klimecki2013differential}. Guilt has the same shape pointed at one's own conduct: guilt, which construes the act as wrong, predicts reparative behavior, while shame, which construes the self as wrong, predicts withdrawal and defensiveness \autocite{tangney2007moral}. In each case the emotion is doing motivational work directly. It is not information that motivation then acts on.

\runin{Two senses of motivation}
\textit{Action-producing motivation} is whatever reliably selects one action over another and sustains the selection across conflict. On the other hand, \textit{experienced motivation} is wanting, caring, being drawn toward something, being set back when it is lost.

The first sense is not in dispute and there is an existence proof close to hand. Language models running as agents pursue objectives across long horizons, revise plans when obstructed, and hold to an instruction against conflicting local pressure. That settles the first sense and leaves the second untouched: nothing in the behavior shows whether anything matters to them while they do it. (And you cannot establish the opposite either — you cannot establish that nothing matters to them.)

\runin{Where the dispute actually is}
So it is clear that reason can produce goal-directed behavior. What is not clear is whether an outcome can \emph{matter} to the agent rather than merely govern what the agent does. Producing a verbal justification or computing a normative relation does not seem like evidence that the consideration matters to the system. The evidence above bears on the second question, and every item in it comes from humans and other animals, where something already does matter.

\runin{What a non-affective counterexample would look like}
Harry Frankfurt's second-order volitions give the shape of a candidate: an agent with desires, and with desires about which of its desires shall move it, where identification with the higher-order desire rather than the strength of the lower one settles what the agent does \autocite{frankfurt1971freedom}. The shape is all that is borrowed: Frankfurt's own account of caring puts it on the affective side, an investment that leaves the one who cares vulnerable to the object's losses and susceptible to its gains \autocite{frankfurt1982importance}. Gary Watson's version separates a valuational system from a motivational system, so that what an agent values and what an agent is moved by are different things \autocite{watson1975free}.

Build the valuational system as an explicit structure that ranks outcomes, give it authority over action selection that does not depend on any state registering as mattering, and hold that authority stable under pressure, and you would have an architecture that satisfies the first sense of motivation through what the agent values, with nothing occupying the second. Systems are now trained to consult a written specification that ranks what they should prefer, which is that architecture in outline; what none of them has is the second half of it, an authority over action that holds under pressure applied by the party who wrote the specification. That architecture is the maintained justification. Half of it is deployed and the other half has never been built, so the architecture as a whole is untested, and it is the first of the two constructions this book asks to be built before anything that can be wronged is built.

\runin{What this licenses and what it does not}
Non-affective moral motivation may well be possible, and the architecture sketched above is where to look for it. What stands in the way of checking is that the output of a system in which something matters and the output of a system that has learned what mattering sounds like are the same text. So the generalization survives in the only form it can: every agent known to have something register as mattering is an affective agent, and no design has yet demonstrated that state in a design that installed nothing answering to affective concern.

That is not a proof that non-affective moral agency is impossible. The burden I place on whoever proposes to engineer non-affective concern is a burden of demonstration: the capability chapter~\ref{sec:3} specifies, shown under pressures its builders did not write. A bearer carries the same burden. What the induction adds is a prior about which route will meet it first and nothing more; nobody is asked to show that a system feels nothing, which no instrument can show of any construction. Chapter~\ref{ch:route} takes the induction up again and reduces it twice over: no one has yet tried the route, and an absence of machine examples is weak evidence when nobody has looked; and the agents generalized from are one lineage, in which affect co-varies with every other property of biological cognition.

Two claims run through this, and which one a reader is holding changes what could settle it. One is about which route to build first, the kind of claim a measurement could overturn. The other is about what would be owed to whatever the building produces.
