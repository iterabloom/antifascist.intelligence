\subsection{Six Ethical Frameworks, and Where Each One Runs Out}
\label{sec:2.1.1}
I give each of these six frameworks with a case that defeats it; these depictions will inform the structure chapter~\ref{sec:3} proposes.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Consequentialism judges actions by their ramifications rather than their nature. It is defeated by the difficulty of forecasting long-term consequences, which puts individual rights and collective welfare into direct conflict.
  \item Deontology weighs adherence to moral rules and duties over consequences: a system built on it holds to specific obligations — e.g., respecting privacy, avoiding harm, truthful communication — regardless of outcome. Conflicts between duties defeat it, as do cases the rules simply do not cover.
  \item Virtue ethics develops moral character rather than following rules or calculating outcomes. It is defeated by the problem of translating an intangible trait like courage into a concrete action.
  \item Care ethics centers relationships and responsiveness to another's needs. It is defeated by several stakeholders' conflicting needs at once.
  \item Contractualism derives moral principles from agreements among rational individuals. It is defeated by the prior problem of reaching consensus on shared values at all.
  \item The capabilities approach asks not what a system does but what it leaves a person able to be and do. An adaptive tutor that meets a student where they are expands a capability; the same system gating what a student may study expands nothing, however well it teaches inside the gate. It is defeated by measurement: capability is harder to measure than an outcome or a rule, and a system optimizing a \emph{proxy} for capability will drift toward that proxy instead.
\end{enumerate}
The capabilities approach is what chapter~\ref{sec:12} uses to ask what a life touched by an AI system is free to become. Deontology's unconditional constraints are what the floor needs, and the objection that sinks it as a complete theory has no force against a constraint that decides a small number of things and leaves the rest to the other five.

The pluralism this section recommends is one where a deontological floor forces argument and conflict among the five other frameworks to bottom out somewhere; in other words, the floor means that some things are not up for debate.

\runin{What compiling a principle into a metric decides}
Turning any of the six into a machine learning objective means distilling a broad construct into a measurable target: in a diagnostic system, non-maleficence becomes minimizing false negatives and fairness becomes balanced accuracy across patient demographics. What defeats the translation is not vagueness in the principles, which can be stipulated away, but that the stipulation is where the ethics now lives. Someone chose that reading of non-maleficence, and chose the demographic groups accuracy is balanced across, and chose what to do when balancing them costs accuracy overall. Each choice is defensible and each is a moral decision made by a person and then compiled into something that looks like a specification. A system optimizing the result has no access to the decision and no way to reopen it, which is why section~\ref{sec:3.1}'s first way — the floor as a property of the artifact — cannot notice when the reason for a constraint has left the building.

\runin{Which value conflicts a designer may resolve by choosing}
Values assigned to a system will conflict, and most of those conflicts are ordinary design work: rank the values explicitly, keep a record of how comparable cases were resolved, and audit afterward for the gap between the principles a system was meant to implement and what it did. One conflict is not like the others, and it is sometimes misguidedly filed under “cultural difference.” The UN's 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted with forty-eight states in favor and none against; the eight that abstained were the Soviet bloc, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa — which is to say that the objection came from the governments whose treatment of their own populations the Declaration was written to name \autocite{un1948udhr}. That pattern is evidence of who finds the floor inconvenient rather than of cultural disagreement about human rights. What varies by community is how a right gets translated into local practice, and a jurisdiction refusing to extend the right at all is not a local variant to accommodate.

A system that treats every value conflict as a weighting problem has no way to represent the difference between a case where reasonable people disagree and a case where somebody wants a right withdrawn from somebody else. The first calls for the machinery above; the second is what the floor is for.
