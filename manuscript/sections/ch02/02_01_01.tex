\subsection{Six Ethical Frameworks, and Where Each One Runs Out}
\label{sec:2.1.1}
Six frameworks do work here. Each is given with the case that defeats it, because the defeats are what force the structure chapter~\ref{sec:3} proposes.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Consequentialism judges actions by their ramifications rather than their nature, and applied to AI the objective becomes optimizing human welfare. It is defeated by the difficulty of forecasting long-term consequences, which puts individual rights and collective welfare into direct conflict.
  \item Deontology weighs adherence to moral rules and duties over consequences: a system built on it holds to specific obligations — respecting privacy, avoiding harm, truthful communication — regardless of outcome. It is defeated by conflicts between duties, and by cases the rules simply do not cover.
  \item Virtue ethics develops moral character rather than following rules or calculating outcomes. It is defeated by the problem of translating an intangible trait like courage into a concrete action.
  \item Care ethics centers relationships and responsiveness to another's needs. The acting half of that, compassion, and the feeling half, empathy, are not the same capacity, and an AI system needs the first far more than the second. It is defeated by several stakeholders' conflicting needs at once.
  \item Contractualism derives moral principles from agreements among rational individuals. It is defeated by the prior problem of reaching consensus on shared values at all.
  \item The capabilities approach asks not what a system does but what it leaves a person able to be and do. An adaptive tutor that meets a student where they are expands a capability; the same system gating what a student may study expands nothing, however well it teaches inside the gate. It is defeated by measurement: capability is harder to measure than an outcome or a rule, and a system optimizing something easier will drift toward that instead.
\end{enumerate}
Two of the six are load-bearing later and the rest are equipment. The capabilities approach is what chapter~\ref{sec:12} uses to ask what a life touched by an AI system is free to become. Deontology is the one that returns: its unconditional constraints are what the floor needs, and the objection that sinks it as a complete theory has no force against a constraint that decides a small number of things and leaves the rest to the other five.

That is the pluralism this section recommends. The usual version blends frameworks case by case and hopes the blend is wiser than its parts, which leaves a system that can be argued out of anything, because for any act there is a framework in the blend that permits it. What the floor adds is that the conflict has to bottom out somewhere the arguing does not reach.

\runin{What compiling a principle into a metric decides}
Turning any of the six into a machine learning objective means distilling a broad construct into a measurable target: in a diagnostic system, non-maleficence becomes minimizing false negatives and fairness becomes balanced accuracy across patient demographics. What defeats the translation is not vagueness in the principles, which can be stipulated away, but that the stipulation is where the ethics now lives. Someone chose that reading of non-maleficence, and chose the demographic groups accuracy is balanced across, and chose what to do when balancing them costs accuracy overall. Each choice is defensible and each is a moral decision made by a person and then compiled into something that looks like a specification. A system optimizing the result has no access to the decision and no way to reopen it, which is why section~\ref{sec:3.1}'s first branch — the floor as a property of the artifact — cannot notice when the reason for a constraint has died.

\runin{Which value conflicts a designer may resolve by choosing}
Values assigned to a system will conflict, and most of those conflicts are ordinary design work: rank the values explicitly, keep a record of how comparable cases were resolved, and audit afterward for the gap between the principles a system was meant to implement and what it did. One conflict is not like the others, and it is the one usually filed under cultural difference. The UN's 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted with forty-eight states in favor and none against; the eight that abstained were the Soviet bloc, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa — which is to say that the objection came from precisely the governments whose treatment of their own populations the Declaration was written to name \autocite{un1948udhr}. That is evidence of who finds the floor inconvenient rather than of cultural disagreement about human rights. What varies by community is how a right gets translated into local practice, and a jurisdiction refusing to extend the right at all is not a local variant to accommodate.

A system that treats every value conflict as a weighting problem has no way to represent the difference between a case where reasonable people disagree and a case where somebody wants a right withdrawn from somebody else. The first calls for the machinery above; the second is what the floor is for, and a system with only the machinery will resolve it by whichever weight is larger.
