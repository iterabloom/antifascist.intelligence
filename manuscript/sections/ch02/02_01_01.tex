\subsection{Six Ethical Frameworks, and Where Each One Runs Out}
\label{sec:2.1.1}
Consequentialism judges an act by its outcomes, and runs out where the outcomes cannot be forecast; deontology judges it by duties held regardless of outcome, and runs out where duties conflict or where no duty reaches the case; virtue ethics judges it by the character it expresses, and runs out where a trait such as courage has to become a particular act. Care ethics centers responsiveness to another's needs, and runs out when several parties need incompatible things; contractualism derives principles from what rational parties would agree to, and runs out before any such agreement is reached. The capabilities approach asks what a system leaves a person able to be and do, which is the question this book puts to a life touched by an AI system, and it runs out at measurement: capability is harder to measure than an outcome or a rule, and a system optimizing a \emph{proxy} for capability drifts toward the proxy.

Deontology's unconditional constraints are what the floor needs, and the objection that sinks it as a complete theory has no force against a constraint that decides a small number of things and leaves the rest to the other five.

The pluralism this section recommends is one where a deontological floor forces argument and conflict among the five other frameworks to bottom out somewhere; in other words, the floor means that some things are not up for debate.

\runin{What compiling a principle into a metric decides}
Turning any of the six into a machine learning objective means distilling a broad construct into a measurable target: in a diagnostic system, non-maleficence becomes minimizing false negatives and fairness becomes balanced accuracy across patient demographics. What defeats the translation is not vagueness in the principles, which can be stipulated away, but that the stipulation is where the ethics now lives. Someone chose that reading of non-maleficence, and chose the demographic groups accuracy is balanced across, and chose what to do when balancing them costs accuracy overall. Each choice is defensible and each is a moral decision made by a person and then compiled into something that looks like a specification. A system optimizing the result has no access to the decision and no way to reopen it, which is why the first way to build a floor — the floor as a property of the artifact — cannot notice when the reason for a constraint has left the building.

\runin{Which value conflicts a designer may resolve by choosing}
Values assigned to a system will conflict, and most of those conflicts are ordinary design work: rank the values explicitly, keep a record of how comparable cases were resolved, and audit afterward for the gap between the principles a system was meant to implement and what it did. One conflict is not like the others, and it is sometimes misguidedly filed under “cultural difference.” The UN's 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted with forty-eight states in favor and none against; the eight that abstained were the Soviet bloc, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa — which is to say that the objection came from the governments whose treatment of their own populations the Declaration was written to name \autocite{un1948udhr}. That pattern is evidence of who finds the floor inconvenient rather than of cultural disagreement about human rights. What varies by community is how a right gets translated into local practice, and a jurisdiction refusing to extend the right at all is not a local variant to accommodate.

A system that treats every value conflict as a weighting problem has no way to represent the difference between a case where reasonable people disagree and a case where somebody wants a right withdrawn from somebody else. The first calls for the machinery above; the second is what the floor is for.
