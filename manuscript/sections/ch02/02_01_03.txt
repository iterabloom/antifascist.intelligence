2.1.3. Navigating AI Values, Priorities, and Moral Dilemmas
Building an AI system on explicit, deliberately chosen values means working through ethical theory, cultural difference, and individual preference all at once, and none of them settle on their own.

Three problems recur when values are assigned and prioritized:
<<list>>
1. Resolving ethical tensions: different frameworks favor different values, and they collide — a consequentialist lens maximizes well-being, deontological ethics holds to a duty regardless of the outcome. The challenge is a balance where the AI system's judgment is both effective and ethically defensible.
2. Accommodating cultural difference: the UN's 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the General Assembly with zero dissenting votes, is the universal floor [[cite:C0703]]. What varies by community is how a right gets translated into local practice — a real design problem, worth cross-cultural input built into the design process and a system that weighs how a right is honored in context rather than assuming one fixed application works everywhere. A jurisdiction refusing to extend the right at all isn't a local variant to accommodate — it's a violation the system doesn't launder as diversity.
3. Building in ethical adaptability: an AI system needs the flexibility to re-evaluate its values as new information or interaction with humans warrants it, which is what lets its decision-making keep improving and respond to situations no one anticipated.
<</list>>

A superintelligent AI system also needs to resolve moral paradoxes and value trade-offs directly. Six strategies do that work:
<<list>>
1. Context sensitivity: an AI system that understands its surroundings applies ethical principles more accurately, incorporating domain-specific knowledge and adapting its response to the situation it is in.
2. Hierarchical value systems: ranking values explicitly — human dignity and autonomy taking precedence over broader societal goals, for instance — gives the system a structure for deciding among conflicting principles instead of weighing them all as equal.
3. Case-based moral reasoning: an AI system can draw on a record of past moral dilemmas and how they were resolved to make a sound, context-sensitive decision on the trade-off in front of it.
4. Multi-objective optimization: where principles conflict outright, formal multi-objective optimization can balance the competing objectives mathematically rather than by fiat.
5. Human input: building direct human mentorship into the AI system's decision-making keeps a human perspective in the loop — section 4.1.3 takes up what that mentorship looks like in practice.
6. Ethical auditing: regular audits catch the gap between the ethical principles a system was meant to implement and what it is actually doing, by examining its outputs, its decision structure, and its measured ethical performance directly.
<</list>>

An autonomous vehicle facing a choice between a pedestrian collision and passenger safety is context-aware reasoning and a hierarchical value system in practice, used to minimize overall harm.

AI healthcare diagnostics juggle beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice in every patient-care decision; case-based reasoning and human input are what let them handle a contentious case rather than default to the nearest simple rule.

Criminal-justice AI systems — bail recommendations, recidivism prediction — carry a comparable burden for fairness, transparency, and accountability. Multi-objective optimization and frequent ethical audits are what keep due-process rights intact under that load.
