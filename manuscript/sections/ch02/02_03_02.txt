2.3.2. Affective Computing: AI Systems that Comprehend and Respond to Emotions
Affective computing sits at the intersection of AI, psychology, and human-computer interaction: systems that recognize, interpret, and respond to human emotion. It is the practical, applied half of what section 2.3.1 treats as theoretically unsettled — a system can be useful at reading and responding to emotional signal regardless of which account of what an emotion actually is turns out to be right.

Techniques for emotion recognition
<<list>>
- Facial expression analysis: computer-vision systems trained to classify emotional expression from facial images. This is a narrower and harder problem than face verification — telling two photos are the same person, which is what widely deployed systems like Facebook's DeepFace actually do — and emotion-classification accuracy claims should be read with that distinction in mind [[cite:C0311]].
- Voice pattern analysis: decoding emotional signal from pitch, tone, pace, and volume. Cogito's voice-analysis tools, used to give call-center agents real-time coaching, are one deployed example [[cite:C0313]].
- Physiological signal analysis: bio-sensing hardware — heart rate, skin conductance, pupil dilation — captured by devices like the Empatica E4 wristband, feeding an inference about emotional state from the body rather than the face or voice [[cite:C0312]].
<</list>>

Deployment
<<list>>
- Mental health: Woebot, a cognitive-behavioral-therapy chatbot, offers one real example of emotion-aware support for stress, anxiety, and depression [[cite:C0291]].
- Education: Affectiva's Emotion AI assesses student engagement from facial signal to inform teaching-strategy adjustments [[cite:C0295]].
- Entertainment: games and virtual-reality platforms that adjust content to a player's immediate emotional response.
<</list>>

Challenges
<<list>>
- Privacy: collecting and analyzing emotional data requires meaningful consent, not a buried terms-of-service clause, given how sensitive the data is.
- Accuracy: emotional expression varies culturally and individually, and a system trained on one population's expressive norms will misread another's — confidently.
- Bias: an AI system inherits whatever bias sits in its training data, with the same downstream discrimination risk as any other biased classifier applied to people.
- Manipulation: a system that understands human emotion well enough to respond to it also understands it well enough to exploit it — the same governance question chapter 2 keeps returning to.
<</list>>
