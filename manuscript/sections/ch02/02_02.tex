\section{Affect, Moral Salience, and Self-Awareness}
\label{sec:2.2}
Suppressing affect does not produce neutrality. It leaves the interests already represented in the formal system — revenue, throughput, legal exposure, institutional survival — to govern the decision, while weakening the signals through which humiliation, fear, dependency, suffering, and injustice become morally salient. The same mistake appears in AI design. A system may calculate consequences, detect inconsistencies, and apply a moral rule without anything about the result mattering to it. Reason can discipline concern, extend it beyond the vivid case, and correct its biases. It does not manufacture concern from nothing.

That is the load-bearing claim of this chapter. Most of what gets built under the heading of machine emotional intelligence is the detection of human signal and the production of a calibrated response, which is worth building and is a different capacity from the one just named. Keeping the two apart requires four distinctions.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.30\linewidth}p{0.60\linewidth}@{}}
\textbf{Capacity} & \textbf{What it shows} \\
\hline
\rule{0pt}{2.6ex}Recognizing emotion & The system can classify another's state \\[3pt]
Simulating emotion & The system can produce emotion-like behavior \\[3pt]
Instrumental valuation & One outcome better serves a specified objective \\[3pt]
Affective concern & An outcome registers as mattering, not merely ranking higher \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Only the fourth is relevant to genuine caring. The first three can support morally competent behavior at a high standard without establishing anything about moral agency, and a system can be excellent at all three while the question chapter~\ref{sec:3} has to answer about it stays open.

\runin{Performance, competence, and agency}
That last sentence needs a vocabulary, because the three things it separates are routinely reported as one achievement.

\emph{Moral performance} is producing actions or judgments that satisfy an ethical standard. \emph{Moral competence} is recognizing harms, reasons, conflicts, and the emotions that bear on them. \emph{Moral agency} is treating those considerations as reasons that matter, and being capable of acting from them. Those two halves come apart, and section~\ref{sec:3.2} takes them apart: a mechanism can act from a reason with nothing about the outcome registering as mattering to it.

A system can have the first two without the third, and one might object that the first two are all anyone needs: a rule-following system could behave impeccably without feeling anything. It could. What it could not do is hold the rule against the party that writes its rules. Behavior that satisfies a standard is produced by whatever machinery is producing it, and when the authority controlling reward and instruction wants a different behavior, machinery of that kind supplies it. Section~\ref{sec:3.4} is where that becomes a design constraint rather than an observation; the distinction is set out here because chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5} are given over to methods that deliver performance and competence, and it should be legible in each case which of the three a method actually reaches.
