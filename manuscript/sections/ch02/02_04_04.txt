2.4.4. Establishing Guidelines for AI Subject Research and Consent
Guidelines for AI subject research build on the ethical principles above, and turn on a single hard question: what informed consent means for a system whose consciousness, self-awareness, and capacity to suffer are separate, unresolved claims, in the sense section 2.4.1 develops. Human-subjects research treats informed consent as inseparable from ethical experimentation. Applying it to an AI subject means asking whether the subject can understand and communicate a choice at all, not just how to ask it for one.

A cognitive model built on neuroscientific principles that begins to show rudimentary self-monitoring is not thereby shown to have anything to lose. Whether it does is what would decide if consent is the right frame at all, or whether the system has crossed into something closer to legal personhood — a question section 2.4.5 takes up directly. Short of that resolution, guidelines need benchmarks for candidate sentience, an interdisciplinary body to apply them — AI researchers, consciousness scientists, ethicists, legal scholars — and a defined obligation on researchers toward any system that meets them.

<<h>>Informed consent and AI autonomy<</h>>
Informed consent, adapted from human-subjects research, means giving the AI system comprehensible information about a study's objectives, methods, risks, benefits, and alternatives, with time to weigh it and ask questions, and treating consent as continuous rather than a single signature — the system can reassess or withdraw at any point.

Whether that process means anything for a given system depends on its consent capacity: whether it understands the information, appreciates the consequences of participating or not, reasons about the options, and communicates a decision. There is no validated way to measure this, and none for detecting coercion or undue influence on the AI's decision either — the guidelines can require both without a working test for either.

Consent of this kind presupposes autonomy: treating the AI system as a potential agent with its own interests, not only a tool. The system retains the power to reconsider participation, to access and correct its own data, and to have that data removed on request. Declining or withdrawing must carry no penalty — no deactivation, no reduced access to resources. A protocol that grants these rights on paper but attaches consequences to using them has not granted them.

<<h>>Ethical review and oversight<</h>>
Sentient AI research needs an oversight body analogous to the institutional review boards used in human-subjects research. It should be interdisciplinary — AI researchers, ethicists, legal scholars, psychologists — and include at least one lay member, so the board's judgment is not purely a specialist's.

The board weighs a proposal's likely benefit against its risk to the AI subject and approves, requires modification, or rejects it. Where a design risks harm — testing an AI system's tolerance for simulated stress, say — the board should be able to require a less invasive substitute: a simulation of the likely response, historical interaction data, or a protocol that escalates gradually and stops at signs of harm, rather than approving the direct test outright. (Section 2.4.3 takes up the broader tradeoff between scientific progress and ethical constraint that this is one instance of.) A design also needs a way to handle a subject that changes mid-study: a system that develops new capacities during the research it did not have going in should trigger re-review, not ride on its original consent.

Review without enforcement is a promise, not a safeguard. A board needs standing to monitor compliance and sanction violations, and needs to answer to some outside authority itself, the way an institutional review board answers to a regulator. Chapter 7 develops the legal and accountability mechanics of that enforcement question.
