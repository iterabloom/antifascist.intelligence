2.1. Ethical Frameworks and AI Alignment
The ethics that guide a superintelligent AI's behavior, interactions, decisions, and learning are the linchpin of whether it turns out friendly at all. That makes the choice among competing ethical theories central to AI alignment — specifically, which frameworks support the development of an antifascist, altruistic, and sentient AI system.

Consequentialism, which judges an action by its outcomes, is one starting point, but it runs into trouble applied to AI systems — from whether a system can accurately and comprehensively predict the consequences of its own actions, to the inherently subjective question of what counts as the "best" outcome.

Deontological ethics takes the opposite approach: rules and duties over outcomes. The rules themselves have to adhere to, and actively promote, democratic principles and antifascist values — and the framework's limitation is its rigidity, which can limit an AI system's adaptability in novel, unforeseen situations.

The capabilities approach offers another alternative: it focuses on enhancing what a being can be and do — its "beings and doings" — rather than dictating outcomes or rules directly. Applied to AI, it points toward systems that expand individual and collective capability while respecting other ethical constraints.

Virtue ethics takes a third approach, emphasizing character over rules or outcomes — a value-based, holistic route to alignment. Applied to AI, it points toward systems built to act compassionately rather than merely follow rules or optimize outcomes; section 2.2 argues that compassion, not felt empathy, is the separable and more tractable capacity worth building.

Two further frameworks matter enough to the argument that follows to name here: care ethics, which centers relationship and responsiveness to another's need rather than rule or outcome, and contractualism, which grounds moral principle in what parties could agree to. The capabilities approach is the one this book returns to furthest from here — chapter 8 uses it to ask what an AI-affected life is actually free to become, which is a different question from whether a system behaved well.

Implementing any one of these theories alone raises the same kinds of problems: how to represent moral principles computationally, and how to keep an AI system's ethical reasoning flexible enough for evolving understanding and context-dependent scenarios. A hybrid approach — drawing on each framework's strengths while limiting its weaknesses — is one plausible way through those problems. Section 2.1.5 gives that approach a specific shape: an unconditional floor of things a system will not do, with learned judgment operating above it.

Translating these principles into concrete machine objectives — through the design of reward systems, learning algorithms, and decision-making protocols — is its own task, one that needs AI developers, ethicists, and other stakeholders working together. Section 2.1.2 works through what that translation looks like.
