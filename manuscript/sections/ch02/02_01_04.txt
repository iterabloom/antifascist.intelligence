2.1.4. Antifascist Ethics for AI Alignment
Antifascist ethics opposes concentrating power and authority in AI systems and counters the potential misuse of AI technology by authoritarian actors. The word is chosen deliberately: fascism is the narrower and more charged term, and I use it because it is more honest about the politics in which this book is being written — but the target is authoritarian power in every form, and a system built on these principles is anti-authoritarian in full, not only antifascist.

A stance needs a definition to be a design specification, and this is where books of this kind usually stop. Opposing "concentrated, unaccountable power" gives an engineer nothing to build. It does not say what a system would detect, what would count as a false positive, or how anyone would know the system was wrong. So before the strategies, the definition.

<<h>>Fascism as a structural signature<</h>>
I take fascism to name a structure rather than a costume. Four features travel together, and each is visible from outside the institution displaying it:

<<list>>
1. Recuperation of dissent. The institution does not suppress disagreement; it metabolizes it. Refusal is converted into a managed appearance — a permitted outlet, a consultation, a feedback channel — which the institution can then point to as proof that it listens. Debord aimed this warning at his own concept, noting that even denunciation of the spectacle can become a hollow formula that reinforces what it denounces, and that dissatisfaction itself can be sold back as a commodity of opposition [[cite:C0708]]. The tell is that the mechanism exists, is used, and moves nothing.

2. Aestheticization of the metric. The measure substitutes for the thing measured, and the substitution is experienced as rigor. Benjamin's account of fascism organizing the masses by granting expression in place of rights is the political form of this [[cite:C0709]]; the administrative form is a number that collapses two states — done and verified — into one, so that whatever falls outside the number is not refuted or dismissed but simply unnamed.

3. Coding of exception as betrayal. Marking a case as one the rule does not fit becomes an act requiring justification, and then an act of disloyalty. The honest statement that a standard does not apply to this case reads as weakness. It is in fact the irreducible labor of deciding, case by case, what a rule is for.

4. Decoupling of the model from the world it claims to track. The institution's representation of reality and reality itself come apart, and the institution defends the representation. This is the load-bearing one, because the other three are what protect the decoupling from correction.
<</list>>

Those four are testable. A system asked whether an institution shows them is being asked about observable artifacts: whether the dissent channel has ever changed an outcome, whether the metric admits an unmeasured remainder, what happens to the person who files the exception, whether the model is ever checked against anything outside itself.

<<h>>Two scales<</h>>
Deleuze and Guattari's distinction between molar and molecular fascism is the reason the four features recur at sizes that seem unrelated [[cite:C0707]]. Molar fascism is the state, the regime, the camp. Molecular fascism is the office someone commutes to, the form they fill out, and the desire with which they fill it out. The same administrative pattern is being rediscovered at each scale, which is why a definition pitched only at the state cannot see the version an AI deployment actually instantiates. At state scale the pattern kills journalists. At organizational scale it turns an engineering decision into scripture: the constraint remains, its reason dies, and asking why becomes the deviant act. A system that can only recognize the molar case will certify the molecular one as healthy.

<<h>>What makes it fascism and not authoritarianism in general<</h>>
The structural signature is shared with a good deal of ordinary institutional decay, and a detector tuned to it alone will flag every badly run organization on earth. Fascism proper adds features that are narrower and, for the same reason, more discriminating: mass mobilization, which mere obedience does not require; a leader cult; a scapegoated out-group, which Eco places at the origin — the first appeal is against the intruder [[cite:C0706]]; contempt for procedural legality as such, so that law is an obstacle to the national will and not a constraint on power; and deniability as technique, in which the movement's violence is disowned at the top while being rewarded at the bottom. Paxton's compressed version is worth having in full: fascism is "a form of political behavior marked by obsessive preoccupation with community decline, humiliation, or victimhood and by compensatory cults of unity, energy, and purity" [[cite:C0710]].

Those are detection targets. Concentrated, unaccountable power is not. I will return in section 9.1.7 to what remains genuinely unsolved about turning this list into a working detector, because the honest answer is that naming the targets is the easy half.

In AI alignment terms, then, antifascist design means building systems that enhance democratic values, civil rights, and inclusive solidarity, that refuse to facilitate autocratic practices or prop up an oppressive regime, and that do not themselves display the four features above. Seven strategies do that work, and each should be read as an answer to a specific item on the list:
<<list>>
1. Democratic decision-making: build AI systems whose decisions respect and promote democratic values. An AI system used in media and journalism, for instance, should weight balanced coverage, unbiased reporting, and transparent sourcing, cutting the odds it ends up spreading disinformation or propaganda — natural language processing that flags potential bias in a news article and surfaces alternative sources is one way to do it.

2. Protecting privacy and autonomy: design AI systems that respect individual privacy and autonomy rather than enabling mass surveillance or manipulating individual choices — federated learning and differential privacy, applied to systems handling sensitive healthcare, financial, or communications data, are two ways to do it (section 5.4.2 goes further into both as technical countermeasures against authoritarian misuse). A privacy-preserving diagnostic tool, for instance, can keep a patient's data encrypted on their own device rather than sending it to an external server for anything beyond the diagnosis itself.

3. Equitable distribution of power and resources: build AI systems that keep the distribution of power and resources fair, transparent, and accountable. An AI decision-making system used in social services or law enforcement should work against power concentration, bias, and discrimination rather than reinforce them — an algorithm allocating scholarships, for instance, distributing them by need and correcting for the systemic bias already in the data it was trained on.

4. Transparency and accountability: an AI system needs an explanation of its own algorithm and outcomes that a person can actually read, and an independent body to audit whether it holds to antifascist principles in practice rather than on paper.

5. Resistance to market power: decentralized AI architectures resist the monopolistic and monopsonistic concentration that a handful of corporate entities would otherwise have over the field. Multi-agent systems and edge computing, publicly funded and released under a strong copyleft license, let AI agents function independently and make decisions collaboratively without a profit-motivated gatekeeper positioned to shut down whatever threatens its position.

6. Monitoring and counteracting misuse: build the mechanisms to detect misuse, unintended consequences, and violations of antifascist principles as they happen — AI-powered censorship, digital surveillance, an oppressive use of facial recognition — and the redress and corrective action to follow, in something closer to real time than an after-the-fact report.

7. AI systems that support dialogue and collaboration: design AI systems that promote open discussion and creative problem-solving among a diverse set of stakeholders, built around active listening and mutual understanding rather than around manufacturing a feeling of empathy in either party. A translation tool or a content-moderation tool that reduces the practical barriers to communication between people from different backgrounds is doing this kind of work already.
<</list>>
