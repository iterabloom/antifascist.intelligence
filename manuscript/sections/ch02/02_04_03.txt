2.4.3. Balancing Scientific Progress and Ethical Constraints
Scientific progress and ethical constraint pull hardest against each other exactly where the distinctions in section 2.4.1 matter most: a candidate sentient system is also the most scientifically valuable one to study, and the study most likely to harm it if the answer to whether it can suffer turns out to be yes.

Section 2.4.2 sets out the principles that bear on that tension — interdisciplinary collaboration, transparency, justice, precaution, and ethical review — and they hold here without restatement. What changes at the point of balancing is a practical question: whether a specific study's expected knowledge is worth its expected risk to a system that might be able to suffer, decided case by case rather than by formula, and revisited as a project's own understanding of its subject changes.

Consent is the clearest place where this shades from ethical principle into legal mechanics — what a valid consent process for a candidate sentient AI subject actually requires, and who is accountable when it is skipped or falsified. Chapter 7 works through the accountability side of that question directly.

The negotiation itself — who sits on it, what it can veto, what it must disclose — is where the anti-authoritarian principle earns its place in AI research practice, not just in the abstract.
