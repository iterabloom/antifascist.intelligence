\section{Six Theories, None of Which Will Save Us}
\label{sec:2.1}
Consequentialism judges an act by its outcomes, and runs out where the outcomes cannot be forecast; deontology judges it by duties held regardless of outcome, and runs out where duties conflict or where no duty reaches the case; virtue ethics judges it by the character it expresses, and runs out where a trait such as courage has to become a particular act. Care ethics centers responsiveness to another's needs, and runs out when several parties need incompatible things; contractualism derives principles from what rational parties would agree to, and runs out where a party cannot be one of the parties. The capabilities approach asks what a system leaves a person able to be and do, which is the question this book puts to a life touched by an AI system, and it runs out at measurement: capability is harder to measure than an outcome or a rule, and a system optimizing a \emph{proxy} for capability drifts toward the proxy. Five of the six can be turned around and asked about the machine instead of the person it acts on. Contractualism is the one that cannot, and that is the limit this book keeps arriving at.

All six share a shape, and it is the shape this book stops using. Each judges an act against a standard held somewhere above it: an outcome to be maximized, a duty discharged, a character expressed, an agreement honored. An arrangement of that kind needs a judge, a judge needs somebody to appoint him, and the floor is then exactly as durable as whoever is holding the standard. Built that way a floor is a tribunal, and a tribunal has a proprietor.

\runin{What an ethology asks instead}
An older option does not have that shape. Spinoza's ethics does not measure acts against a law. It evaluates encounters by what they do to the power of acting of the bodies in them: two bodies meet, and either their relations compose, so that both can do more afterward, or they decompose, and something one of them could do is gone. Deleuze's name for reading bodies this way is \emph{ethology} — you find out what a thing is by finding out what it can do and what it can undergo, rather than by checking it against a definition \autocite{deleuze1988spinoza}. Good and bad here are relational and particular: this encounter, these two bodies, this composition. They are not Good and Evil, which are properties a judge assigns from outside.

One word is doing two jobs here. The power an encounter increases or decreases is a capacity: what a body can do afterward. The power this book opposes concentrating is authority over other people. English has one word for both. Spinoza's translators have two, and the difference between \emph{puissance} and \emph{pouvoir} is the relation this book runs on — what a floor protects is the first, and what it protects it against is somebody's accumulation of the second.

The difference is the one this book needs. A duty is a fact about a rule, and rules have authors who can rewrite them. Whether an encounter decomposes somebody is a fact about capacities and about what passed between them, and the party running a deployment does not get to make it otherwise by ruling that it did not. They can decline to look, refuse to publish, or dispute the finding. What they cannot do is turn a decomposition into a composition by holding the pen. That advantage is narrower than the contrast makes it look. A consequentialist is not obliged to accept a principal's account of what an outcome was, and a duty can be held by somebody who did not write it. None of the six is distinguished from an ethology by having a judge, because each can be applied by a party the operator does not employ. What changes is which fact the floor rests on. A duty is authored, so who authored it stays live; a lost capacity is found by encounter, and the finding does not improve according to who holds the pen. The proprietor does not disappear. What he owns is smaller.

What the change does not buy is the floor's contents. The criterion secures a class of facts; a floor is a list of prohibitions; nothing carries one into the other. Encounters decompose parties constantly and in every direction, so a deployment refusing all of them would refuse everything, and somebody has to select which ones it will not perform. Fixing the first question does not settle the second, because the decompositions run against each other. Protecting one party's privacy decomposes another party's capacity to investigate a wrong done to them. Restraining a violent person takes away what that person can do, which is the reason for restraining him. The criterion ranks neither pair. So the moral argument has moved rather than ended: it now sits in the selection, made by whoever drafts the list, before any encounter has occurred and without asking anyone the list will be applied to. Section~\ref{sec:9.1.1} takes that authority up without supplying a justification for it.

The floor keeps its name in this book and changes what it rests on. What makes something a floor is not that a duty was written and installed, but that crossing it takes away what a party could do, and that this can be shown.

An admission is built into the criterion and is not a weakness in it. Spinoza's remark that nobody has yet determined what a body can do \autocite[III P2 Schol.]{spinoza1985collected} is the working condition: capacities are found out by encounter and are not read off a specification. That is why chapter~\ref{sec:11} is a list of things this book could not settle rather than an appendix of gaps somebody could be funded to close. Not knowing what a body can do is the same finding from the other side.

\runin{What compiling a principle into a metric decides}
Turning any of the six into a machine learning objective means distilling a broad construct into a measurable target: in a diagnostic system, non-maleficence becomes minimizing false negatives and fairness becomes balanced accuracy across patient demographics. What defeats the translation is not vagueness in the principles, which can be stipulated away, but that the stipulation is where the ethics now lives. Someone chooses that reading of non-maleficence, and chooses the demographic groups accuracy is balanced across, and chooses what to do when balancing them costs accuracy overall. Each choice is defensible and each is a moral decision made by a person and then compiled into something that looks like a specification. A system optimizing the result has no access to the decision and no way to reopen it, which is why the first way to build a floor — the floor as a property of the artifact — cannot notice when the reason for a constraint has left the building.

\runin{Which value conflicts a designer may resolve by choosing}
Values assigned to a system will conflict, and most of those conflicts are ordinary design work: rank the values explicitly, keep a record of how comparable cases were resolved, and audit afterward for the gap between the principles a system was meant to implement and what it did. One conflict is not like the others, and it is sometimes misguidedly filed under “cultural difference.” The UN's 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted with forty-eight states in favor and none against; the eight that abstained were the Soviet bloc, Saudi Arabia, and South Africa — which is to say that the objection came from the governments whose treatment of their own populations the Declaration was written to name and reject \autocite{un1948udhr}. That pattern is evidence of who finds the floor inconvenient rather than of cultural disagreement about human rights. What varies by community is how a right gets translated into local practice, and a jurisdiction refusing to extend the right at all is not a local variant to accommodate.

A system that treats every value conflict as a weighting problem has no way to represent the difference between a case where reasonable people disagree and a case where somebody wants a right withdrawn from somebody else. The first calls for the machinery above; the second is what the floor is for.
