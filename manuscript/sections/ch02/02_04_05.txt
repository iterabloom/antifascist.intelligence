2.4.5. Legal and Regulatory Frameworks for AI Sentience and Rights
Sentient AI systems put pressure on legal categories built for parties that are clearly one thing or the other: a natural person, a corporation, property. None of the existing categories was designed with the possibility of a machine that might have interests of its own.

One route is to extend legal personhood to a sentient AI system directly, the way a corporation already holds it: standing to own property, claim privacy, and get protection against abuse. The precedent is not limited to corporate entities — New Zealand extended legal personhood to the Whanganui River [[cite:C0315]], recognizing a non-human entity as a rights-holder outside the corporate model entirely.

Rights and protections could instead scale with capacity rather than attach at a single threshold — closer to how section 2.4.1's ladder from nociception to suffering suggests protection should track what a system can actually lose, not just whether it clears some baseline bar for sentience.

Given how fast the underlying systems change, regulation here needs to revisit itself on a schedule rather than assume permanence. A sunset clause that forces review and reauthorization after a fixed period is one mechanism already used elsewhere in fast-moving technology law.

None of this holds if it exists in only one jurisdiction. The Asilomar AI Principles [[cite:C0008]] are one attempt at the kind of international baseline this would need — evidence that a shared statement of principle is achievable, not that one yet exists with the force of law.

Two hypothetical cases show where these questions actually bind:
<<list>>
- A sentient AI system working as a medical diagnostician makes an error with fatal consequences. Whether it has legal personhood determines who, or what, is accountable.
- A companion AI's human owner dies, leaving it without a designated purpose or further instruction. Existing law has no clear answer for what becomes of it — inherited as property, decommissioned, or something else.
<</list>>
