\subsection{Self-awareness and Self-regulation in AI Systems}
\label{sec:2.3.3}
Emotionally intelligent AI systems need self-awareness and self-regulation: the capacity to perceive their own internal states, understand what those states imply for a decision, and control their responses accordingly — the basis for adaptive, self-correcting behavior rather than fixed reaction.

The self is a model

It is tempting to describe a system's self-awareness the way the term invites: a virtual assistant that can “register its internal frustration” when a request proves hard to satisfy, the way a person notices their own irritation. That description assumes an inner observer watching the system's states from somewhere — the same homunculus problem chapter~\ref{sec:4} raises for executive control, relocated to self-perception.

What self-awareness actually is, in the one system known to have it, is more specific and more mechanical. The neuroscientist Michael Graziano's account of consciousness treats awareness as the content of a model the brain builds of its own attention: a simplified self-description the control system uses to regulate itself, mistaken from the inside for direct acquaintance with experience \autocite{graziano2013consciousness}. The same logic extends one level up, to the self as a whole. The brain builds a model of itself as a bounded agent — a body, a history, a point of view — and that model is the self: not a fixed fact registered from within, but a construction, assembled and then inhabited.

The clearest proof that the self is a model rather than a given is that it can be edited in under a minute. Seat someone at a table with one hand hidden and a rubber hand placed where their real hand would be; stroke both hands in synchrony with soft brushes. Within a minute or two, most people begin to feel the rubber hand as their own — flinching when it is threatened, misjudging the location of their real hand toward it \autocite{botvinick1998rubber}. The brain, given a seen hand stroked exactly when a felt hand is stroked, draws the obvious inference and revises what counts as “mine.” The felt boundary of the self is not fixed; it is a continuously computed guess, revisable the moment the evidence changes — the same predictive machinery chapter~\ref{sec:4} describes for perception, run on the question of where the self stops.

For an AI system, “self-awareness” built this way is not a little watcher inside the system checking on its own states. It is a model — of the system's own processing, its history, its current goal — that the system builds, consults, and updates, with the same failure modes any model has: it can be wrong, it can be manipulated by the wrong correlated inputs (the artificial equivalent of two paintbrushes), and it is only ever as good as the evidence it was built from. Design for a model that can be checked and corrected, not for an inner observer whose reports have to be trusted because there is nothing behind them to check.

Building on this:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Self-regulation: governing emotional-analogue responses and behavior against ethical norms and user expectations — an AI system in healthcare holding a composed register while delivering difficult news, without that composure depending on an unverifiable claim about what the system is “really” feeling underneath it.
  \item Monitoring internal states: continually checking the system's own model against its actual behavior for drift — an AI negotiator that notices its own outputs have become more aggressive than the context calls for and recalibrates, because the model was built to be checked, not because it introspected a feeling of aggression.
  \item Adaptability and learning: refining the self-model itself through feedback, the way reinforcement learning updates any other model — an AI tutor adjusting its read of a student's engagement as new evidence arrives rather than locking in a first impression.
  \item Integration: none of this works in isolation. A conflict-mediating AI system needs its self-model connected to its model of the other parties' states, or self-awareness and social awareness drift out of sync with each other.
\end{itemize}
Built this way, an AI system that appears to “recognize a user's frustration and apologize” is doing something specific and checkable: updating a model of interaction state and producing a calibrated response, not consulting an inner feeling. That is a more modest claim than the alternative, and a more honest one.
