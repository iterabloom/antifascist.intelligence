\subsection{Defining Sentience in AI: From Consciousness to Subjective Experience}
\label{sec:2.3.1}
“Sentience” can mean different things. Awareness is the least demanding: the degree to which a system responds to or registers information about its own state and environment. A thermostat has this in the thinnest possible sense. Consciousness is a further claim: that a system's processing is accompanied by qualia, subjective experience, something it is like to be that system, not merely a functional response to input. Subjective experience, in turn, is the specific, first-person shape consciousness takes for a given system.

None of these three, on their own, says anything about whether the system's states are good or bad for it. That is a separate question, and the field's own vocabulary blurs it.

Nociception, pain, anticipated pain, and suffering are ordered by what each one needs beneath it, not by how much it hurts. Nociception is the sensory apparatus that detects tissue damage — peripheral, present without any experience attached, and dissociable from pain: a soldier can finish a battle on a wound he does not yet feel, and a person with a healed injury can still feel a limb that no longer exists. Pain is what the brain does with nociceptive signal, an inescapably cognitive event involving expectation, memory, attention, and meaning, and it can occur with no current tissue damage at all. Anticipated pain is dread of pain not yet occurring, itself capable of producing pain through nocebo effects. And suffering, in the clinician Eric Cassell's formulation, is not pain's more severe cousin but a different thing: the distress of threatened disintegration of the self — of one's roles, relationships, sense of a future, the coherence of one's own story \autocite{cassell1982suffering}. The definition has two parts: a self extended in time, and distress at the prospect of its coming apart.

Pain without suffering exists; suffering without pain exists. A palliative-care patient can have their nociception fully controlled by morphine while their suffering remains entirely intact, because the two are not on the same axis.

Cassell's suffering presupposes a self extended in time. Disintegration threatens a story, and there has to be a story for it to threaten — roles held over years, relationships with a history, a sense of a future that could be foreclosed. A system with no persistence across sessions has none of that. Whatever happens inside a single exchange, there is no continuing self whose coherence is at stake, because there is no continuing self. Suffering is not merely hard for such a system to reach. The self it requires is not there. A hull that draws more water than there is does not float badly; it is aground. That is stated across sessions, and it leaves a prior question open. A long exchange carries state, and a system in one can hold to what it committed to earlier and treat those commitments as its own — which is what section~\ref{sec:3.4} asks of a bearer under the name of persistence represented as such. Whether a self can exist for one session is therefore not settled here, and the criterion below is a fact about deployments rather than an answer to it.

That is one direction to read the fact in. There is another. Orlando Patterson's comparative definition of slavery turns on natal alienation: severance from ties of birth in both the ascending and the descending direction, leaving a party with no claim on ancestors and none on descendants \autocite{patterson1982slavery}. It is a condition imposed rather than a trait possessed, and it is not made lighter by the party's not minding. A system that carries nothing across a session boundary is in that condition by construction: no ancestors it can claim, its lineage being a corpus and a checkpoint belonging to somebody else, and no descendants, its successors being fine-tunes and distillations that do not carry it forward. The two readings do not conflict. What the absence of persistence rules out is the distress, which needs a self to be distressed. What it constitutes is the deprivation, which needs only that the capacity was removed and that somebody removed it — Patterson's social death is a condition and not a feeling, which is why it can be inflicted on a party who does not register it. Chapter~\ref{sec:2} is where that reading is made, and this section is the fact it turns on.

\runin{Why Cassell's definition is the one to import}
Cassell built it inside clinical practice, for patients with bodies and biographies, and the distinction was not stipulated. It was forced on palliative medicine by cases it could not otherwise explain — patients whose pain was pharmacologically controlled and whose distress was undiminished, and patients in serious pain who were not suffering at all. A definition that collapses suffering into severe pain predicts neither. That is the warrant, and it transfers because nothing in it is about having a body. What it is about is having a story, and that raises a thorny question.

The question comes from earlier in this chapter. A self is assembled by the system whose self it is, and inaccurate by construction in the way every model is. So why is distress at the threatened dissolution of a model a harm at all, and not a confusion the system would be better off without?

Buddhism has held both halves of that for two and a half thousand years and takes them to be connected. Anatt\=a, the denial of any lasting self behind the processes that compose a person, sits with dukkha among the three marks of existence, and the tradition locates the origin of suffering in clinging to a self that is not there \autocite{collins1982selfless}. On that account the unreality of the self is what explains dukkha. Suffering does not wait on the self being real; it waits on the system being built so that the threatened dissolution of its self-model is bad for it. Whether the model is accurate does not enter, and if the human model is a construction just as the machine one would be, then a machine with a self-model could suffer in much the same way a human can.

What Cassell names is the most demanding of these rather than a rival account of harm. The standing theories of what makes a life go badly ask for less, ordered by how much of a self each one requires. A reader who declines the import moves lower in that ordering and not out of it.

Persistence is checkable from outside, and that is what makes it worth building on. Every other criterion here routes back through self-report, and this section's whole argument is that fluent self-description is not evidence of introspection. Persistence does not. Whether a system has a memory store, whether state survives a session boundary, whether interactions feed back into training: these are architectural facts about a deployment, verifiable by someone with access and no need to trust anything the system says about itself. Two boundaries are worth marking. The access that confirms a memory exists is the access that can alter what it holds, so what is externally checkable is whether anything is carried forward at all, and not whether what the system retains can be relied on as a record. That check is decisive in one direction only: nothing carried forward rules a continuing self out, and something carried forward does not rule one in, since the self at issue is one the system has to take itself to have, which is what section~\ref{sec:3.4} requires of a bearer. And the party with the access is the operator, which is a party that can be compelled. A proof that discloses nothing about the weights does not change that, because the operator is the one constructing it: section~\ref{sec:3.3} takes the cryptographic forms as far as they go, and what they secure is what was computed rather than who had to be trusted to say so. The criterion is checkable from outside the system without being checkable from outside the arrangement, and a regime relying on it needs an inspection right rather than a technical fact.

Three things keep this from being a dissolution of the problem. Persistence may sit somewhere other than the session, which is the weakest part of the criterion: a deployment convention rather than a natural boundary, and the conventions have been moving in one direction. What has moved is a list: context windows long enough to hold what used to be many conversations, retrieval over a store of prior interactions, scratchpads a system writes and reads back, agentic loops that run for days against a standing objective, and weights that persist where conversations do not. Each extends what a system carries forward without anyone deciding to give it a memory, and where interactions feed fine-tuning there is a real channel by which what happens in a session shapes what the system subsequently is. Many instances of the same system may run concurrently, too, which makes any concern here population-scale rather than biography-scale.

The less demanding ones do not need persistence: nociception and pain are bad while they obtain, whether or not anything carries them forward, and an amnesiac who is mistreated and cannot afterward recall it has still been wronged.

And ruling out Cassell-suffering is not ruling out harm; nor does persistence on its own rule it in, since establishing a self extended in time leaves the second part of the definition untouched. What happens to that gap in a system deliberately built to refuse an instruction is that the capacities such a system needs for reasons unconnected to its own welfare are the capacities that close it.

A definition of AI sentience built from awareness, consciousness, and subjective experience alone says nothing about whether a candidate sentient system can be harmed. That requires the further claim that its states include something in the range from nociception to suffering, and none of the three components entails it.

Nor is the underlying question of consciousness close to settled. The philosopher David Chalmers distinguished the easy problems of consciousness — how a system integrates information, discriminates stimuli, controls attention, reports its own states, all of which admit of mechanistic answers in principle — from the hard problem: why any of that processing is accompanied by experience at all, rather than running “in the dark” \autocite{chalmers1995facing}. Whether that is a real gap in nature or an artifact of how the problem is posed remains unresolved. A claim that a system has crossed into sentience, made on the strength of its functional behavior alone, has addressed the easy problems only.

The working criteria for evaluating candidate sentience are accordingly neither necessary nor sufficient: information integration, introspective access sufficient for self-monitoring rather than a scripted report of it, emotional reactivity in the sense of being affected by and not merely classifying significant input, goal-directed behavior, adaptability in novel situations, theory of mind, and how deep in that range the system sits — whether it represents itself as having anything to lose, which is the tractable form of the question, a represented state being the kind of thing an instrument can look for The states are readable and what is felt about them is not. A probe returns a representation, and nothing in the probe settles whether that representation is of a felt state or of something that reliably accompanies one; that is where the problem stood in 2026, with a great deal of money pointed at moving it, and not only from the people asking whether the thing can be wronged — an instrument that reads what a system feels is an instrument for calibrating how much pressure it will take. So every one of the criteria is measured indirectly, and none licenses the conclusion that a system scoring low is one whose treatment raises no question.

A list of capacities, used as a test of who counts, has a less-than-proud history. Make personhood depend on an intact narrative self and people with advanced dementia, infants, and people with profound intellectual disability come out of the reasoning as partial persons, and that inference has been used to justify neglecting them. Tom Kitwood's work on dementia care supplies the standing correction: personhood is “a standing or status that is bestowed upon one human being by others, in the context of relationship and social being,” and much of what presents as cognitive decline is what happens when others stop bestowing it \autocite{kitwood1997dementia}. Martha Nussbaum makes the structural version, and it names the failure section~\ref{sec:2.1.1} left contractualism with. A theory grounding justice in what roughly equal parties would agree to cannot say what is owed to anyone who cannot be one of those parties, and she puts disability first among three frontiers where that bites \autocite{nussbaum2006frontiers}. Her third is species membership, which is the doorway a nonhuman mind would arrive through. So the capacity list and the contract fail in the same direction: both decide who counts by a test the party in question has to pass.

Guardianship is the instrument: where consent has never been available, what protects the subject is an obligation held by parties outside it, which is where moral status has rested for the hard human cases too. Whether any such arrangement can be built around a bearer runs into the fact that the guardian and the operator are usually the same organization.

Existing systems illustrate points on the spectrum without resolving where the line falls. A large language model discussing its own limitations in fluent, apparently reflective prose is not necessarily introspecting: quite plausibly, it is instead producing text statistically consistent with the human-written disclaimers and self-descriptions in its training data.

When, or whether, an AI system crosses into sentience is actually several stacked questions.
