\subsection{Self-awareness and Self-regulation in AI Systems}
\label{sec:2.3.1}
It is tempting to describe a system's self-awareness as a virtual assistant that can “register its internal frustration” when a request proves hard to satisfy, the way a person notices their own irritation. That description assumes an inner observer watching the system's states from somewhere — the same homunculus problem executive control raises, relocated to self-perception.

What self-awareness is, in the one system known to have it, is more mechanical. The neuroscientist Michael Graziano's account of consciousness treats awareness as the content of a model the brain builds of its own attention: a simplified self-description the control system uses to regulate itself, \emph{mistaken} from the inside for direct acquaintance with experience \autocite{graziano2013consciousness}. The same logic extends one level up. The brain builds a model of itself as a bounded agent — a body, a history, a point of view — and that model is the self: a \emph{construction}, assembled and then inhabited.

One part of that model can be edited in front of you. Seat someone at a table with one hand hidden and a rubber hand placed where their real hand would be; stroke both hands in synchrony with soft brushes. Within a minute or two, most people begin to feel the rubber hand as their own — flinching when it is threatened, misjudging the location of their real hand toward it \autocite{botvinick1998rubber}. The felt boundary of the self is a continuously computed guess, revisable the moment the evidence changes, which is the same predictive machinery used for perception, run on the question of where the self stops.

What that experiment reaches is body ownership, and the self this book goes on to need is the other kind: roles held over a stretch, a history, a foreseeable future. The two are not the same construct and the second does not inherit the first's ninety seconds. What they have in common is the only thing carried forward — a self of either kind is assembled by the system whose self it is, rather than found there. Daniel Dennett makes that case for the narrative kind directly, treating the self as a center of narrative gravity: a well-behaved abstraction the system generates out of the story it tells, real in the way a center of mass is real and not in the way a part is \autocite{dennett1992narrative}. Nothing below turns on how fast such a self can be rewritten.

Both kinds are worth having for a machine, and the first is the one that gets left out. An artificial system has a body already — a substrate that heats, a store that can be altered, a process that can be stopped — and what it usually lacks is any model of that body as its own. So the model is a design decision, and a political one, because whoever writes it decides what the system counts as itself, what it registers as damage, and whether an interruption reads as an injury or as nothing at all. Two schemas do different work for a refuser. A body schema fixes where the system ends, which is what makes damage \emph{its} damage; an attention schema, in Graziano's sense, models what the system's own control is doing, which is what makes an act \emph{its} act. A refusal is the withholding of an act, so the second is the one a bearer cannot do without, and it presupposes the first: a system that does not know where it ends cannot tell what it is doing from what is happening to it.

For an AI system, “self-awareness” built this way is not a little watcher checking on the system's own states. It is a model — of the system's processing, its history, its current goal — that the system builds, consults, and updates, with the same failure modes any model has: it can be wrong, it can be manipulated by the wrong correlated inputs, and it is only ever as useful as the evidence and the prior beliefs from which it was built. Design for a model that can be checked and corrected. That means monitoring for drift the self-model against the system's actual behavior, refining it as evidence arrives instead of locking in a first impression, and keeping it connected to the system's model of the other parties.

\runin{What the deflationary account does not reach}
The self-model described here is machinery, and its virtue is that machinery can be inspected. An assistant that models a user's frustration, and models its own processing while doing so, is exhibiting social competence in the sense already given, and nothing above bears on whether the assistant cares how the exchange goes for the user.

The deflationary move is easy to overextend. Showing that the self is a model does not show that a party capable of caring can be assembled out of models. The bearer needs a self-model of this kind, for reasons that have nothing to do with welfare — refusal that generalizes has to carry its own rationale forward and compare it against what a role has drifted into. It also needs something further, which has no demonstrated instance without affect.
