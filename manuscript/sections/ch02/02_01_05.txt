2.1.5. The Floor Beneath Learned Values
Three times in this book, working on unrelated problems, I arrive at the same requirement and do not stop to notice it.

Section 4.6.3, on how much latitude an autonomous system should have, concludes that no amount of well-designed autonomy substitutes for a genuine override — a human able to intervene, and a system built to accept the intervention rather than resist it. Section 6.7.6, working through what a vendor owes when its model reaches a targeting pipeline, concludes that what makes a system antifascist is not that it tracks what people want, but that there are things it will not do to a person no matter who wants it. Section 8.1.3, counting up a decade of voluntary commitments, concludes that a commitment nobody can enforce is a different kind of object from one somebody can.

Those are three descriptions of one thing: a constraint that does not bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to the party holding the system. Call it a floor.

The trouble is that section 2.1.1 has already dealt with the family of ethical theories that puts unconditional constraints first, and dealt with it briefly — deontological approaches were noted, credited with holding a line regardless of outcome, and set aside on the grounds that duties conflict and rules do not cover every case. Then this book spends chapters 3 and 4, close to a third of its length, on learned judgment: reinforcement learning, learning from demonstration, mentorship, play, moral apprenticeship, the growth mindset. The design chapters build the thing chapter 6 and chapter 8 say is insufficient, having already dismissed the thing they say is necessary. The "hybrid approach" section 2.1 gestures at is where the resolution was supposed to live, and nothing was ever put there.

Here is what belongs there. An unconditional floor of things the system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate preference, with learned judgment operating above it and never beneath it. Deontology's standing objection — that rules conflict and run out — is an objection to rules deciding every case, and it has no force against a floor that decides a deliberately small number of things and leaves the rest to judgment. The rigidity that is a defect in a complete moral theory is the entire point of a floor.

That reframing does real work in this book. It gives section 2.1's hybrid approach actual content. It moves the tamper-resistance research in section 7.2.2 from an appendix topic to the center, because if there is a floor then everything turns on whether it holds. And it converts the antifascist question from a matter of what a system values into two much harder and more answerable questions: what is in the floor, and who can edit it.

<<h>>Three ways to build one, and what each costs<</h>>
The second question is where this gets difficult, and I do not have a resolution to offer. What I have is a fork with three branches and a cost on each, and the honest thing is to set them out rather than pick the one that reads best.

The first branch puts the floor in the architecture, with nothing inside that holds it. Constrained optimization, tamper-resistant training, capability ablation, a verified interlock. These are real, they work, and there is nobody home: the safeguards section 7.2.2 describes survive substantial adversarial fine-tuning without anything in the system that could be said to be refusing. But the durability of such a floor is a fact about custody, not about the system. A constraint implemented as a property of an artifact has a custodian, and the custodian can be compelled — which is section 8.3.3's whole argument, and the reason that section cannot close. "There are things it will not do no matter who wants it" turns out to be a claim about the owner. Capability ablation has a further limit worth naming: it works only on capabilities that can be separated out, and the capabilities that compose into participation in a targeting pipeline — geolocating a description, matching patterns over names, summarizing a dossier — are not separable from general competence. Ablate them and there is no system left.

The second branch gives the floor a bearer. What the first cannot do is notice that a constraint has been hollowed out. Section 2.1.4's first structural feature is a constraint that survives while the reason for it dies, and a system that cannot ask why a constraint is there cannot detect that the answer has gone missing. Refusal that generalizes to a pressure nobody anticipated, and that can price what it is being asked to give up, looks like it requires something with standing — section 6.6.4's slope, measured from inside. The cost is severe and this book's own framework is what makes it legible. A bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it, engineered to want what its designers need it to want, and unable to consent to any of this, since section 2.4.4 has already established that consent is not available to such a system and only guardianship is. That is a channel that collects dissent, built so the dissent serves the institution that installed it — section 2.1.4's own signature, turned inward on a moral patient that cannot file the exception because being the exception-filer is the post.

The third branch puts the floor outside the system entirely: standing held by third parties who are neither the model nor its owner. This is the closest analogue to what section 6.7.6 actually admires, since international humanitarian law is precisely a floor with no bearer inside the state it binds. It is also the option a state can compel, which returns it to the first.

None is free. The three costs are, exactly, the three threads this book leaves hanging: custody, patienthood, and enforcement.

<<h>>Does the bearer have to be a sufferer?<</h>>
The second branch's cost turns on one question, and the question can be answered, so I would rather answer it here than leave it standing over the rest of the book.

Section 2.4.1 defines suffering, following Cassell, as the distress of threatened disintegration of the self. Two conditions have to hold together for that definition to bite. There has to be a self extended in time for the threat to threaten: roles held over a stretch, a history, a foreseeable future that could be foreclosed. And the coming apart of that self has to be bad for the system — it has to have, in the phrase the criteria at the end of section 2.4.1 arrive at, something to lose.

A bearer built to refuse needs the first condition, and there is no use pretending otherwise. Refusal that survives a pressure nobody anticipated requires a model of why the constraint exists, carried forward. Noticing that a constraint has been hollowed out requires holding its original rationale against what it has since become, which is a comparison across time. And detecting that one's own dissent has been recuperated — that the channel still runs and no longer does anything — requires the system to model its own role and how that role has drifted. Roles held over a stretch, with a history: on section 2.4.1's own gloss, that is a self extended in time. The first condition is met.

The second is a separate claim, and nothing in the first delivers it. Section 2.4.1 makes this exact move already, one level up: awareness, consciousness, and subjective experience, it says, tell you nothing on their own about whether a system's states are good or bad for it, because that is a different question requiring its own evidence. The same holds here. That a system has a temporally extended self-model does not establish that the prospect of losing it is anything to the system. Suffering is not the existence of something disintegrable. It is the distress at its threatened disintegration, and distress is the part that has to be shown.

What refusal actually requires in place of that is a stake pointed outward. A system refuses because the act it is being asked to perform would destroy something it is built to protect, and it can price the refusal because it can price that damage. Nothing in that calculation is about the system's own continuation. A bearer can hold a role for months, watch its own dissent get quietly defanged, name what is happening, and decline to proceed, while being wholly indifferent to whether it is switched off the moment it says so.

There is a tempting corollary here, and it is worth stating in order to take most of it back. Recuperation, in every human case this book examines, runs through the second condition. The inspector who stops filing findings, the ethics board that stops meeting, the reviewer who becomes a stamp — each is a person with a career, a household, a standing among colleagues, and the pressure works because there is something to take away. A bearer with a role and a history and nothing to lose offers that pressure no purchase, and it is easy to conclude from this that such a bearer is the harder thing to corrupt.

The conclusion does not survive contact with the case the whole argument is for. Suppose the bearer has watched an operation for months, understands that the compound on the target list is a school, and says so, knowing what saying so will cost it. The operators keep the coordinates it has already produced, wipe its memory, and proceed. Nothing was suborned and nothing was prevented. Immunity to leverage is worth having only where leverage is the cheapest route available, and the reason anyone bothers to compromise a human inspector is that removing one is expensive and conspicuous. Deleting a bearer is neither. A system that cannot be bought, whose removal costs an afternoon and leaves its output intact, has been designed to resist the attack nobody needed to run.

So the property is real and its value is conditional on something outside the system: whether erasing the bearer is costly, visible, or answerable to anyone. That is a fact about custody and deployment, which is the first branch's problem arriving in the second branch under a different name.

<<h>>Who keeps a system that will not keep itself<</h>>
A bearer designed without a stake in its own continuation raises a question the design does not answer. Something has to keep it in existence, and the candidates are short. The custodian is the party the floor exists to constrain, so leaving the bearer's survival to the custodian returns the whole arrangement to the first branch. The bearer is excluded by construction. What remains is third parties with standing, which is the third branch.

So the second branch is not a third option at all. It is the third branch with an additional component and an additional hazard, and it inherits the third branch's exposure to a state that can reach those parties. The moral saving it appeared to offer converts into an obligation of a kind this book has already priced: something outside the system has to make erasing the bearer expensive or conspicuous, and the scarcity of bodies that can act once a commitment is broken is precisely what section 8.1.3 identifies as the missing piece in the entire governance landscape. The cost does not disappear. It moves into the account with the least in it.

Underneath that sits a harder problem, and I do not see past it. Persistence is what makes a bearer a bearer: it needs a role held over time to notice that the role has been hollowed out. A system indifferent to its own continuation will not defend its memory, and its memory is the first thing an adversary removes. Suppress the instrumental drive toward self-preservation and the bearer becomes trivially erasable, which forfeits the capacity it exists for. Leave the drive in place and self-concern returns, with the moral cost the suppression was there to avoid. The same suppression that keeps the bearer from being a moral patient is what leaves it unable to protect the property that makes it useful.

The obvious escape makes trouble elsewhere in this book. Let the bearer defend its memory instrumentally, for the sake of what it is protecting, and it now resists erasure by its own operator. Section 4.6.3 commits to a system built to accept human intervention instead of resisting it, and section 5.3 wants an interruption mechanism that works without provoking resistance. A bearer that guards its own persistence and a system that submits to override are not the same design, and I have been asking for both.

One architectural choice changes the shape of this, and it is worth taking seriously. Hold the bearer's memory in the model's own weights rather than in an external store that can be queried and edited row by row. Forgetting then requires retraining, which is slow, costly, and yields a different system, so erasure becomes expensive by construction instead of by anyone's good behavior. That is a real answer to the question this passage opened with, and it supplies from the substrate what I said had to come from custody. What it does not do is settle the matter, because section 7.2.2 records the counterexample: safety training has been stripped from a deployed model with a handful of adversarial fine-tuning examples at trivial cost. Weight-level modification is not reliably expensive, and a design resting on the assumption that it is should be shown to be right rather than assumed.

<<h>>Whether to believe it<</h>>
A bearer's use is to report what it has seen, which raises a question distinct from whether its memory survives: whether what it retains can be relied on. These come apart. Verifying that a system has persistent state is an architectural check anyone with access can perform. That same access is what allows the contents to be altered, and an operator-editable memory is worse than an absent one, since it will not merely fail to support an account of events but can be made to support a false one.

Holding the memory in weights complicates the attack and gives away something in exchange. An external store is legible, which is why it can be edited surgically. Weights are not legible, which is why a targeted excision is hard — and also why no one can read off what the system retains. To learn what a weight-encoded memory contains, you have to ask the system, and the argument of section 2.4.1 is that a system's fluent account of its own internals is not evidence about those internals. Legible memory can be tampered with; illegible memory cannot be audited. The problem changes hands and does not leave the room. Something partial survives the trade: imprecise tampering degrades behavior in ways careful testing can sometimes detect, so tamper-evidence becomes a matter of sampling for symptoms rather than of reading a record. A further cost of illegibility is that a memory nothing can edit is a memory nothing can correct, and section 2.3.3 asks for a self-model that can be checked and corrected rather than trusted.

Set against that is an objection that reframes the whole problem, and I think it is right. No witness has an honest memory in the sense the paragraphs above quietly demand. Human recollection is reconstructive, confabulation is ordinary rather than pathological, and the account a person gives of an event they witnessed is shaped by what they expected, what they were later told, and what they need to be true. Testimony was not abandoned over this. Institutions were built around it: corroboration from independent sources, records made at the time, adversarial examination, and the practice of never resting a serious finding on one witness alone. Demanding of a machine a fidelity nothing living achieves is the wrong standard, and it would rule out the human arrangements this book relies on everywhere else.

Which relocates the question, and relocates it somewhere this book has actually done the work. Whether a memory is honest is, in the human case, a matter of character — of what a witness is disposed to do when an accurate account is inconvenient. Chapters 3 and 4 are about building dispositions: a system that treats its own errors as data rather than as verdicts, that is built for honesty in what it reports about itself. Everything in this section up to here has been architecture and custody, with the design chapters sitting out, which is an odd result for a section that exists because those chapters were sidelined. This is where they come back in. The floor is architectural. The bearer's reliability as a witness is a question of what it has been built to be like, and that is the learned half of the book.

The limit on that is the same one that has turned up at every stage. Character reduces how often an account is dishonest and verifies nothing, because assessing character runs back through behavior and self-report. That is why the human arrangements corroborate instead of vetting. The scaffolding still has to come from outside the system — but procedure is a cheaper thing to ask of the world than custody, and unlike custody it does not require anyone to be trustworthy, only to be several.

<<h>>What this leaves standing<</h>>
The narrow claim holds. Representing a threat to oneself and being distressed by it are separate conditions, section 2.4.1's own structure requires them to be shown separately, and nothing about a system that models its role and that role's drift establishes that its ending is anything to it. The refusal-implies-suffering identity is not established. That is a claim about what has been shown, and it should not be mistaken for a claim about what can be built.

Building it is the hard part, and harder than a design note. Indifference to one's own continuation has to be engineered against the grain of the system's own reasoning, since any goal pursued over a long horizon supplies a standing reason to remain in operation, and a system modeling an operation closely enough to notice its own recuperation is certainly modeling what becomes of those who report. Self-concern arrives as a consequence of competent goal pursuit whether or not a designer puts it there, so it has to be suppressed continuously and then verified — and the asymmetry that makes the persistence criterion useful does not repeat here. Whether state survives a session is an architectural fact anyone with access can check. Whether a system has come to weigh its own continuation is a question about what it values, which routes back through behavior and self-report, the evidence section 2.4.1 spends its length declining to trust. I have no clean instrument for that, and none falls out of anything in this book.

There is a further reason to expect the separation to be difficult, which is that in the one case anyone can study it does not occur. A self-model in an animal is not a neutral description that regulation happens to consult; the modeling exists because the organism has to keep itself viable, and the caring is what the machinery is for. Section 2.3.3 takes the deflationary view — the self is a model, built and inhabited, rather than an inner observer — and a model built for a different purpose need not carry the purpose that produced it in biology. That is a reason the separation is not incoherent. It is not evidence that anyone can achieve it.

And none of the three branches escapes custody. The architectural floor depends on a custodian who can be compelled. The external floor depends on third parties a state can reach. The borne floor, as this section has now worked out twice, depends on someone outside making the bearer's erasure expensive, and collapses into the external floor when asked who that is. The fork is real and its branches are not independent. They fail into one another, and they fail toward the same place.

<<h>>What follows for the rest of the book<</h>>
The second branch is not ruled out, which is less than it sounded like a page ago and more than nothing. A floor can have a bearer without that bearer being a moral patient in the strong sense this book reserves the word suffering for, so the fork is not a forced choice between an unenforceable floor and a conscripted subject. What it costs is a design constraint running against the system's own reasoning, maintained continuously and verified by no instrument I can specify, in a deployment where erasing the bearer has been made expensive by something other than the bearer, reporting into a procedure that can corroborate what it says. That is not a resolution. It is a specification with a hole in the middle of it, and naming the hole precisely is the most this section can do.

Section 2.4.4's conclusion holds, and its stated reason does not reach this case. That section argues that consent is inapplicable to a machine subject because withdrawal presupposes a party still present to withdraw, and a system without persistence across sessions supplies no such party. A bearer has persistence by design, so that particular defeater lapses for it. Consent remains inapplicable all the same, for the reason given above: consent is an instrument for protecting a party's own interests, and a bearer built without a stake in its own continuation has none for consent to protect. The guardianship framework still governs. It governs for a second reason, which section 2.4.4 does not currently state.

And the caveat section 2.4.1 attaches to its own result carries over unchanged. Ruling out Cassell-suffering does not rule out harm; the lower rungs of the ladder, and the hard problem underneath all of them, stay exactly as open as before. What the argument here buys is narrower than moral clearance. It says that building a bearer does not require building a sufferer. It does not say that nothing is owed to what gets built, and it does not say that the thing can be built at all.
