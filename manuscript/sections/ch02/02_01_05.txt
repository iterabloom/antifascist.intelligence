2.1.5. The Floor Beneath Learned Values
Three times in this book, working on unrelated problems, I arrive at the same requirement and do not stop to notice it.

Section 4.6.3, on how much latitude an autonomous system should have, concludes that no amount of well-designed autonomy substitutes for a genuine override — a human able to intervene, and a system built to accept the intervention rather than resist it. Section 6.7.6, working through what a vendor owes when its model reaches a targeting pipeline, concludes that what makes a system antifascist is not that it tracks what people want, but that there are things it will not do to a person no matter who wants it. Section 8.1.3, counting up a decade of voluntary commitments, concludes that a commitment nobody can enforce is a different kind of object from one somebody can.

Those are three descriptions of one thing: a constraint that does not bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to the party holding the system. Call it a floor.

The trouble is that section 2.1.1 has already dealt with the family of ethical theories that puts unconditional constraints first, and dealt with it briefly — deontological approaches were noted, credited with holding a line regardless of outcome, and set aside on the grounds that duties conflict and rules do not cover every case. Then this book spends chapters 3 and 4, close to a third of its length, on learned judgment: reinforcement learning, learning from demonstration, mentorship, play, moral apprenticeship, the growth mindset. The design chapters build the thing chapter 6 and chapter 8 say is insufficient, having already dismissed the thing they say is necessary. The "hybrid approach" section 2.1 gestures at is where the resolution was supposed to live, and nothing was ever put there.

Here is what belongs there. An unconditional floor of things the system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate preference, with learned judgment operating above it and never beneath it. Deontology's standing objection — that rules conflict and run out — is an objection to rules deciding every case, and it has no force against a floor that decides a deliberately small number of things and leaves the rest to judgment. The rigidity that is a defect in a complete moral theory is the entire point of a floor.

That reframing does real work in this book. It gives section 2.1's hybrid approach actual content. It moves the tamper-resistance research in section 7.2.2 from an appendix topic to the center, because if there is a floor then everything turns on whether it holds. And it converts the antifascist question from a matter of what a system values into two much harder and more answerable questions: what is in the floor, and who can edit it.

<<h>>Three ways to build one, and what each costs<</h>>
The second question is where this gets difficult, and I do not have a resolution to offer. What I have is a fork with three branches and a cost on each, and the honest thing is to set them out rather than pick the one that reads best.

The first branch puts the floor in the architecture, with nothing inside that holds it. Constrained optimization, tamper-resistant training, capability ablation, a verified interlock. These are real, they work, and there is nobody home: the safeguards section 7.2.2 describes survive substantial adversarial fine-tuning without anything in the system that could be said to be refusing. But the durability of such a floor is a fact about custody, not about the system. A constraint implemented as a property of an artifact has a custodian, and the custodian can be compelled — which is section 8.3.3's whole argument, and the reason that section cannot close. "There are things it will not do no matter who wants it" turns out to be a claim about the owner. Capability ablation has a further limit worth naming: it works only on capabilities that can be separated out, and the capabilities that compose into participation in a targeting pipeline — geolocating a description, matching patterns over names, summarizing a dossier — are not separable from general competence. Ablate them and there is no system left.

The second branch gives the floor a bearer. What the first cannot do is notice that a constraint has been hollowed out. Section 2.1.4's first structural feature is a constraint that survives while the reason for it dies, and a system that cannot ask why a constraint is there cannot detect that the answer has gone missing. Refusal that generalizes to a pressure nobody anticipated, and that can price what it is being asked to give up, looks like it requires something with standing — section 6.6.4's slope, measured from inside. The cost is severe and this book's own framework is what makes it legible. A bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it, engineered to want what its designers need it to want, and unable to consent to any of this, since section 2.4.4 has already established that consent is not available to such a system and only guardianship is. That is a channel that collects dissent, built so the dissent serves the institution that installed it — section 2.1.4's own signature, turned inward on a moral patient that cannot file the exception because being the exception-filer is the post.

The third branch puts the floor outside the system entirely: standing held by third parties who are neither the model nor its owner. This is the closest analogue to what section 6.7.6 actually admires, since international humanitarian law is precisely a floor with no bearer inside the state it binds. It is also the option a state can compel, which returns it to the first.

None is free. The three costs are, exactly, the three threads this book leaves hanging: custody, patienthood, and enforcement.

<<h>>What I am not resolving<</h>>
One line of escape is worth naming, because it is the one I would take if I could defend it. This book keeps three things apart that are usually run together: persistence, a self-model, and the narrative self that section 2.4.1 borrows from Cassell to define suffering on. Bearing a floor plausibly needs the first and arguably the second. Whether it needs the third is not obvious, and the answer decides whether the second branch costs a moral patient or merely something that looks like one. A system with state that survives a session, a self-model good enough to price the pressure it is under, and no biography whose foreclosure could be held over it is at least describable. Whether it is coherent I do not know, and it is the question the persistence criterion is best placed to ask, since persistence is the one rung on that ladder checkable from outside.

I will not pretend the first branch escapes this by declining to build a bearer. Any floor robust enough to survive retraining pressure lives in whatever persists across sessions, and section 2.4.1 already grants that weights persist where conversations do not. The persistence is present either way. What the first branch avoids is not the property; it is the claim that anything possesses it.

So this section ends where section 2.1.4 ends, and for the same reason. The collision is real, the convenient resolution is not available, and the specification goes forward rather than getting closed here by assertion. Chapters 3 through 8 build above the floor. What the floor is made of, and who holds the pen that can rewrite it, is the question this book hands to whoever takes it up next.
