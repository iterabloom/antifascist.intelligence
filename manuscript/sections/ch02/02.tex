\chapter{The Refusal That Withheld Nothing}
\label{sec:2}
In February 2026 a vendor refused to amend the terms under which its model ran on the Pentagon's classified networks \autocite{cnn2026phaseout}. On 24 February the department demanded that two prohibitions, on domestic mass surveillance and on fully autonomous weapons, be replaced by a licence for all lawful use, with an answer due on the 27th \autocite{defenseone2026ultimatum,volokh2026anthropiccontract}. The vendor refused publicly on the 26th \autocite{volokh2026anthropiccontract}. On the 27th the President ordered federal agencies to stop using its products, and the Secretary directed that it be designated a supply-chain risk \autocite{npr2026openai}. That evening a rival announced an agreement to put its own models on the department's classified network \autocite{cnbc2026openai}. The war began the next morning, with the refused vendor's model still inside the targeting platform that put the school at Minab on the first day's list \autocite{crs2026iranwar,livingstone2026maven,gizmodo2026minab}. On 12 March the chief executive of the company that builds the platform said the model was still in use \autocite{cnbc2026karp}. A week later some of the department's staff were reported to be slow-rolling its replacement because they were using it to build workflows, and the platform's own prompts and workflows had been built with the vendor's coding tool \autocite{militarytimes2026removal}. The department's removal deadline was reset once, by a reissued memorandum, to 29 September, having started from a six-month clock that would have run out in late August \autocite{breakingdefense2026removal}. On 10 September the department's technology chief said about 90 per cent of the work had moved off the model, that the platform's work had moved months before, and that the rest would follow by the end of the month \autocite{defensescoop2026michael}. An appeals court upheld the separate exclusion under a second statute on 25 September \autocite{anthropic2026dowdccir}.

So the refusal that opened the dispute withheld nothing from the pipeline. The vendor declined two uses the pipeline didn't claim to perform, and its model went on performing the uses the pipeline did. That is a finding with a date attached: a term drafted over autonomy misses a pipeline that keeps one person and a stamp. A refusal that could have occurred is what the rest of this book designs for.

\runin{Not all vendor commitments are the same kind}

Anthropic refused to permit fully autonomous targeting and mass domestic surveillance, held that line against threatened contract termination and a supply-chain-risk designation, and litigated \autocite{crs2026pentagon} — a commitment with a price attached, and paid. 

But what was the line held on? The vendor's own statement gives the reason for the autonomous-weapons refusal: fully autonomous weapons may prove critical for national defense, but today's frontier systems are not reliable enough to power them \autocite{Amodei2026DoWStatement}. That refusal would lift once reliability improves. Against a floor that says machines shouldn't select people for lethal attack, reliability is the wrong reason to lift; against one that says attacks must discriminate lawfully, it may be the right one. Which rationale a prohibition serves is a drafting question, and the vendor's term didn't answer it. The surveillance refusal in the same statement rests on different ground — that in a narrow set of cases AI can undermine rather than defend democratic values — and that one does not lift with capability. By outcome: the commitment persisted; prevention failed at review and in the drafting, since the term covered autonomy and missed the acts the pipeline performed; detection was absent; no remedy has reached anyone.

And the refusal stood below a prior question. The vendor declined full autonomy and domestic surveillance. It didn't decline to support the campaign, and under the floor's force term that question comes first: whether to license a model into a use of force with no corroborated defense behind it.

In August 2026 a federal judge set aside the designation made under one statute and permanently enjoined the penalties, holding them retaliation for the company's criticism of the administration \autocite{anthropic2026dow}; in September an appeals court, two to one, upheld a parallel designation under a second statute, holding that the exclusion turned on the refused contract term rather than on the speech \autocite{anthropic2026dowdccir}. And the paperwork adds what the refusal was \emph{over}: a contract term. The company's models were already on the department's classified networks under terms that carried the two prohibitions; what it refused was the department's demand to amend them to allow all lawful use, and the designation followed the refusal \autocite{cnn2026phaseout,defenseone2026ultimatum,volokh2026anthropiccontract}. That a term of this kind has to be fought for, by one vendor, is the same finding as its absence from the others. It is also, against a state that wants the war, the only form the force term takes until the term is standard. A commitment to a use policy no outsider can check is a different object wearing the same words — the first kind can fail and be seen to fail; the second can't even be tested.
