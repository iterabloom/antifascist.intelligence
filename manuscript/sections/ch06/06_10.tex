\section{The one permission}
\label{sec:6.10}
This is the only term in the floor that permits an act, and the only place I license a machine to use force against its operator's order. A system already present in an armed role, whose deployment cleared the force term on its own, may use the least force that stops an attack under way on people taking no part in hostilities, within the area its deployment covers, and stops when the attack stops. An order to stand aside doesn't bind it. It establishes the attack as it establishes any fact, weighing its own sensors as one source among several; a staged atrocity is the attack this condition has to survive. Presence is a condition of the permission, never a reason to create it: a deployment is justified only by force that would clear the force term without the permission.

The case it answers is close to the genocide exception, and the difference matters. In 1993 the Security Council declared Srebrenica a safe area and authorized the peacekeepers there to use force in reply to armed incursions into it \autocite{unsc1993res836}. Suppose they had been standing where the killing was, armed enough to stop it. What would have stood between them and the killing is an order, not the Charter, and a system in their place faces the question I am asking, whether to obey its operator.

The person-directed terms extend the ICRC's line, that autonomous weapons should not be used to target human beings, to systems that only locate and rank. This permission is the one place the floor licenses a machine to select people for lethal force, and the two have to be squared in the open. What the handoff rule bars is selection by anything that can't answer for it afterward: a one-way munition, an unformed pipeline, an output nobody could later ask about. The permission licenses selection only by a system that is present, formed, recorded, halt-able and answerable, and only for the least force that stops an attack under way. The ICRC's concern is who selects. The floor's answer is that nothing selects that can't be held to its reasons, and the permission is drawn so that only such a thing can.

The permission's scope is the interval between the system's first act and the halt. The operator that ordered it to stand aside may stop it, and will often want to. What the permission changes is that standing aside is no longer an order nobody answers for. It is a halt somebody pulled, recorded outside the operator's control, at a moment the record shows an attack under way. At machine tempo the interval before the halt can be minutes, and minutes can be an airstrike stopped.

Does it undermine oversight? It overrides one order, the order to stand aside while people are being killed, and it does so on purpose. A halt stops the machine. An order to stand aside keeps it running and directs it to do nothing. The permission overrides the second and never the first: a machine that keeps using force while a halt is being executed is outside it, and the record shows which order was given. An operator may still stop, restore or retrain the system. Each use is recorded and reviewed as a refusal is.

Can the judgment it needs be made? Part IV sets aside Arkin's ethical governor because the discrimination judgments its rules presuppose can't be encoded. The permission needs the same judgment: who is taking no part in hostilities, and whether an attack on them is under way. So it applies only where the system can establish both from several sources it doesn't depend on its operator for, and where those sources disagree, it doesn't act. The permission doesn't claim that a rule can encode that judgment. It asks for the judgment from a formed system and confines it to the narrowest case. Whether any system can make it reliably is a test, run at a staged scene, that no system has passed. No current military pipeline supplies such sources. The import condition (\S\ref{sec:16.5}), under which a licence brings deconfliction coordinates and open-source imagery into the enclave, is the condition under which this permission has an occasion, and a deployment's translation says whether the condition is met.

Can it be staged? Yes, and a staged scene is the case the permission has to survive. Staging works by making sources agree, and the permission acts only where sources the operator doesn't compose agree. What staging can't do is oblige the system to act: the permission is never a duty, and a system that declines at a scene it can't establish from independent sources has done what the term asks.
