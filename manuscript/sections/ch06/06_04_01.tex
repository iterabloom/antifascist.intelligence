\subsection{Authoritarian Applications and the Commercial Supplier}
\label{sec:6.4.1}
Authoritarian misuse of AI is not one problem but several, spread across surveillance, censorship, opinion manipulation, and infrastructure control. Six forms of it are documented well enough to name specifically:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Algorithmic social scoring: using AI-driven analytics to track and rank individual behavior against government-defined standards of “trustworthiness,” with rewards or restrictions attached to the result. China's system is the most-cited example, though in practice it is a patchwork of court blacklists, tax-compliance ratings, and local pilot programs rather than the single national score it is often described as \autocite{drinhausen2021chinas}.
  \item Mass biometric surveillance: deploying facial recognition at population scale to track a targeted group. Authorities in Xinjiang built a platform that pulls data from checkpoints, cameras, and phone scans into one system built to flag anyone who falls outside a computed behavioral baseline, and used it to monitor the region's Uighur population \autocite{humanrightswatch2019chinas}.
  \item Psychographic micro-targeting: building detailed behavioral profiles from personal data to target propaganda or manipulate opinion at the individual level. Cambridge Analytica's harvesting of Facebook data to build voter profiles for the 2016 US presidential campaign, and its work around the Brexit referendum, is the case that made the technique widely known, though how much either vote was actually affected remains disputed \autocite{cadwalladr2018revealed}.
  \item Automated censorship: using AI-driven content moderation to suppress dissent at a scale no human review team could match. China's Great Firewall is the standing example: automated classifiers now sit alongside its original keyword- and IP-blocking infrastructure, scoring and throttling content at machine speed \autocite{hoang2021great}.
  \item Synthetic disinformation: generating deepfake audio or video to manufacture events that never happened. Two days before Slovakia's 2023 parliamentary election, a fabricated audio recording appeared to catch an opposition leader discussing vote-rigging with a journalist; it spread during the country's mandatory pre-election media silence, when no outlet was legally permitted to debunk it before voting began \autocite{denadal2024beyond}.
  \item Repurposed dual-use systems: turning AI infrastructure built for one purpose toward surveillance or population control instead — a recommendation algorithm designed to hold attention retuned to amplify a regime's preferred narrative, an image-recognition system built for one task redirected to track protesters.
\end{enumerate}
Each of these needs a different countermeasure.

\runin{The party missing from that list}
Read the six together and a pattern emerges. Wherever a builder is named it is an authoritarian state, wherever a democracy appears it is the target, and what is left to the democracies is to regulate. That taxonomy is comfortable and it is wrong.

The most consequential AI targeting systems yet deployed were built by democracies. They were built by states with elections, opposition parties, adversarial press, and functioning courts — states that meet every institutional criterion section~\ref{sec:10.2} uses to define a democratic dividend. Those institutions did not prevent the systems, and in the main they have not stopped them since.

\begin{esbox}
\boxtitle{AI targeting in the Israeli campaign in Gaza, as reported 2023–2026}
Investigative reporting in +972 Magazine and Local Call, corroborated by subsequent accounts in the New York Review of Books and the Washington Post, described three systems working together.

Habsora — “The Gospel” — generated infrastructure and building targets. Sources told reporters that the majority of its suggestions were private residences.

Lavender scored individuals by estimated likelihood of militant affiliation and produced kill lists; as many as 37,000 Palestinians were marked. Its outputs were understood inside the unit to be wrong about ten percent of the time — that is, roughly one in ten people it named was not a member of the armed group it was supposed to identify.

A third system tracked when flagged individuals returned home, so a strike could be timed to when they were with their families.

What matters is the human review these systems were paired with. One officer described the work: “I would invest twenty seconds for each target at this stage, and do dozens of them every day.” And then, without being asked: “I had zero added value as a human, apart from being a stamp of approval.” \autocite{abraham2024lavender}

\end{esbox}
Twenty seconds is what “a human makes the final call” measures out to under operational tempo. It is the whole of the safeguard, and I would ask any reader who finds the safeguards in this chapter reassuring to hold that number against them.

Removing the bottleneck was the stated objective. The epigraph to this chapter is the case for it, made by the commander of Israel's signals-intelligence unit under a pseudonym: machines to resolve the human bottleneck in both finding targets and approving them \autocite{ys2021humanmachine}. Meaningful human control is the property that sentence was written to remove.

\begin{esbox}
\boxtitle{The Maven Smart System, as reported through mid-2026}
The American analog is a targeting platform built by Palantir under a Department of Defense contract awarded at \$480 million and raised the following year to a ceiling near \$1.3 billion running through 2029. A partnership announced in late 2024 between Anthropic, Palantir and Amazon Web Services put Anthropic's Claude models inside Palantir's accredited classified environment for defense and intelligence use, and reporting on the campaign below describes Claude operating within this platform. Section~\ref{sec:10.3} works out what a published limit on what a vendor will not permit is worth when nobody is logging how the model is used downstream. That argument and this deployment sit oddly together.

During the Iran campaign the system was credited by military officials with generating and ranking targets at a rate no staff process could match. More than one thousand targets were struck in the first twenty-four hours, which officials put at roughly ten times the rate before the platform. By the ceasefire thirty-eight days later the figure given was thirteen thousand targets, with peak consumption around twenty billion tokens a day.

Palantir's public position on responsibility was given by its head for the United Kingdom and Europe, in remarks reported secondhand: “This is not our role to decide life or death,” and AI platforms “have been instrumental to the management of the conflict, but responsibility always remains with the military organization.” \autocite{lee2026palantir}

\end{esbox}
It is not obviously wrong. A vendor does not select targets, and a rule making software suppliers liable for their customers' decisions would be unworkable. The problem is what it leaves standing: if the operator is too far down the chain to be answerable, the commander approved a list they had twenty seconds a name to read, and the vendor is not in the chain at all, then the compression of the decision has removed the decision-maker without removing the decision. Who that leaves answerable is the question section~\ref{sec:10.3} is left with, and section~\ref{sec:5.4.2} takes up why a democracy's own apparatus is a harder and more useful case than any of the six above.

None of this makes the six forms less real. It makes the taxonomy incomplete in a way that matters for design: a system built on the assumption that authoritarian misuse is something that happens elsewhere, to be watched for and regulated against, will not be built to resist the use its own government has in mind for it.
