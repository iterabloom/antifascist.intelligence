A system can have a moral sense and still fail badly — because it was trained on skewed data, because it breaks in situations its training never covered, because its own incentives point somewhere other than where its designers intended, or because someone with power over it decides to point it at people rather than for them.

The epigraph is a design document rather than a critique, and section~\ref{sec:6.4.1} shows what it looks like once it has been implemented.

Racial capitalism — the political theorist Cedric Robinson's term for capitalism's tendency to accumulate by differentiating and devaluing along racial lines \autocite{robinson1983black} — is the pattern a biased hiring model, a discriminatory credit score, and a skewed policing algorithm reproduce when they automate the historical data those hierarchies generated; the sociologist Ruha Benjamin calls the result the New Jim Code, discrimination that runs faster and reads as neutral because a machine is doing it \autocite{benjamin2019race}.

Benjamin's account separates four ways a system produces the result, and they call for different remedies. Engineered inequity builds the hierarchy in deliberately. Default discrimination reproduces it by ignoring it, which is the failure most of this chapter's cases are. Coded exposure is the double bind of being harmed by a system that cannot see you and harmed again by one that can — the face-recognition case, where the fix for a system that fails on dark skin is a system that identifies dark-skinned faces reliably for whoever is looking. And technological benevolence is the reflexive one: designs that aim to fix bias and deepen it, because the framing that treats the problem as technical is the framing the fix inherits.

A system built to correct for the skew in its training data, audited for the disparities it produces, and answerable to the people it scores works against that pattern; a system built without those commitments works for it by default.

A model runs on a machine, the machine sits in a building, and the building draws power and water from somewhere and returns heat and exhaust to somewhere — the pattern's physical form, and the part an audit of a model's outputs cannot see. Where those buildings go is a decision, taken on the price of land and power and the speed of a permit, which is also a description of the places least able to refuse one.

\begin{esbox}
\boxtitle{What the computation runs on, 2024-2026}
The International Energy Agency put data-center electricity use at about 415 terawatt-hours in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of world consumption, growing about 12 percent a year since 2017 — more than four times the growth rate of electricity demand as a whole — and projected to reach around 945 terawatt-hours by 2030 \autocite{iea2025energyai}. Google reported that 87 percent of its freshwater withdrawal in 2025 came from sources at low or medium risk of depletion or scarcity, which is the company's own way of recording where the remainder came from \autocite{google2026environmental}. In April 2026 the NAACP, represented by the Southern Environmental Law Center and Earthjustice, sued xAI in federal court over the gas turbines powering a data center near Memphis, alleging that 27 of them were run without an air permit near homes, schools, and churches, and that the plant could emit more than 1,700 tons of nitrogen oxides a year \autocite{naacp2026xai}.

\end{esbox}
What makes this the same pattern and not a separate environmental complaint is the accounting. The benefit of the computation is realized wherever the model is sold. The cost is borne within a few miles of the turbines, by residents who are not parties to the transaction and whose consent was not among the inputs to the siting decision. A system can be calibrated across every demographic group its outputs touch and still have been assembled this way, which no audit of its outputs would reach: nothing in the model's behavior records it. This gives a government leverage.
