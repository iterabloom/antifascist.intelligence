6.2.1. Engaging Diverse Stakeholders in AI Development

An AI system designed by one kind of person, inside one kind of institution, tends to reproduce that person's blind spots at scale. Bringing in people with different expertise, backgrounds, and stakes in the outcome is what catches a problem before deployment.

<<list>>
1. Cross-sector research collaboration. Funding joint work across industry, academia, and civil society surfaces problems no single sector would catch alone. The University of Cambridge's Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, launched in 2016 with a £10 million grant from the Leverhulme Trust, brings together philosophers, machine-learning researchers, and scholars from fields including history, engineering, and media studies to study AI's effects on society [[cite:C0471]]. Section 6.7.6 covers the Partnership on AI, the industry consortium built for cross-sector coordination at a larger scale.

2. Inclusive design workshops. Putting ethicists, social scientists, and members of an affected community in the same room as the engineers building a system catches a problem while it is still a prototype. Data & Society, a research institute studying the social implications of data-centric technology, runs workshops built for exactly this, including its AI.Assembly Workshop, which brings together artists, academics, technologists, and humanists of color to work through how AI systems affecting their communities get designed and governed [[cite:C0472]].

3. Ethics advisory boards. A review board only works if it can say no to something. IBM created its AI Ethics Board after publishing three company-wide AI principles in 2018; the board, co-chaired since 2019, is a cross-company body that reviews how IBM builds and uses AI and rules on the hard cases against those principles [[cite:C0473]].

4. Community-driven development. A system built with the people who will use it, rather than for them, is more likely to fit the problem they actually have. That means treating local knowledge as a design input, not a focus-group afterthought, especially where a system is meant to serve a community outside the one that built it.
<</list>>

Section 6.2.2 takes up the fifth piece directly: bringing the public itself, not just the organizations that claim to represent it, into AI governance.
