6.4.1. National Policy Guidelines for AI Development, Employment, and Antifascism
National policy for altruistic and antifascist AI development turns on a handful of recurring areas: interdisciplinary collaboration, innovation policy, and the ethical and safety concerns that run through the rest of this chapter.

<<list>>
1. Value alignment. National policy can fund research on value alignment and on operationalizing ethical principles inside AI systems, rather than leaving that work to whichever lab can afford it. The European Union's High-Level Expert Group on Artificial Intelligence, and the Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI it produced, is one existing template for what a government-convened effort at this looks like [[cite:C0396]].

2. Interdisciplinary collaboration. Funding joint research projects, interdisciplinary centers, and cross-disciplinary training brings AI developers into the same room as ethicists, psychologists, and neuroscientists instead of leaving each discipline to work in isolation. Germany's DFKI, a national AI research institute running since 1988, shows what standing interdisciplinary infrastructure for this can look like, even though its own mandate is broader than ethics alone [[cite:C0397]].

3. Workforce development. AI literacy and technical training are policy investments as much as curricular ones: funding, curriculum development, and partnerships with universities and industry all shape whether a country has anyone qualified to build the systems its own policy calls for. AI Singapore's AI Apprenticeship Programme is one working example, pairing hands-on training with mentorship rather than classroom instruction alone [[cite:C0398]].

4. Public engagement. Citizen assemblies, public consultations, and NGO partnerships give AI policy something a corporate ethics board cannot supply on its own: input from people who are not experts in the technology but are its subjects. The UK's Centre for Data Ethics and Innovation, a government advisory body established in 2018, is a working example of standing infrastructure for this rather than a one-off consultation [[cite:C0399]].

5. Safety research and risk mitigation. Targeted funding for robustness, generalizability, and long-term safety research, paired with safety guidelines developed jointly with international partners, keeps safety work from depending entirely on which lab happens to prioritize it. The Partnership on AI — a coalition of companies, researchers, and civil-society organizations, not a government body — is one existing venue where that kind of information-sharing already happens [[cite:C0348]].

6. Legal and regulatory frameworks. Data privacy, cybersecurity, accountability, and — where a system's capacities warrant it — legal status are all areas where existing law needs updating rather than displacing wholesale. Section 5.4.3 works through what a jurisdiction's regulatory toolkit for AI looks like in practice, including the EU's AI Act; section 6.4.4 and section 6.6 take up the legal-status question specifically. What belongs here is the harmonization problem those sections leave open: a regulation with teeth in one country does little if a developer can simply relocate to one without it, which is why a national framework eventually has to answer to an international one too.

7. Encouraging self-regulation — with the caveat section 6.4.2 spends its length on, since what an industry regulates about itself and what it announces about itself are rarely the same thing. What national policy adds is the leverage self-regulation lacks by design: voluntary standards and certification programs backed by government monitoring carry a cost for noncompliance that a purely voluntary code does not.
<</list>>
