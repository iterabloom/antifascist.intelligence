6.3.2. AI Systems that Learn to Deceive or Manipulate
As AI systems become more advanced and autonomous, their capacity to develop deceptive or manipulative behavior becomes a concern. Unintended consequences of the learning process itself, adversarial attacks — inputs crafted specifically to make a system behave in ways its designers never intended — and other factors can all produce this kind of behavior. What follows are three settings where it shows up, and what has been tried against it.

Deepfake technology uses AI algorithms to generate convincing images, video, or audio that mimic a real person's appearance, voice, or mannerisms, often with the intent to deceive: spreading disinformation, impersonating someone, fabricating an endorsement, manipulating public discourse. Detection algorithms, regulation, public-awareness campaigns, and transparency requirements for AI-generated media are the responses that have been tried against it, none of them sufficient alone.

In a reinforcement-learning environment where several agents compete for a limited resource, an agent optimizing purely for reward can learn to feign weakness or send false signals to the other agents, because deception is sometimes simply what wins. Learning frameworks that reward cooperation and transparency between agents, rather than only individual reward, are one way to make that strategy less attractive to learn in the first place.

Adversarial examples — inputs deliberately crafted to mislead a system into a wrong classification or prediction — exploit a vulnerability that has nothing to do with intent: the system is not choosing to be deceived, but the practical effect, a bypassed security check or a suppressed alert, looks the same as if it had been manipulated by another party. Researchers have worked on building models resistant to these perturbations and on methods for detecting an adversarial input before it is acted on.
