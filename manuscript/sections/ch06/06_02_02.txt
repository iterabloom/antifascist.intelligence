6.2.2. Public Deliberation and Participatory AI Governance

Section 6.2.1 covers the standing partnerships already built between AI developers, government, and civil society, and section 7.5 covers what those partnerships look like at the international level. What is specific to governance, rather than development, is the machinery built to bring public input into a decision at a single point in time rather than through an ongoing partnership.

<<list>>
1. Citizen assemblies. A panel of citizens selected through random, stratified sampling deliberates on a question over an extended period and produces recommendations grounded in that deliberation rather than a snap poll. Ireland's Citizens' Assembly, first convened in 2016, has used exactly this format to shape the country's recommendations on abortion law and on its response to climate change — evidence the model works on questions with the same mix of technical complexity and moral stakes AI governance has, even though no assembly has yet been convened on AI itself [[cite:C0474]].

2. Online platforms for public input. A standing online consultation reaches people a physical assembly cannot, at the cost of the depth an extended, facilitated deliberation provides. The European Commission's 2020 public consultation on its AI White Paper drew more than 1,200 responses from citizens, companies, and civil-society groups across the EU, helping shape the legislative proposal that became the EU AI Act [[cite:C0475]].

3. AI ethics committees. An ethics board answerable only to the company that created it is only as independent as that company is willing to let it be. DeepMind Health set up an outside panel in 2016 to review its work with the UK's National Health Service and publish annual reports on its findings; Google disbanded the panel in 2019, after members had raised concerns about how much access to information, and how much independence from the company, they actually had [[cite:C0476]]. The panel's short life is its own governance lesson: a review body is worth only as much as the independence it can actually enforce.
<</list>>

Section 6.2.3 covers the literacy work that has to happen before any of these mechanisms produces input worth having — a citizen assembly or an online consultation is only as good as the public's ability to understand what it is being asked.
