6.4.5. Encouraging the Development of Altruistic, Antifascist, and Ethical AI Systems
Sections 6.4.1 through 6.4.4 covered the instruments — national guidelines, self-regulation, public-private partnerships, legal status. What they are instruments for is the subject of this closing section: AI development that is altruistic and antifascist by design, not as an afterthought.

<<h>>Policies to Counteract Authoritarian Uses of AI<</h>>
Section 5.4.3 already covers the core policy toolkit for this — export controls, prohibited-practices regulation, and developer-facing standards — and section 4.6.3 covers the safeguards a system needs built in from the start. Two pieces of that toolkit are worth adding here: measurement, and a standing international body.

A government cannot restrict what it cannot see. The Carnegie Endowment's AI Global Surveillance (AIGS) Index, published in 2019, assessed 176 countries and found AI-enabled surveillance technology already in active use in at least 75 of them, documenting how far the practice had already spread [[cite:C0409]]. An index like this is not itself a countermeasure — it is the evidence base that makes the case for one, and for whichever export controls or prohibited-practices rules a jurisdiction decides its evidence justifies.

The Global Partnership on Artificial Intelligence, initiated by G7 countries in 2018 and launched in 2020, does for state-level AI policy roughly what the Partnership on AI does for industry: a standing forum rather than a one-time agreement [[cite:C0410]]. The difference is membership — GPAI's members are national governments, which is the piece section 6.4.1 already noted the Partnership on AI itself does not have.

<<h>>AI Systems That Support Democratic Values<</h>>
Section 4.4.6 already works through what this looks like with working examples — Google's Perspective API for scoring content toxicity without necessarily removing it, and Taiwan's vTaiwan process for gathering structured public input on contested policy questions — along with the caution that belongs next to both.

One gap those examples leave open: election-integrity monitoring specifically, an AI system built to flag anomalies in real-time reporting or detect coordinated interference during a vote. What would make a system like that trustworthy is the same list section 4.4.6 already gives for any AI role in democratic process — privacy-preserving design, an audit trail, and a multi-stakeholder review body that includes more than whichever party happens to hold power at the time — applied to the specific case of an election.

<<h>>International Cooperation on Shared Standards<</h>>
The case for international cooperation on shared AI ethics standards — joint research, common guidelines, treaties, capacity-building — is made at length elsewhere in this book: section 5.4.3 makes it for the antifascist case specifically and hands the general question of coordination between states to section 6.3, which is where it belongs. Restating that argument's shape here would not strengthen it.

What is worth adding is one concrete instance of it happening. UNESCO's member states adopted the Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence in November 2021, after a draft text circulated the year before — the first AI ethics standard agreed at that scale by a body with near-universal membership [[cite:C0411]]. Whether a non-binding recommendation adopted by acclamation changes what any signatory government actually does is a separate question from whether the standard exists, and the record so far answers only the second one.
