\subsection{Long-Term AI Safety Considerations}
\label{sec:6.3.5}
As capability rises, the safety question changes shape. The problem stops being an error to correct after the fact and becomes whether the values still track anything human once a system acts at a scale and speed no supervisor watches in real time.

Three pressures make the long-run version harder than the near-term one. Value alignment has to survive time, which means a system needs some way to revise its understanding of what it was built to serve without that revision becoming the route by which the commitments erode — the distinction drawn in section~\ref{sec:6.2}, at a scale where nobody is checking each step. Competition compresses the schedule: a race leaves less time for safety work than a less contested field would, and the counterweight on offer is international agreement, which section~\ref{sec:12.1.2} counts the results of. And a more capable system is a more attractive target, so a portion of what looks like misalignment will be someone else's deliberate work.

The catastrophic scenarios are usually told as control of critical infrastructure. The mechanism matters less than the shape: capability outrunning the arrangements meant to keep it answerable. Nothing about the largest version of it is different in kind from the office-sized version chapter~\ref{sec:7} describes.
