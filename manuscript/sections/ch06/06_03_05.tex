\subsection{Remediating a Harm Once It Is Identified}
\label{sec:6.3.5}
Identifying a harm and repairing it are separate problems, and nothing about the first delivers the second. In 2019 the health economist Ziad Obermeyer and coauthors found that a widely used risk-prediction algorithm, sold to hospitals across the United States by the health-services company Optum to flag patients for extra care management, was scoring Black patients as lower-risk than white patients with the same underlying disease burden. The algorithm used past healthcare spending as a proxy for medical need, and less money gets spent on Black patients at any given level of illness, so the algorithm read a spending gap as a health gap and routed care accordingly — Obermeyer estimated the bias cut by more than half the additional care the tool would otherwise have flagged Black patients to receive. Obermeyer's team then worked directly with Optum to retrain the algorithm against a different target, combining healthcare costs with a patient's documented chronic conditions rather than costs alone, which cut the disparity in diagnosed chronic conditions between Black and white patients scored at the same risk level by 84 percent \autocite{obermeyer2019dissecting}. What made the fix possible was not a better audit — the disparity had already been measured, by comparing the algorithm's scores against an independent record of how sick the patients actually were. It was that the company whose product caused the harm and the researchers who found it could still work from the same data toward a specific, measurable target.

A comparable pattern played out in remote exam-proctoring software during the shift to online testing. Proctorio's face-detection system, built on the OpenCV library, failed to detect Black students' faces in test footage up to 57 percent of the time, an investigative report found \autocite{feathers2021proctorio}. A student the system could not find anywhere in frame drew more scrutiny, not less: the tool universities had bought to catch cheating flagged the wrong students disproportionately. The remediation here did not come from Proctorio. It came from the universities that had bought the product: the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, UC Berkeley, and Baruch College dropped it in 2021, citing accessibility and bias concerns that student petitions at more than a dozen other campuses had already raised \autocite{flaherty2021illinois}. Where the healthcare case shows a vendor and the researchers who found its bias fixing the underlying model together, the proctoring case shows what happens when a vendor does not: the customers leave.

Job displacement does not have as clean a fix. The main channel the United States uses to retrain workers whose jobs automation has displaced, the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act, was evaluated by researchers Julian Jacobs and Jordan Canedy in 2026 against 23 million participation records spanning 2017 to 2023. The verdict was not encouraging: the program rarely moved a participant into work materially less exposed to automation than the job they had lost, and where it produced a wage gain, the gain traced more often to workers catching back up to their old earnings than to any real change in what kind of work they were doing. What performed better, in the same data, was not classroom retraining but employer-led apprenticeship \autocite{jacobs2026wioa}. A program built to answer that question — what happens once an identified harm needs remediation — can still fail at it even when it exists, is funded, and is genuinely trying.

A remediation can also be a single design decision applied everywhere, rather than a case-by-case fix. Messages forwarded without limit on WhatsApp carried rumors through 2017 and 2018 that preceded a string of mob killings in India — unconfirmed claims about child abductors and organ thieves circulating faster than any correction could follow them. WhatsApp's response did not try to classify and remove each rumor. It capped forwarding at five chats at a time for users in India in 2018, then extended the same limit worldwide, cutting off the mechanism the rumors depended on rather than chasing their content \autocite{whatsapp2019forwarding}. It is a narrower fix than a moderation system that judges every message — it does nothing about a rumor two people believe and repeat face to face — but it targets the part of the harm that was actually mechanical: forwarding one message to hundreds of chats at once is not what person-to-person communication looks like, whatever the message says.

Disaster response adds a constraint the other four domains above do not share: the remediation has to run on the timescale of a fire spreading or a building finishing its collapse, not the months a university's procurement review or a federal program evaluation takes. The RoboCup Rescue Simulation League tests exactly that problem, fielding coordinated teams of simulated fire-brigade, ambulance, and police agents against fire, building-collapse, and road-blockage scenarios in which any one agent's allocation choice comes at the direct cost of some other task going unaddressed. Dynamic resource allocation is built into the competition's own agent-development framework as one of five core problems entrants have to solve, alongside path-finding and information-sharing, because a system that cannot reallocate scarce agents in real time as a scenario unfolds is not being tested against anything a real disaster would demand of it \autocite{robocup2025rescue}. What the healthcare, education, and information-ecosystem cases above do at the pace of an audit or a policy change, a disaster-response system has to do inside the disaster itself.

None of the healthcare, education, or information-ecosystem remediations above ran through the guideline-setting bodies chapters~\ref{sec:9} and \ref{sec:10} catalog — the EU's Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI, the Partnership on AI's cross-industry coordination work (section~\ref{sec:9.1}, section~\ref{sec:10.4}). They ran through a research team with access to a company's own data, a set of universities exercising the pull of a purchasing decision, an act of Congress evaluated against its own participation records, and a company deciding a blunt technical limit was cheaper than moderating case by case. A voluntary guideline can set an expectation for what a developer ought to do once a harm turns up. Getting one actually fixed, in each of these cases, took someone with a specific lever to pull — the same specificity RoboCup Rescue is built to test for a harm that cannot wait for an audit cycle at all.
