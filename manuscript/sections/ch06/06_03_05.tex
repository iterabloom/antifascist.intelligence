\subsection{Long-Term AI Safety Considerations}
\label{sec:6.3.5}
As capability rises, the safety question changes shape. The problem stops being an error to correct after the fact and becomes whether the values still track anything human once a system acts at a scale and speed no supervisor watches in real time.

Three pressures make the long-run version harder. Value alignment has to survive time, so a system needs some way to revise its understanding of what it was built to serve without that revision becoming the route by which the commitments erode — accommodation, at a scale where nobody is checking each step. Competition compresses the schedule. And a more capable system is a more attractive target, so a portion of what looks like misalignment will be someone else's deliberate work.

The catastrophic scenarios get told as control of critical infrastructure. The mechanism matters less than the shape, which is capability outrunning the arrangements meant to keep it answerable, and nothing about the largest version of that is different in kind from the office-sized version.
