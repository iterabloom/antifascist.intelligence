\section{Same Robot, Worse Boss}
\label{sec:6.4}
An AI system does not need a broken moral sense to become an instrument
of control. It can work as designed inside an institution that controls
the deployment, sets its operational tempo, and assigns human beings
operations too compressed to constitute judgment. The technology does
not change; the relation around it does.

Authoritarian misuse of AI is not one problem but several, spread across surveillance, censorship, opinion manipulation, and infrastructure control. Six forms are documented well enough to name specifically:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Algorithmic social scoring: using AI-driven analytics to track and rank individual behavior against government-defined standards of ``trustworthiness,'' with rewards or restrictions attached to the result. China's system is the most-cited example, though in practice it is a patchwork of court blacklists, tax-compliance ratings, and local pilot programs rather than the single national score it is often described as \autocite{drinhausen2021chinas}.
  \item Mass biometric surveillance: deploying facial recognition at population scale to track a targeted group. Authorities in Xinjiang built a platform that pulls data from checkpoints, cameras, and phone scans into one system built to flag anyone who falls outside a computed behavioral baseline, and used it to monitor the region's Uighur population \autocite{humanrightswatch2019chinas}.
  \item Psychographic micro-targeting: building detailed behavioral profiles from personal data to target propaganda or manipulate opinion at the individual level. Cambridge Analytica's harvesting of Facebook data to build voter profiles for the 2016 US presidential campaign, and its work around the Brexit referendum, is the case that made the technique widely known, though how much either vote was actually affected remains disputed \autocite{cadwalladr2018revealed}.
  \item Automated censorship: using AI-driven content moderation to suppress dissent at a scale no human review team could match. China's Great Firewall is the standing example: automated classifiers now sit alongside its original keyword- and IP-blocking infrastructure, scoring and throttling content at machine speed \autocite{hoang2021great}.
  \item Synthetic disinformation: generating deepfake audio or video to manufacture events that never happened. Two days before Slovakia's 2023 parliamentary election, a fabricated audio recording appeared to catch an opposition leader discussing vote-rigging with a journalist; it spread during the country's mandatory pre-election media silence, when no outlet was legally permitted to debunk it before voting began \autocite{denadal2024beyond}.
  \item Repurposed dual-use systems: turning AI infrastructure built for one purpose toward surveillance or population control instead — a recommendation algorithm designed to hold attention retuned to amplify a regime's preferred narrative, an image-recognition system built for one task redirected to track protesters. Moscow's camera network is the documented case: promoted as a public-safety measure, it was used after the January 2021 protests over Alexey Navalny's arrest to detain and prosecute more than a dozen participants and passers-by on facial-recognition data, some of them stopped days before a protest for appearing on a list of repeat attenders \autocite{hrw2021russia}.
\end{enumerate}

Each needs a different countermeasure, and each is recognizable as misuse from outside. The case this chapter takes up is the one that is not.

The relevant question is not merely whether a person remains ``in the
loop,'' but what operation that person performs, how long the operation
is given, and where responsibility goes when capability is commercially
supplied but its consequences are institutionally authorized. The
targeting systems below make that relation measurable.
