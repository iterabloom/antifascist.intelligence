6.3. The Political Economy of AI and Power
Concentrated power is close to the default outcome of AI development, not a side effect of it, because the same inputs that make an AI system capable — capital, compute, data, talent — are exactly the inputs that concentrate wherever nothing pushes back. Section 2.1.4 names resistance to market power among AI alignment's antifascist design goals; this section works out what that resistance takes as economic and political fact, not design principle.

The argument runs in three stages. What economic incentives reward shapes what gets built long before any ethics review sees it (6.3.1), and that reward structure concentrates power in a small number of firms with the resources to compete for it at all (6.3.2). Automation then reshapes who works and who gets paid what (6.3.3), which is a problem policy can act on directly rather than only describe (6.3.4). None of the rest is possible to act on without two things this section closes on: a public and a policymaking class that understand AI well enough to govern it (6.3.5), and researchers and developers trained across the disciplines a single-discipline education leaves out (6.3.6).
