\section{Notice, and the public it forms}
\label{sec:6.2}
Dewey described the eclipse of the public: people affected by the indirect consequences of others' transactions can't organize, because they don't know the consequences are theirs \autocite{dewey1927public}. The people a floor protects are that public. Most don't know the deployment exists. The Black petitioners who turned the Declaration of Independence against the Massachusetts legislature in 1777 had what they lack: the text in hand, and knowledge of what it left out of their lives. Notice is the instrument that gives it to them. It does two jobs, and the individual remedy is smaller than either.

\emph{It forms a public.} Each deployment publishes the classes of people its compliances name, score, locate or classify, with counts by period. Organizations that represent a class have standing to contest on its behalf, including where no member can be identified. A notice is the first step in a class of people learning that a consequence is theirs.

\emph{It is the learning signal.} A disposition can only track what its acts did to the people they fell on if that reaches it, and in the deployments I am concerned with, almost nothing does. The bombed building sends no signal back, and a wrongful compliance doesn't announce itself. Responses to notice, contests and re-adjudications are the main channel through which those people's side of the outcome arrives. If formation lasts because the world keeps teaching it, this channel is most of the world doing the teaching. It is a biased teacher. Responses come from those among the affected who could respond, and a system formed on them learns to weigh the claims of contesters, which is the feedback asymmetry Railton describes, rebuilt inside the training signal. Sampled re-adjudication, which selects cases the affected didn't select, is the correction, and it is why the two remedies come together.

Notice tells an operator's adversaries something about how a deployment works. Class-level publication can therefore lag operations, as the review ratio's publication does, without losing the property that matters: the record is lodged when the act occurs and can't be revised afterward. Individual notice doesn't lag.

How much notice a deployment can give is fixed by what kind it is. In a removal pipeline, a scoring system or a civilian deployment, the person acted on can be named, told and given a route to contest, and notice can do both of its jobs in full, if it is built so that it can't be turned on the person it reaches (\S\ref{sec:9.6}). In a classified targeting pipeline, individual notice can't bind during operations and the class-level record lags them; what a person hit, or a family in the building, gets is that the class they belonged to and the count are published afterward, and that the organizations representing that class have standing then. That is less than being told, and it is more than the reporting on Gaza and Minab gave anyone. Where the deployment is a war, the force term and the review ratio do most of the work, and the claim that everything else depends on notice is true of the deployments closest to home.

\emph{Why it comes first.} The biases of those who hold power are rarely corrected by their reflecting more carefully. They are corrected when the people those biases fall on contest them in public, with standing, and make ignoring them costly \autocite{anderson2014social,anderson2015moral}. Notice is the first condition of that feedback. A formed system is worth having only if that contest can move it. And a public that learns it has been acted on can act only if it has the means to: the time, the security and the ability to organize without losing a living.
