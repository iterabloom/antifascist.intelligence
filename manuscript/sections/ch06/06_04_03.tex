\subsection{Promoting AI Development Policies that Discourage Authoritarian Use}
\label{sec:6.4.3}
Section~\ref{sec:6.4.2}'s technical safeguards raise the cost of building a system that can be turned toward surveillance or control. Whether anyone has to pay that cost is a policy question, and it splits into two different ones that this book keeps separate rather than treating as one undifferentiated call for “more cooperation.” One is about coordination between states — shared norms, joint standards, the machinery of international agreement — and that argument has its home in section~\ref{sec:10}, specifically section~\ref{sec:10.2}. The other, taken up here, is narrower and more concrete: what rules should govern which AI capabilities get built, sold, and deployed in the first place, regardless of who is coordinating with whom.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Export and trade controls on surveillance-capable systems. A government cannot misuse AI-enabled surveillance technology it was never able to buy. The EU's 2021 recast of its dual-use export regulation added a human-rights-based control specifically for cyber-surveillance items: an exporter who becomes aware that an item is likely to be used in serious human-rights violations must seek approval before shipping it, even when the item falls outside the pre-existing control lists \autocite{europeanunion2021regulation}. The rule is narrower than it sounds — neither “internal repression” nor “serious violation” is defined in the text, and member states have interpreted both inconsistently — but it establishes the principle that an export license, not just a sale, is where this kind of misuse can be stopped before it starts.
  \item Prohibited-practices regulation. Rather than regulate AI in the abstract, a jurisdiction can name specific designs as off-limits regardless of who builds them. The EU AI Act took this approach: its Article 5 bans, among other things, government social-scoring systems, the untargeted scraping of facial images to build a recognition database, real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces outside narrow law-enforcement exceptions, and predictive policing based on profiling alone rather than individualized evidence \autocite{europeanunion2024artificial}. Clearview AI — fined tens of millions of euros by regulators in France and Italy for building exactly the kind of untargeted facial-recognition database Article 5 now prohibits — is what this provision was written in response to, not a hypothetical it anticipates \autocite{cnil2022clearview}. A list this specific gives other jurisdictions something concrete to adapt rather than a principle to reinvent from scratch.
  \item Developer- and corporate-facing design standards. Regulation is not the only lever, and it is usually the slowest one. The Toronto Declaration, presented by Amnesty International and Access Now at the 2018 RightsCon summit, set out non-discrimination standards for machine-learning systems years before most governments had draft legislation on the subject \autocite{amnestyinternational2018toronto} — evidence that a standard with no legal force behind it can still move faster than state regulation and shape what a responsible developer treats as baseline practice in the meantime — the Partnership on AI (section~\ref{sec:5.6.2}) being another such venue.
\end{enumerate}
None of these tools works alone. Export controls with no prohibited-practices list at home let a government ban a technology abroad while permitting it domestically; a design standard with no legal teeth behind it binds only the developers already inclined to follow it. What makes the three work together is that each closes a different door — who can buy the technology, what can be built, and what a developer treats as normal — and an authoritarian government, or a company willing to serve one, has to get through all three rather than just whichever one happens to apply in its jurisdiction.
