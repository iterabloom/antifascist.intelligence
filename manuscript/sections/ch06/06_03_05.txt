6.3.5. Building AI Literacy and Technical Skills
Section 4.6.3 names AI literacy among developers, policymakers, and the public as one safeguard against authoritarian misuse of AI: a safeguard nobody understands is a safeguard nobody can invoke. What building it takes differs by audience far more than the single phrase suggests, and only one of the audiences is well served.

Developers and students are the easy case, and the mechanisms are familiar — AI concepts and their ethical stakes introduced in school curricula before higher education, interdisciplinary degree programs that put computer scientists alongside social scientists and humanists, specialized training for working engineers that goes past a compliance checklist. All of it reaches people already inside the pipeline or heading for it, and it works about as well as curriculum reform generally works.

The public is the hard case, because no institution has the job. Elements of AI, a free course built jointly by the University of Helsinki and the Finnish company Reaktor, is the clearest instance of somebody treating public understanding as an obligation rather than a market: Finland funded the course's translation into every EU member-state language during its EU presidency, specifically to carry it past its own citizens [[cite:C0390]]. That took a national government deciding a course was infrastructure. Nothing in the ordinary funding of AI education produces that decision, which is why the public tier stays thin while the developer tier fills in.

AI education for communities historically locked out of the field belongs in this section and not in a separate one about diversity. A next generation of AI developers drawn from a narrow slice of the population reproduces exactly the narrow set of assumptions section 5.1.1 identifies as a source of AI bias, which makes access a technical requirement on the systems as much as a fairness claim about who gets to build them.
