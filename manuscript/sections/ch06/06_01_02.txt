6.1.2. Establishing Joint Research Initiatives and Programs
Joint research initiatives turn interdisciplinary collaboration from an event into an institution — a standing lab, a grant program that outlasts whoever wrote the first proposal. Two features of the funding decide what the institution is worth.

The first is who pays. The National Science Foundation's Fairness in Artificial Intelligence program funds work at the intersection of computer science and the social-science problem of deciding what "fair" means for a given system, and it has been run jointly with Amazon [[cite:C0458]]. That is a workable way to get a program funded and an awkward way to get its findings believed, since a company co-funding research into fairness is co-funding research that may reach its own products. Section 6.4 comes back to the general form of this: splitting the funding is what keeps the incentive to find a problem from tracking whoever paid to look for one. The problem bites hardest on interdisciplinary programs specifically, because they are the ones industry is most willing to underwrite.

The second is how long the money lasts. The European Commission's SHERPA project put eleven organizations across six countries — academia, industry, civil society, standards bodies — to work on the ethical and human-rights dimensions of AI and big-data systems, on a Horizon 2020 grant that ran four years and then ended [[cite:C0459]]. The consortium was real for as long as it was funded. Project-term money buys a network with an expiry date attached, and when the term runs out the assembled expertise disperses back into the departments it was borrowed from, which is the outcome a standing institution exists to prevent.
