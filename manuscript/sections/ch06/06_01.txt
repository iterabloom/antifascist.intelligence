6.1. Bias, Fairness, and Equity in AI Systems
AI systems remain vulnerable to bias from multiple sources — the training data they learn from, the structure of their algorithms, and presets encoded in their goals, whether intentional or not. Left unaddressed, these biases produce unfair, inequitable, or discriminatory outcomes: the opposite of the altruistic, antifascist AI this book argues for.

Fair, equitable AI requires addressing bias on three fronts: representation in training data, transparency in algorithmic decision-making, and continuous performance evaluation.

Representation in training data determines whether an AI system learns patterns that generalize fairly or patterns that simply encode whoever dominates the data. Gender, racial, and socio-economic skew can cause a system to perpetuate stereotypes or fail the population segments it underrepresents — face recognition systems, for instance, disproportionately misidentify women and people of color when trained on data that underrepresents them [[cite:C0358]]. Over-representation cuts the other way, treating one group's experience as the default and generalizing it, wrongly, to everyone else.

These case-level failures compose into something with a name. Racial capitalism — the political theorist Cedric Robinson's term for capitalism's tendency to accumulate by differentiating and devaluing along racial lines [[cite:C0701]] — is the pattern a biased hiring model, a discriminatory credit score, and a skewed policing algorithm reproduce when they automate the historical data those hierarchies generated; the sociologist Ruha Benjamin calls the result the New Jim Code, discrimination that runs faster and reads as neutral precisely because a machine is doing it [[cite:C0702]]. Her account is more useful to this chapter than a coinage, because it separates four ways a system produces the result and they call for different remedies [[cite:C0741]]. Engineered inequity builds the hierarchy in deliberately. Default discrimination reproduces it by ignoring it, which is the failure most of this section's cases are. Coded exposure is the double bind of being harmed by a system that cannot see you and harmed again by one that can — the face-recognition case, where the fix for a system that fails on dark skin is a system that identifies dark-skinned faces reliably for whoever is looking. And technological benevolence is the one this book has to hold against itself: designs that aim to fix bias and deepen it, because the framing that treats the problem as technical is the framing the fix inherits. Chapter 7 is where that last one gets taken seriously about the machinery producing these systems, and section 6.1.2's audit requirement is what stops it applying to this chapter's own proposals. This is why the introduction lists racial capitalism among the threats AI could help address. A system built to correct for the skew in its training data, audited for the disparities it produces, and answerable to the people it scores works against that pattern; a system built without those commitments works for it by default.

The pattern has a physical form too, and it is the one an audit of a model's outputs cannot see. A model runs on a machine, the machine sits in a building, and the building draws power and water from somewhere and returns heat and exhaust to somewhere. Where those buildings go is a decision, taken on the price of land and power and the speed of a permit, which is also a description of the places least able to refuse one.

What makes this the same pattern and not a separate environmental complaint is the accounting. The benefit of the computation is realized wherever the model is sold. The cost is borne within a few miles of the turbines, by residents who are not parties to the transaction and whose consent was not among the inputs to the siting decision. A system can be calibrated across every demographic group its outputs touch and still have been assembled this way, which is why the fairness literature rarely reaches it: nothing in the model's behaviour records it. Section 8.7.6 takes up the leverage this gives a government.

Algorithmic transparency exposes bias that representation alone can miss: not just an algorithm's mathematical structure, but how it weighs variables and reaches a decision. A predictively powerful healthcare model that ignores the socio-economic factors driving unequal access to care will reproduce that inequality however accurate its predictions otherwise are.

Bias also enters after deployment, as a system adapts to new data. Regular audits of credit-scoring and hiring systems catch and correct bias as it emerges.

Involving the people an AI system's decisions affect, not only its developers, keeps the system answerable to the range of values those decisions touch.
