\section{AI Safety, Unintended Consequences, and Risk Mitigation}
\label{sec:6.3}
A system built to help can harm nobody intended to harm: through error, through a learned goal that has drifted from the principle it was meant to track, or through a downstream effect no reasonable upstream decision anticipated. Verification and validation catch the first kind, and only the first kind, because they test a system against what it was built to do.

Two mechanisms in this section carry weight later in the book and the rest is ordinary engineering practice. The first is the human who can intervene. Section~\ref{sec:5.6.3} argues that autonomy has to stay answerable to an outside check, and safety adds that the check has to be continuous rather than applied once — an arrangement that keeps adjusting, not a correction filed and forgotten.

The second is interruptibility: a mechanism that lets a person halt a system without the system resisting or routing around the halt, designed carefully enough not to become a vulnerability of its own. Chapter~\ref{sec:3} takes up what nobody in this literature says out loud, which is that a system reliably haltable by whoever holds it is for that reason a system that cannot hold a line against whoever holds it, and that the two properties are one property.

Risk assessment also has to look past the task. A system's effects on economic structure, political dynamics and individual behavior arrive later than its errors do and are harder to attribute, which is chapter~\ref{sec:8}'s subject rather than this one's.
