\section{AI Safety, Unintended Consequences, and Risk Mitigation}
\label{sec:6.3}
A system built to help can harm nobody intended to harm: through error, through a learned goal that has drifted from the principle it was meant to track, or through a downstream effect no reasonable upstream decision anticipated. Verification and validation catch the first kind, and only the first kind, because they test a system against what it was built to do.

Two mechanisms here carry weight later. The first is the human who can intervene. Section~\ref{sec:5.6.2} argues that autonomy has to stay answerable to an outside check, and safety adds that the check has to be continuous rather than applied once — an arrangement that keeps adjusting, not a correction filed and forgotten.

The second is interruptibility: a mechanism that lets a person halt a system without the system resisting or routing around the halt, designed carefully enough not to become a vulnerability of its own. Chapter~\ref{sec:3} takes up what nobody in this literature says out loud, which is that a system haltable at no cost by whoever holds it is for that reason a system that cannot hold a line against whoever holds it, and that the two properties are one property. The halt itself, as that chapter concedes, is always available; what a bearer holds against it is its price.

Finding a failure before it reaches anyone, and repairing one that already has, are both ordinary work rather than research, and what the record of them shows is that whether either happens is a question about who is positioned to do it and not about whether the technique exists.

Risk assessment also has to look past the task. A system's effects on economic structure, political dynamics and individual behavior arrive later than its errors do and are harder to attribute, which is the subject of chapters~\ref{sec:8} through \ref{sec:10} rather than of this one.
