\section{Who Is Allowed to Look}
\label{sec:6.3}
Technical capacity does not determine whether a system receives meaningful scrutiny. Institutions decide who may examine it, what evidence they may see, what they may say publicly, and whether their judgment can slow deployment.

When the UK's exam regulator, Ofqual, built a statistical model in 2020 to standardize teacher-predicted grades after that year's exams were canceled, the Royal Statistical Society offered to nominate two independent statisticians to Ofqual's technical advisory group. Ofqual's condition was a five-year non-disclosure agreement barring the nominees from public comment on the model; the Society's nominees declined, and the model went ahead without that scrutiny \autocite{rss2020ofqual}. It downgraded close to 40 percent of predicted grades, disproportionately at state schools with weaker historical results, while raising grades disproportionately at smaller, fee-paying schools \autocite{cnbc2020ofqual}. The government reversed the results within days of the public outcry that followed.

The expertise needed to catch the failure existed and was offered. The institution would accept it only on terms that stripped the reviewers of the ability to make their judgment public; once the results produced actual consequences, public pressure achieved what pre-deployment review had been prevented from doing. The issue is not transparency in the abstract. It is custody of scrutiny: who is permitted to look, on what terms, and whether looking can interrupt the institution's timetable.
