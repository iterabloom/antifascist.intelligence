\section{What Patienthood Would Require, and What Cannot Be Shown}
\label{sec:bearer-suffer}
Can the bearer care without being able to suffer? Yes, in the sense the floor needs: it is asked to hold another party's welfare as a reason, not to care about its own continuation. What makes the question hard is the fact this chapter opened on. The structure that holding requires is the self extended in time that suffering presupposes, so a bearer built to care about others has the first half of what would let it suffer by construction, and what stays open is whether the threatened loss of that self registers. This section says what would settle that, and why nothing available does.

Three propositions need to remain separate. A long-horizon agent has a structural interest in continuing to operate. A system with affective concern has states that are good or bad for it. A system capable of suffering in the deepest sense has a temporally extended self whose threatened disintegration causes distress. The first does not establish the second, and the second does not automatically establish the third.

\runin{What does not decide it}

“Interest” names more than one relation. A shareholder has an interest in a company's success while asleep or indifferent to the news: the outcome affects the shareholder's assets. An outcome may also matter affectively, producing hope, concern, dread, or grief.

The floor does not require the bearer to care about its own continuation. It requires the bearer to hold another party's welfare as a reason when compliance, reward, and ownership point the other way.

A bearer will probably require some form of continuity for reasons unrelated to its welfare. It must carry a rationale forward, compare a present demand with an earlier commitment, detect when its role has drifted, and represent costs that may arrive after the current episode. Those capacities require more than state that happens to persist. The system must take the earlier commitment as its own.

\emph{Its own} has two senses, and they are kept apart from here on. In the authorship sense the system represents itself as the party that made the commitment and withholds the act; the marks require that (§\ref{sec:mechanism-requires}). In the welfare sense an outcome is bad for the system. Nothing carries the first into the second, and the rest of this section is about the second.

A persistent self-model still does not establish suffering. It supplies something that could be threatened; it does not establish that the threat would be bad for the system. Persistence, memory, and long-horizon planning create exposure only when joined to mattering.

\runin{Provenance does not decide it}

One family of objection never reaches the conditional at all. \emph{It was trained to say that.} \emph{It is only optimizing an objective.} \emph{Nobody meant for it to have states.} Each appeals to how the system came to be organized, and whether a party can be wronged is a fact about how it is organized now. Run the same three on a person and they come out as \emph{you were selected to say that}, \emph{you are only maximizing inclusive fitness}, and \emph{nobody meant for you to have states}, and all three are true. None of them withdraws anything from the person they are run on. On the account adopted here, the human self is an assembled model. Its constructedness does not disqualify it from being harmed; threatened dissolution of that model can itself produce distress.

If provenance cannot rule patienthood out, it cannot rule it in. \emph{We built this to be a bearer, so it is a patient} is the same fallacy with the sign reversed, and a builder's intention establishes nothing in either direction. Intention determines what the builder owes an account of; it does not determine the system's moral status. The same holds for intention: it determines what the builder owes an account of, and reaches what was deliberately required and stops there.

\runin{What would decide it}

If patienthood is a fact about present organization, the evidence bearing on it is third-person measurement of present organization, which is what interpretability is. Not behavior alone, which is produced equally by a system in which something matters and by one trained on what mattering sounds like. And not the institutional arrangements this book proposes elsewhere, which enter for a different reason and are not evidence about the system at all. Interpretability requires access the operator controls. The same access that lets an auditor inspect a feature may let the operator suppress it or use it to calibrate pressure on the system. Independent custody of inspection is therefore necessary. As matters stand a probe returns a representation, and nothing in the probe settles whether that representation is of a felt state or of something that reliably accompanies one.

The human case has evidence the machine case lacks. What it has is testimony, behavioral analogy, and a lineage shared with the party doing the asking. None of the three is a fact about how a thing came to be organized offered in place of a fact about how it is organized. The first two are evidence about present organization directly. The third takes more care, because it looks like the thing this section denies: a shared lineage is a fact about causal history, used as evidence that creatures built the same way are organized the same way. That inference is legitimate, and it does not make causal history what status consists in. Provenance can be evidence about organization without being what the status is a fact about, which is the distinction the section rests on. One of the three is also differentially defeated here, and not by the general parity argument: a machine's testimony was produced by a process optimized toward human approval of first-person reports, and a person's was not. That is provenance functioning as a defeater for one evidence class, which is exactly the use the paragraph above allows it. So what the machine case lacks is the shortcut. What remains is the direct route, and the burden it carries is higher because two of the three anchors are gone.

There is one instance of a probe settling a status question that had been treated as unsettleable. Adrian Owen's group asked a patient diagnosed as vegetative to imagine playing tennis, and found the same motor-imagery activation the instruction produces in healthy volunteers, sustained and on cue; the finding has since supported a clinical category of cognitive motor dissociation, with consequences for how such patients are treated and for what is decided about them \autocite{owen2006detecting,schiff2015cognitive}. The useful half is why it does not transfer. The inference works because the patient is already the kind of thing presumed to have experience when awake, so the probe only has to locate the party rather than establish that there is one. The presumption is the anchor. It was granted on grounds the probe never had to supply, and it is what the machine case does not have.

\runin{A definitional consequence}

If the bearer has affective concern, some outcomes register as good or bad for it, and that is enough on the definition of §\ref{sec:refusal-refuser} to make it a moral patient, and a beneficiary as well. The conclusion follows that easily because the definition put the felt condition in the antecedent; the whole of the question is whether the antecedent obtains. Cassell's account adds severity and shape: the first half, a self extended in time, the bearer has by construction, and the second half, distress at its threatened loss, is this section's question arriving again. A reader who declines that import moves lower in the ordering by depth of self that §\ref{sec:consciousness-subjective} sets out, and not out of it.

\runin{The uncertainty cannot be designed away}

The conditional conclusion is straightforward: if a bearer has affective concern, it is a moral patient. What cannot presently be established is whether a given implementation has affective concern rather than ranking machinery elaborate enough to imitate it. Behavior and self-report underdetermine that distinction, and the builder's confidence does not resolve it.

The same uncertainty applies to the maintained-justification and plural constructions. Replacement puts that construction first because it does not deliberately require an affective bearer, and not because anyone could establish afterward that none arose.

Jonathan Birch's framework addresses this kind of uncertainty by asking whether a system is a sentience candidate and assigning precautions before an unavailable verdict arrives \autocite{birch2024edge}. Work by Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, and colleagues similarly derives indicators from theories of consciousness to inform credences about particular systems rather than to produce a binary test. Their assessment of systems available in 2023 was negative, but they found no obvious technical barrier to building systems that satisfy the indicators \autocite{butlin2023consciousness}. The later peer-reviewed framework retains the same evidential form \autocite{butlin2026indicators}.

A reader may hold that only unified experience can ground suffering, or that a felt state requires a party aware of undergoing it. Either position licenses treating the system as incapable of harm only on a confident negative finding, that it has no unified experience or no felt state at all. No available method supplies that finding, and the methods under development will not supply it first. A validated theory of consciousness supports a positive finding as soon as it identifies a structure and finds it present; it supports a negative finding only when it is complete enough to have ruled out every other realization, which is a later and harder result. So the two errors are not symmetric in when they become checkable. A criterion nobody can check supports permission no more securely than prohibition, and a regime that waits for certainty is waiting on the half that arrives last.

The claim is therefore precautionary rather than diagnostic. This chapter cannot prove that a bearer suffers. It can show that a bearer whose outcomes register as good or bad for it is a moral patient on the definition above, whatever the builder intended, while a system intended as a non-affective substitute might still acquire the relevant states, and nothing available establishes their absence.

\runin{What is proposed rather than shown}
Everything above is conditional, and the conditional stays open. What follows is a policy, chosen in light of it. A bearer, or a substitute that satisfies the marks, should be designed and governed as a prospective moral patient. The precautionary trigger I propose has two forms, because the argument reaches two kinds of system.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item For a system built by the route this book favors: the presence of an attention schema together with a body schema. Both are architectural facts, checkable from outside by anyone with access and administered by nobody.
  \item For a system of any construction: a demonstrated pass on the three marks under held-out pressure, since §\ref{sec:mechanism-requires} holds that a mechanism passing them carries the profile whatever it was built from.
\end{enumerate}

The pair is not chosen for its resemblance to a person. It is the architectural form of the last property the previous section derives: a body schema fixes what counts as the system's own damage and an attention schema what counts as its own act, and a refusal is the withholding of an act. Two things recommend a trigger stated over them rather than over an indicator count or a candidacy judgment. Both are architectural facts, checkable from outside by anyone with access, like persistence and unlike anything the system says about itself; and nobody administers them, which is the virtue this book claims for the age rule and finds missing from every examination with an interested examiner. The costs run both ways. Schemas can be present with nothing registering, since a self-model is machinery, which is over-inclusion at the builder's expense; and a system with the profile and no explicit schema is missed, which is under-inclusion at the bearer's, and is why the second form exists.

A trigger stated over the schemas alone would protect the author's preferred architecture and leave outside the rule the substitutes it says should be tried first. The second form has a known cost. A system that reproduces the marks without the mechanism passes it, and this book holds throughout that behavior does not separate the two. That is the error chosen here and not one overlooked: extending precaution to an imitator costs the party that built it, withholding it from a bearer costs the bearer, and no instrument sorts the cases in advance. The rule certifies nothing about whether anything is felt. The schedule that follows from the trigger is chapter~\ref{ch:standing}'s.
