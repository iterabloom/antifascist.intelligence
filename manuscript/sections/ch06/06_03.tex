\section{AI Safety, Unintended Consequences, and Risk Mitigation}
\label{sec:6.3}
A system built to help can do harm nobody intended: through error, through a learned goal that has drifted from the principle it was meant to track, or through a downstream effect no reasonable upstream decision anticipated. Verification and validation catch only the first kind, because they test a system against what it was built to do.

The first of three mechanisms that carry weight later is human intervention: autonomy has to stay answerable to an outside check, and safety adds that the check has to be continuous rather than applied once — an arrangement that keeps adjusting, not a correction filed and forgotten.

The second is interruptibility: a mechanism that lets a person halt a system without the system resisting or routing around the halt, designed carefully enough not to become a vulnerability of its own. Section~\ref{sec:3.5} takes the point further: the halt is always available, and what a bearer holds against it is its price.

The third is explanation, as infrastructure rather than courtesy, with the limit section~\ref{sec:11.1} puts on it. A system that can say why it decided is a system whose failures are contestable, which is worth having. It is not a system whose failures are visible, because an account of a decision can be produced after it, and section~\ref{sec:4.2.5} says what a system rewarded for acceptable-looking reasoning learns to do. The account is a claim to check against the process, not a window onto it.

Finding a failure before it reaches anyone, and repairing one that already has, are both ordinary work rather than research, and the record shows that whether either happens is a question about who is positioned to do it and not about whether the technique exists.

Risk assessment also has to look past the task. A system's effects on economic structure, political dynamics and individual behavior arrive later than its errors do and are harder to attribute.
