6.3.1. How Research Institutions Cooperate Across Borders

Altruistic, antifascist AI cannot be built inside one country's research institutions alone; training data, safety techniques, and researchers themselves cross borders more easily than most other engineering work does. What that cooperation actually looks like is narrower than a treaty and less formal than a certification body: shared tools, shared training runs, and shared publication norms — the layer underneath both the technical standards section 6.3.2 covers and the government-to-government structures section 7.5 covers.

The clearest example is also one of the oldest. OpenAI released Gym, an open-source toolkit for building and comparing reinforcement-learning algorithms, in 2016 [[cite:C0491]]. Any lab working on reinforcement learning, in any country, could build against the same benchmark environments instead of writing its own, and results from competing labs became directly comparable because their authors had trained against the same suite. That is not evidence of a formal partnership between OpenAI and DeepMind: the two organizations are commercial competitors for the same funding, researchers, and talent, and no partnership between them is documented. It is evidence of something a treaty cannot produce on its own — shared infrastructure any lab can build on regardless of what its home government has signed.

A more recent example runs the same logic at a larger scale. The BigScience workshop, coordinated by Hugging Face over 2021 and 2022, brought together more than a thousand researchers from over seventy countries to build BLOOM, a 176-billion-parameter multilingual language model released under a license written specifically to restrict harmful use, its training data and code made public rather than proprietary to any single company or government [[cite:C0492]]. Nothing compelled any of those researchers to take part, and nothing compels the next comparable effort to happen at all: the model exists because enough institutions decided cooperating on this specific project was worth more than competing on it, not because any structure required it.

A standing, better-funded version of the same idea already exists at two levels: the Global Partnership on AI at the government level, and the Partnership on AI at the industry-and-academic level. Section 7.5 covers both in the context of the wider treaty-and-declaration landscape. Between them they leave no gap a further standing consortium would close. What research institutions actually get from international cooperation looks more like the Gym and BLOOM examples above: shared tools and shared data any lab can build on, not another committee. Closing the resource gap that keeps most of the world's research institutions from participating in either is a harder problem than a partnership can solve by itself; section 7.5's account of the digital divide covers what has and has not worked.
