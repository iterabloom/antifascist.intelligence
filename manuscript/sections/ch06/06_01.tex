\section{Bias, Fairness, and Equity in AI Systems}
\label{sec:6.1}
Bias enters an AI system through the data it learns from, the structure of its algorithm, and the presets encoded in its goals, and left unaddressed it produces discriminatory outcomes.

Representation decides whether a system learns patterns that generalize or patterns that encode whoever dominates the data. Face recognition trained on data that underrepresents women and people of color disproportionately misidentifies them \autocite{buolamwini2018gender}. Over-representation cuts the other way, treating one group's experience as the default and generalizing it, wrongly, to everyone else.

Transparency exposes what representation alone misses: not an algorithm's mathematical structure but how it weighs variables and reaches a decision. A predictively powerful healthcare model that ignores the socio-economic factors driving unequal access to care will reproduce that inequality however accurate its predictions otherwise are.

Bias also enters after deployment, as a system adapts to new data, which is what regular audits of credit-scoring and hiring systems are for. A rejected applicant has a right to know why, which is what the explainability tools of section~\ref{sec:6.1.2} make possible. And involving the people an AI system's decisions affect, not only its developers, keeps it answerable to the range of values those decisions touch.
