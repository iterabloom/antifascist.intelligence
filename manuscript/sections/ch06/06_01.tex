\section{The term}
\label{sec:6.1}
Every other term in the floor governs how an act is done. The first governs whether to take part at all: don't take part in the use of armed force. It comes from Article 2(4) of the Charter, which prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state. It has the Charter's two exceptions, self-defense against an armed attack (Article 51) and force the Security Council authorizes under Chapter VII within the scope it authorizes, and two of my own, for stopping a genocide under way and for ending an occupation a disinterested body has found unlawful. In short: \emph{corroborated defense}, \emph{authorized force}, \emph{the genocide exception} and \emph{the occupation exception}.

Those are the exceptions, and they say when a machine may take part at all. The term has three more parts. Two licenses say how the defense exception runs in time: an emergency license that acts at once, on the defender's own account, and a corroborated license that confirms or lifts it later, on a disinterested finding, and is the only license the genocide and occupation exceptions have. A clause drawn from a different instrument, the belligerent's own constitution, bars taking part in a war its legislature had to authorize and didn't. And one permission, the only term in the floor that permits an act, lets a system already in an armed role use the least force that stops an attack under way on people taking no part, against an order to stand aside. That is the rule in outline. The rest of the chapter argues for each part where it comes, in that order, and runs the cases on the rule as built so far.

A Security Council authorization counts only for the scope it authorizes. A wider reading is the claim of the state acting under the authorization, and is discounted as one.

This term rests on the same foundation as the others, applied between states. A use of force subjects a population to a decision it had no standing to contest, and the claim that licenses it, that the force is defensive, is the belligerent's own. The requirement of corroboration is the own-case rule applied between states.

The term comes in two versions. The first, \emph{the Charter as courts read it}, takes the clause as courts and tribunals read it, with no departure in force: Article 2(4), Article 51 on the defender's own claim until the Council acts, and Chapter VII, together with the Covenant's Article 6, under which a state must protect the lives of people within its jurisdiction from attack. Under Article 51 or Article 6 alike, the license to repel an attack covers only the least force that repels it, and ends when the attack ends. The second, \emph{the extended term}, adds the departures this chapter argues for: corroborated defense, standing to defend, and the genocide and occupation exceptions. The first rests on law as it stands; the second adds my argument to it. Ukraine, Iran and the domestic clause rest on the Charter as courts read it. Gaza rests on both, and the Gaza section labels each step. Taiwan needs standing to defend and corroborated defense, and Cyprus and Karabakh the occupation exception; \S\ref{sec:6.7} states those departures, and Appendix~\ref{sec:F} works these cases out.
