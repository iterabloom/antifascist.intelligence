6.1.1. Cross-disciplinary Communication and Cooperation
Cross-disciplinary communication matters only where it actually happens: a conference, a lab, a classroom, a shared codebase. Four venues do most of that work.

The AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society, first held in 2018, brings researchers, policymakers, and technologists together specifically on AI's ethical and societal questions, rather than treating them as a side track of a general machine-learning venue [[cite:C0452]]. NeurIPS, primarily a machine-learning venue itself, has hosted a recurring Workshop on Machine Learning Safety since 2022, run in partnership with the Center for AI Safety, pulling alignment-focused machine-learning researchers into the same room as philosophers and psychologists working the same problem from a different direction [[cite:C0453]].

Joint research centers do similar work on a longer timescale. The Center for Human-Compatible AI at UC Berkeley, founded in 2016 under computer scientist Stuart Russell, pairs AI researchers with cognitive scientists and philosophers at partner universities — an arrangement no single department could staff on its own [[cite:C0454]].

Academic programs can build the next generation of researchers to already think this way, rather than retrofitting the habit later. Embedded EthiCS, developed at Harvard beginning in 2017 by computer scientist Barbara Grosz and philosopher Alison Simmons, pairs philosophy graduate students with computer-science faculty to write ethics modules directly into standard CS courses, instead of routing ethics through a single stand-alone class students can skip [[cite:C0455]].

Industry-academia partnerships are a fourth channel. The Partnership on AI, discussed in section 7.5, is the clearest standing example: a consortium built to fund and convene cross-disciplinary AI research across companies, universities, and civil-society groups, rather than leave it to any one member to run alone. Shared digital platforms and tools that let researchers collaborate remotely, rather than only at a scheduled event, are a distinct enough mechanism to warrant their own account in section 6.1.3.
