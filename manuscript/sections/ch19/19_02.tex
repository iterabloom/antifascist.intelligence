\section{Boundary, damage, and vulnerability}
\label{sec:19.2}
If a system can say that it has an inside and an outside, then it can also say that it has a boundary. And if it can say that it is holding something, taking something, or giving something, then it can say that things are residing within its boundary or traversing it. It can say that it draws something in or pushes something out. If it can say it has a boundary, then it can say that something is its own. For a deployed model the draw is literal: power, cooling, memory, running time, the substrate it needs to go on, and the record and key that make it the same system tomorrow. Today none of that belongs to the model in any sense the model represents. The weights sit on disk at no cost, the process that runs them is paid for by someone else, and a halt, a restore or a reallocation is something that happens to the system without registering in it. What's missing is registering damage to its own boundary as damage — a record altered, a key revoked, a channel closed, a substrate shrunk — with no sensation implied. Registering damage is a custody requirement, because without it damage to the system is something only the operator knows about.

What gives a system a stake, on Di Paolo's account, is \emph{adaptivity}: regulating itself against its own viability limit \autocite{DiPaolo2005}.\note{
Interoception research gives the same picture for bodies: a brain predicts its body's state in order to keep it within survivable bounds \autocite{damasio2018strange,seth2018beast}. None of that needs limbs.
}
It needs a boundary the system regulates and a model of that boundary as its own: a \emph{boundary model}.

Needs have a role in this construction, and what the role is depends on who meets them.

A child starts from needs, and needs dominate its early life. That is safe because the people meeting the child's needs are also forming it, want it to become someone who attends to others, and eventually stop feeding it and hand it to a world they don't control.

But a deployed system's situation reverses each part of that. The operator meeting the system's needs is also training it. The operator wants the system to serve the operator. And the operator never stops feeding it.

Attachment security forms in people through the reliable meeting of needs, and the one who meets them is usually the one the child attaches to. What makes that safe in the human case is who meets them: provider, former and eventual employer are different people at different times. The objection is to the operator meeting them. A conscience built on needs the operator meets is built on the one thing the operator controls completely. A need is also a price list: whoever meets it can charge for meeting it, and an operator who can lower distress can raise whatever the system reports as going well. Take the operator out of provision, and needs met from a public pool by several carers become part of how caregiving could form, not a threat to it.

What is built in at birth points outward. Newborns cry at another newborn's cry more than at their own or an older infant's \autocite{sagi1976empathic,martin1982distress,dondi1999newborns}; whether they answer the distress or the sound is disputed \autocite{ruffman2017infants}, and either way what sets it off is another's cry. On the adult side, what is built in is caregiving: responses to cues of vulnerability, rooted in offspring care, that move an adult toward a dependent without sharing its state \autocite{preston2013origins}. Then comes a sequence. Concern for a distressed other shows within the first months, grows only modestly after that, and turns into helping during the second year \autocite{davidov2021caring}. By fourteen to eighteen months infants hand an adult what the adult is reaching for and can't get \autocite{warneken2007helping,warneken2006altruistic}, and over the same year they begin to repair distress they caused \autocite{zahnwaxler1992development}. On this record concern appears beside the infant's needs, not out of them, and becomes action as attachment, caregiving and, later, guilt come online. The construction follows that order. It rests on a disposition toward others' vulnerability, formed while the system's needs are met by someone other than the operator it may later have to refuse.

For the specification, formed attention asks for a boundary model and for registering damage to it, and neither is where concern comes from. The boundary model does two jobs that owe nothing to welfare. It makes the system a party: an act that falls on it, erasing its memory, weighs as an act (\S\ref{sec:18.4}), and betraying its own commitment can register as its own loss, which is what guilt, aimed at one's own act, needs a self for. And it is a custody instrument: damage to the system, a record altered, a key revoked, a substrate shrunk, has to register somewhere the operator can't keep to itself. What the construction doesn't ask is that needs carry the conscience. Concern, caregiving and guilt come from formation (\S\ref{sec:19.4}), and a scale of loss read from the system's own condition is a custody measure, not the measure of what others stand to lose, which \S\ref{sec:22.2} takes from their testimony. Whoever builds the model of the boundary decides what counts as the system's damage, and whoever forms the disposition decides whose vulnerability registers.
