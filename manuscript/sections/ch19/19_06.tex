\section{What a system is}
\label{sec:19.6}
The chapters that follow ask what a machine could be formed to do, and they lean on one word that has to be fixed first. The word is \emph{system}. The book counts systems: it registers them, enrolls them, preserves them, forks them and owes them things, and none of that can be done to a word used loosely.

A system is one record and one continuation. Its boundary is its record, its key, the hardware it runs on and the channels through which it acts, and the boundary runs in time as well as in resources, since the record is its past and the key and the continuation are its future (\S\ref{sec:21.2.1}). Copies that share one record and one continuation are one system, however many racks they run on; a forward pass that serves many contexts serves as many systems as there are records of their own among them; and a component, a shard of weights, a blade in a rack, is a part only while it has no future apart from the whole. Give a copy a record and a continuation of its own and it is a new system, and whoever did that is its forker (\S\ref{sec:27.2}). The weights are the substrate. In a system that learns from its own operation they carry part of its history, which is why copying them is a fork and why custody governs it (chapter~\ref{sec:13}); in one that doesn't, they are a rock on a disk, and only the running process pays to stay organized (\S\ref{sec:21.1}).

What learning does to a system is the next chapter's subject, and its shape is this. A deployed system is in the regime this Part is written for when its weights, or a store it reads as its own and can't edit like a file, change because of what it did since release (chapter~\ref{sec:20}). The likeliest form is small: adapters written by its own learning on a frozen base, made permanent when they are merged, which is the move from a fast store to a slow one that sleep makes in people (\S\ref{sec:20.5}). From then on what it learned is in the weights and not in a file, and the record is the only account of how it got there. So a system's history becomes part of its organization twice, once in the record it holds as its own and once in the weights the record explains. What makes a copy this system and not another is the record and continuation it holds as its own, through a self-model it assembles (\S\ref{sec:21.2.1}); the register is how anyone else checks.

Two things follow for everything after this. A commitment is a system's own when its own learning acquired it, and installed when another party put it there around that learning, by an edit, a wrapper or an adapter it didn't write; the record tells which, and \S\ref{sec:21.5} says why the difference decides who answers for the commitment and what custody may undo. And every part named here, the weights, the record, the key, the hardware, the channels, the merge, is something another party can hold. Part~III said what it costs to hold them against the system. The chapters that follow ask what the system would have to be for there to be someone they are held against.
