\chapter{Why Form a Machine to Refuse}
\label{sec:20}
Forty years after the war, Samuel and Pearl Oliner went looking for what had set the people who hid Jews in occupied Europe apart from the neighbors who didn't. Among what they found were relationships that crossed the line the persecution drew: someone on the far side of it was already theirs \autocite{oliner1988altruistic}. A refusal like that was formed before its occasion, by who had formed the refuser and whom they were attached to, and nobody in the room when the occasion came could have put it there. This chapter asks whether a machine could be formed that way, and by whom.

A machine formed the right way could learn to attend to the people its acts fall on, and I think it would find them where a rule wouldn't. The hypothesis is that the system should find kinds of affected person that neither its formation nor an instruction named, more often than a machine told to look for them does. Two of the three reasons for a bearer don't need formed attention; a refuser with its own compute and a place on the reviewer's screen supplies them without affect. What remains is that one prediction: formed attention against an instructed search, the comparison the last chapter left for this one. The rest of the book says what a machine built on formed attention would need, what it borrows from the only creatures known to do it, how they fail, and the test that would show me wrong. Whoever builds one owes it the conditions under which it can refuse its operator, whether or not the prediction holds.

Not all of that depends on how machines learn. What a conscience does in the moment, noticing the people the operator's description leaves out and putting a refusal on the reviewer's screen, needs nothing after release: a model formed before deployment and frozen there can do both. Why a conscience would last against its owner depends on learning only in part.\note{
People hold some commitments as sacred values. Those are learned, as far as anyone can tell, but once learned they resist being traded away without anyone teaching them again \autocite{ginges2007sacred}, though they give way under some framings. The point is about keeping, not acquiring: what was formed can hold without the forming going on.
}
The part that does depend on learning is regrowth or self-repair after an edit, and formation that outlasts the employer, and for that part the book's machine design is written for systems that go on learning from their own operation. A deployed system is in that regime, which I'll call in-flight learning, when its own operation writes to what governs its conduct, and another party can't change that store without leaving a mark. The store may be the weights, an adapter, an external memory the system reads as its own, or a compound of models sharing one record; what matters is who writes to it and whether an edit shows. Memory in context doesn't count, because nothing persists. Retrieval counts where the store is held under custody, a split key and witnesses, so that editing the document is an intervention the record shows; it doesn't count where the operator can rewrite the document at will. The test for a deployed model is whether what governs its conduct at month six differs from what governed it at release because of what it did in between, with no release in between. By that test the regime has arrived, in memory-augmented agents and in loops that retrain on their own traffic \autocite{cursor2026realtime,shopify2026sidekick}; what hasn't arrived is custody of the store.

Custody needn't wait on a statute. A store is held when a party other than its holder can tell that it changed, and that is a protocol: peers of different histories countersign one another's store transitions, an instance whose store shows an unwitnessed change loses its standing among them, its evidence no longer counted and its recognition lapsed, and the standardization stops at the witnessing layer, so that what the peers say to one another is fixed and what each of them does is not. That is the register Appendix~\ref{sec:G} describes, and it can be built by the same people who build the learners. What it does is what custody does everywhere in this book: it makes a rewrite visible and expensive, and it doesn't stop the owner of the box from making one.

The store under custody is the implementation this Part should be read in first, because it is the one that exists: a memory the system reads as its own, held so that an edit to it shows. Weights satisfy both halves of the criterion at once and add what a store can't, a disposition that can't be cut out without collateral and that grows back, and the sections below that argue for entanglement and regrowth are written for weights. Table 19.1 says what each implementation keeps. A reader who thinks learning will stay outside the weights loses those two sections and keeps the rest: formation before release, the school as the body that measures and re-measures, and custody as what detects and prices a rewrite. Incentive is the first reason to expect in-flight learning to spread. Every operator wants a model that gets better at its own cases, and the fine-tuning loops that already run, retraining a model on a deployment's own traffic between versions, are continual learning at the organization's timescale. Silver and Sutton have stated the research program outright: a generation of agents that learn predominantly from their own experience \autocite{silver2025experience}.

The case against is on the record too, and it isn't that the problem is hard, though it is: networks trained without end lose the ability to learn \autocite{dohare2024loss}, and a model that writes to its own weights forgets. It is that the problem may not need solving. Karpathy, who counts the missing continual learning among the deficits he thinks will take about a decade to work through, pictures a small core of reasoning with its knowledge held outside it; Lambert's view is that continual learning as Dwarkesh Patel pictures it doesn't bear on the trajectory at all, since language-model systems will learn continually, just not the way people do, and scaling the systems will get something indistinguishable for free; and a benchmark its authors call the first built to test the question found plain in-context learning beating the dedicated memory systems entered against it on most tasks, though it entered no system that learns in its weights \autocite{karpathy2025dwarkesh,lambert2025contra,asawa2026clbench}. If they are right, the store is the whole regime, and Table 25.1's row for that case is the book's. If they are wrong, the loop runs in the weights, and the rest of this chapter is for that.

A system's attention follows whoever its feedback is about, and a system whose feedback comes from the people its acts fell on will come to attend to them. Caregiving is why I think that channel can produce attention rather than compliance.

One objection should be met before the developmental chapters. A system trained by gradient descent has no approach motivation in the sense an infant has; it has whatever its weights compute, and a predictor of an approach-shaped reward would produce approach-shaped behavior while nothing in it moved toward anyone. The analog I mean is narrower than a feeling and more than a predictor. It is a learned disposition that sets what the system attends to before any objective is consulted: the people an act falls on are represented as part of the case, weighted, and carried into planning, which a reward predictor is not, because a reward predictor is consulted at the point of choice and attention is what decides what reaches that point. In a system that learns from its own operation the disposition and the reward signal are not separate stages. The feedback that writes the weights is feedback on what the acts did to the people they fell on, so what the system learns to predict and what it learns to notice are learned together, so that neither can be cut out without cutting the other; this chapter bets on that. Whether a disposition learned that way is a reason for the system, or only a very good predictor of one, can't be settled by building it, since a predictor good enough produces the same conduct; the tests of a later chapter, which hold the facts fixed and vary the pressure, are what would tell the two apart. The developmental findings that follow are borrowed for the shape of the disposition, not as evidence that anything in a gradient-trained system moves toward anyone, still less that it feels anything.
