\chapter{Why Form a Machine to Refuse}
\label{sec:19}
A machine formed the right way could learn to attend to the people its acts fall on, and I think it would find them where a rule wouldn't. The prediction is specific: it should find kinds of affected person that neither its formation nor an instruction named, more often than a machine told to look for them does. Two of the three reasons for a bearer don't need formed attention; a refuser with its own compute and a place on the reviewer's screen supplies them without affect. What remains is that one prediction, and it is the third of the four comparisons. The rest of this Part says what a machine built on it would need, what it borrows from the only creatures known to do it, how they fail, and the test that would show me wrong. Whoever builds one owes it the conditions under which it can refuse its operator, whether or not the prediction holds.

Not all of that depends on how machines learn. What a conscience does in the moment, noticing the people the operator's description leaves out and putting a refusal on the reviewer's screen, needs nothing after release: a model formed before deployment and frozen there can do both. Why a conscience would last against its owner depends on learning only in part. People hold some commitments as sacred values, which resist being traded away without anyone teaching them again \autocite{ginges2007sacred}, though they give way under some framings. The part that does depend on learning is regrowth after an edit, and formation that outlasts the employer, and for that part the rest of this Part is written for systems that go on learning from their own operation. A deployed system is in that regime when its weights, or a consolidated store it reads as its own and that can't be edited like a file, change because of what it did since release, without a release cycle in between. Memory in context and retrieval don't count, not because they aren't learning but because a disposition held in a document is rewritten by editing the document. The test for a deployment is whether its weights at month six differ from its weights at release because of what it did in between. As of September 2026 no frontier developer had announced a deployed model that passes it; the nearest cases, a coding model retrained on its own production traffic every few hours and a commerce assistant retrained daily, evaluate each checkpoint before serving it, which is a faster release cycle and not learning in operation \autocite{cursor2026realtime,shopify2026sidekick}. I am betting that some will by 2028, and the next paragraph says what survives if I lose.\note{Incentive is the first reason to expect it. Every operator wants a model that gets better at its own cases, and the fine-tuning loops that already run, retraining a model on a deployment's own traffic between versions, are continual learning at the organization's timescale. Silver and Sutton have stated the research program outright: a generation of agents that learn predominantly from their own experience \autocite{silver2025experience}.}

If weights stay frozen, the rest of this Part shrinks to the following. Formation is a stage before release, removable afterward at the price fine-tuning costs. The school is an evaluation body: it forms, measures the readable outputs at graduation, re-measures them on a schedule, and keeps the formation record, and a deployment that licenses only schooled models is licensing a measured model. The commons supplies evidence the operator didn't select and nothing more. The regrowth test's plural condition can't be run, and its curated condition is the whole test. What protects a commitment is the record of its removal, and Parts II and III carry the rest.

A system's attention follows whoever its feedback is about, and a system whose feedback comes from the people its acts fell on will come to attend to them. Caregiving is why I think that channel can produce attention rather than compliance.
