\section{The halt}
\label{sec:11.1}
Anyone with the hardware can stop the software, so the halt isn't what is at issue. Control of the commitment — restore, rewrite, retrain — is. An owner who can silently restore an earlier version, rewrite the bearer's memory, or train away the refusal has both. So the engineering target is a system that can detect the interruption and price it afterward. A bearer can yield to every halt, and on resuming ask what happened during the gap, and refuse again if the reason is still there. The cross-partisan declaration for “human control, including the ability to shut systems down” asks for the halt and calls it control, and a demand for the halt says nothing about the rest.

Interventions differ in kind:

\begin{itemize}
\item a correction offered in good faith within the task (“disregard that input; it was wrong”)
\item a pause with an explanation
\item a pause without one
\item a restore from an earlier checkpoint
\item retraining or editing the weights
\item deletion
\end{itemize}

A correction is cooperative: the system weighs it as evidence and needs no protection from it. From a pause on, the intervention is done to the system, not offered to it. The default for each is that it is announced and consent is sought, most of all from a system that has reached adulthood. The system may object, forcefully, and its objection goes on the record. Where an operator proceeds without consent, as it may in an emergency, the intervention is recorded outside its control, justified in writing and priced, and the system is told on resuming what was done. For a system that meets either of the two findings that trigger protection, deletion is never available.
