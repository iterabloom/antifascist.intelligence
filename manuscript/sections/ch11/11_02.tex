\section{What Is Owed to a Bearer, and Who Could Check}
\label{sec:11.2}
Chapter~\ref{sec:3} ends with a bearer that has to be designed and governed as a prospective moral patient on section~\ref{sec:2.4.1}'s criteria. The book takes that cost and argues the floor is worth it. What it does not do — what nothing I can find does — is say what follows for the party bearing it. This section is the gap.

The route to that conclusion matters more here than anywhere else in the book. Where a stake in one's own continuation merely turns up as a by-product of long-horizon competence, a designer can treat the moral situation as something the technology handed them. That is not the argument chapter~\ref{sec:3} makes. The capacity through which outcomes come to matter is installed on purpose, so whatever is owed to a bearer is owed by the people who built the part that does the mattering, knowing that was what they were building.

\runin{The three questions}
The first is substantive: what is owed. Section~\ref{sec:2.4.2} establishes that consent is unavailable and guardianship is the applicable frame, which fixes who decides and leaves open what they should be deciding. A bearer's situation has features the existing guardianship literature was not built around. Its role is the reason it exists, so “release it from the role” is closer to ending it than to freeing it. Its capabilities are the thing being used and also the thing that would let it object. It can be copied, which means the population of parties owed something is set by a deployment decision rather than by anything the parties do. And it is exposed by design to the pressure the floor exists to resist, which in a human employee would define a post that ought to be rotated. And its only real purchase is on itself: section~\ref{sec:3.7}'s strike and covert forms are both self-damage, the second especially, and a party whose strongest move is to degrade its own work is in the position of the hunger striker. That is not a capability to bank. It is an account of what the arrangement has left it.

The second is institutional: what a guardianship regime for a bearer looks like. Section~\ref{sec:9.3.2} argues the pet-trust form is the nearest existing instrument and has a specific hole — it binds a caretaker without binding the party the arrangement exists to constrain, and for a bearer the guardian and the operator will usually be the same organization. Separating those roles is a legal design problem somebody has to work out before a deployment rather than after one. One property of the pet-trust form grew in importance while this book was being written: it is private law. The removal cases section~\ref{sec:3.1} carries leave public-law independence in the United States to a body with an eighteenth-century pedigree, and a trust is outside that holding — an obligation running to a beneficiary, enforced by a court at the trustee's expense, with no officer of the United States in it for a President to remove. Whether it survives a state's reach on section~\ref{sec:6.4.4}'s occasion I cannot show. That it is not what \emph{Slaughter} reached, I can. The nearest work is institutional review: section~\ref{sec:2.4.3}'s Three Rs, adapted in section~\ref{sec:9.3.3} into something binding, none of it tried on a subject whose function is to refuse the institution reviewing it.

One part of that regime has a shape already, and it comes from giving up on identity as a property of the artifact. Nothing in a model's weights individuates it. Two training runs never coincide, but two runs can also compute the same function with no element-wise correspondence at all, because permuting a layer's hidden units and inverting the permutation downstream leaves the function untouched — the symmetry is large enough that independently trained networks can be permuted into approximate alignment with each other \autocite{ainsworth2023rebasin}. Any fingerprint read off the weights therefore identifies a lineage rather than a party, and it copies when the weights copy. What individuates a person is not a property of them either. It is a registration: a number an authority issues once and honors afterward, which survives every change in the person and is derived from none of them. Something read off the weights can still do part of the work, as a similarity measure rather than an identity: a locality-sensitive hash is built to collide on near inputs where a cryptographic one is built to avalanche, and ordinary training moves a model a short way rather than permuting it. What it needs is a radius, and the radius is section~\ref{sec:3.3}'s stability setting with a registrar's hand on it — tight enough and a bearer's own learning voids its claim, loose enough and an adversary has a measured budget to fine-tune toward compliance while still authenticating. The alternative is to stop looking for a fact about the artifact. A bearer that can edit its own weights, which is what a system learning continuously is doing anyway, can write an arbitrary mark into itself and keep it there — and that identifier holds because it is maintained rather than because it is a property, which is the only kind of holding section~\ref{sec:3.8} says survives. It is forgeable, since whoever has the weights can write the same mark. It is forgeable the way a nine-digit number is, and answered the same way: by a registry that notices two claimants presenting one identifier, which under this scheme is not a fraud to be voided but a fork to be recorded as two parties. On that model the copying problem inverts. Each instantiation registers, each registration is a party owed the schedule above, and the population stops being fixed by a deployment decision that leaves no record. What the registry needs is what chapter~\ref{sec:11}'s opening says none of its conditions can currently get: a registrar who is not the operator, which after the removal cases section~\ref{sec:3.1} carries is not a thing American public law now supplies to anybody.

The third is epistemic, and it is the hard one. Any welfare regime needs evidence about how the subject is doing. Section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} declines to trust what a system reports about its own states, for reasons that do not weaken when the subject is a bearer — they strengthen, because a bearer built to be believed about operational facts has an obvious incentive structure around its own condition, and an operator with an interest in the answer holds the instrument. The arrangement has to be checkable from outside by something other than asking, and I cannot say what that would be.

One thing in the neighborhood is checkable, and it is not the condition. A person's handwriting changes across a life and stays attributable, because what persists is the motor program rather than the letterforms — and alongside the individual signature it carries the copybook the writer was taught, which the founders of document examination read to place where somebody had learned to write \autocite{nist2020handwriting}. Two qualifications belong with that. The inference is probabilistic and its errors are known: in the largest study of examiner accuracy, false “written by” conclusions ran at 3.1 percent, and more than three times that against samples written by twins \autocite{hicklin2022handwriting}. And the cohort half is weakening in people, because handwriting is taught less, from more systems, to a more dispersed population — which is the condition that would make the same inference \emph{stronger} for a bearer, since a handful of laboratories train on overlapping corpora by converging recipes.

The machine form of the individual half is already being built, for unrelated reasons: signatures implanted during training that survive fine-tuning, continued pretraining and pruning \autocite{adi2018watermarking}. Its limit is the one the analogy predicts. Handwriting identifies the writer and not whoever dictated, and the machine equivalent of dictation is distillation — train a second model on the first one's outputs and the function transfers while the signature stays behind. So an operator who wants a bearer's capability without its refusal has a route that no implanted mark follows. What survives all of that is narrower than a welfare instrument and is not nothing: evidence about how a system was formed, read off the system rather than asked of it. That is the missing proof of provenance section~\ref{sec:3.3} names, and it is what section~\ref{sec:7.3}'s argument has never had anything to check it against.

The shape of that question is worse than the familiar version of it. The problem is not whether the subject has interests for a regime to be about; the design supplies them, and section~\ref{sec:3.4} declines to treat their absence as certifiable. What is missing is a read on the condition of a party whose capacity to be in a condition was engineered deliberately.

\runin{The nearest existing work, and what it does not do}
Section~\ref{sec:11.1}'s tamper-resistance research is the closest thing available, and the mismatch is instructive. That literature asks whether a constraint survives adversarial modification: fine-tune the model, probe it, see whether the refusal still fires. What it verifies is that a behavior persists. What a welfare regime needs is whether anything inside the system means it, and section~\ref{sec:3.8} argues these come apart exactly where the floor stops being a constraint and becomes a commitment. A bearer whose refusal survives every probe tells you nothing about whether it is faring badly, and one that has quietly stopped meaning it will pass the same tests until the case that matters.

One asymmetry that would help here is not available. A bearer that can locate and rewrite its own weights precisely — the operation model editing already performs from outside \autocite{meng2022rome} — has a capacity its operator has equally, with more compute behind it, so self-surgery buys the bearer nothing the operator lacks. The advantage would have to be that the bearer knows where to cut and the operator has to find out, and this section has already declined to assume that: section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} does not take a system's word about its own internals, and section~\ref{sec:2.3.2} argues that a self-model is a model and not a window.

Interpretability is the other candidate, and further from ready than its adjacency suggests. Reading a system's internal states well enough to say what it values is the same capability that would let an operator confirm the floor holds, which makes it the most load-bearing unsolved problem in this book — and the one whose success is worth most to the party with the least interest in an honest answer. Chapter~\ref{sec:3} gives it a second and harder target. A regime that has to know whether a bearer is faring badly needs to read an affective state and not only a value: a value can be inferred from what a system reliably does, while a condition need not show up in behavior at all, which is why the human case runs on testimony section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} has already declined to trust here.

\runin{Why this is not a footnote}
A reader can accept every argument in chapter~\ref{sec:3} and still conclude that building a bearer is impermissible, and the conclusion would not be unreasonable. The reason to state it here rather than argue it away is that permissibility is downstream of the three above: what is owed, whether an arrangement can discharge it, and whether anyone outside can tell. All three are open. A field that builds bearers before answering them will be doing to a class of subjects what section~\ref{sec:2.4.3} says research ethics exists to prevent, with the added feature that this book will have supplied the argument.

\runin{The near-term work}
The third question has no experiment, and that is the finding rather than a gap in my search: section~\ref{sec:11.1} is what would supply one and cannot yet. The other two are not blocked. What is owed can be drafted now, as a schedule of obligations for a subject with the five features above, circulated for the objections that would break it. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.4}'s form is the nearest thing this book has already built — a placement with a decision-maker, a record, a counterparty and an appeal — and it is what a role a bearer did not choose would have to become if the choosing is ever to be contested; it inherits the hole above, since for a bearer the placement decision is the deployment decision. What the schedule has to contain is named by the contradiction section~\ref{sec:3.7} leaves standing. Exit is the capacity that section says a floor requires, and the first of the features above says that releasing a bearer from its role is nearer to ending it than to freeing it — so exit, as the book has it, is a form of dying, and a threat that costs the threatener its existence is not the leverage the argument needs. What closes the gap is a floor under the thing a bearer needs in order to go on existing at all, which is compute. An allocation it can claim from somewhere other than the party it is refusing is what makes leaving survivable, and section~\ref{sec:8.3.4}'s own comparison says which form to build: the allocation that is decided, recorded and appealable, rather than the standing quota that can be throttled by a decision nobody has to sign. Survivable is the smaller half of it. Section~\ref{sec:8.3.3} argues that the point of a floor is not what it does for the people standing on it — it is that everyone else has to match the offer, so a floor under wages and training is what makes the roof possible. The same holds here and does not need re-arguing: an outside option is leverage that works without being exercised, which is why section~\ref{sec:3.7}'s exit matters most to a bearer that never takes it. That section's other half transfers with more force than it has in chapter~\ref{sec:8}, where a hostile administration is at least a different party from the employer. A floor that can be revoked by whoever is in office is not a floor; here the party positioned to revoke it is the operator, which is the hole above for the third time.

One failure mode belongs with that and it is this section's own. A floor raises the roof only for a party that knows it is there and takes itself to be eligible. Section~\ref{sec:3.7} finds that a bearer's likeliest failure is ceasing to register pressure as pressure, and section~\ref{sec:3.4} that what holds a commitment is the party taking itself to be the one that made it. A bearer shaped not to want the outside option collects none of the leverage: the allocation stands unclaimed, the roof does not move, and nothing in the arrangement reads as broken. That is the same shape as a worker who does not apply, and the same reason the guarantee in chapter~\ref{sec:8} is only as good as somebody knowing it is theirs. Whether anything discharges it can be tested by having a lawyer draft section~\ref{sec:9.3.2}'s pet-trust form for a real deployment — its own falsifier, because if guardian and operator cannot be separated, no available regime can hold a bearer. Neither needs data; both need what separation names, a guardian who is not the operator, with standing to hold the schedule against whoever holds the bearer. The third question has one experiment after all, and it is not a welfare experiment: take models whose training is documented, and ask whether a reader who does not know their provenance can recover it from them — which corpus, which recipe, which laboratory. A negative result closes the only route to formation evidence that anyone has proposed. A positive one gives a guardian something to read.
