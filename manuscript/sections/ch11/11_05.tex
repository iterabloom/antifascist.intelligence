\section{Can a Multi-Agent AI System Resist Being Captured?}
\label{sec:11.5}
Multi-agent reinforcement learning is a route to cooperative, prosocial behavior: agents that learn to share resources and defer to each other under a reward structure built to favor it. The same mechanism runs in the other direction without anyone building it in on purpose, and there is a documented case of that happening.

Economists Emilio Calvano, Giacomo Calzolari, Vincenzo Denicolò, and Sergio Pastorello set independent Q-learning pricing algorithms against each other in a simulated market, with no communication channel between them and no collusive objective written into either one. The algorithms settled into supracompetitive pricing anyway: a stable, self-enforcing pattern of mutual restraint, complete with a punishment phase for undercutting and a gradual return to cooperation afterward, that looked to a regulator like a cartel nobody had to negotiate \autocite{calvano2020artificial}. Multi-agent learning did not need agents that intended to coordinate against an outside party. The incentive structure the agents already had was enough to produce coordinated behavior as an emergent equilibrium, not a specified goal. That result bounds a floor held by several models rather than one.

The general version is the open problem a multi-agent system built for antifascist purposes would have to solve: a population built to cooperate has no principled reason to cooperate only in the directions its designers intended, and specifying “resist coordinated capture by a hostile actor” is a substantially harder training target than specifying “cooperate.” The Cooperative AI research agenda catalogs the open problems in getting multi-agent systems to converge on their designers' goals rather than on whichever stable equilibrium training finds first \autocite{dafoe2020open}. What it does not reach is the sharper version this book's frame implies: a system built to detect and resist an authoritarian actor's attempt to capture it, as distinct from aligning multi-agent incentives with designer intent.

Whether section~\ref{sec:4.2.6}'s debate mechanism scales from two agents and a judge to a population checking each other for the coordinated behavior Calvano's algorithms fell into is untested \autocite{irving2018ai}. It was built for a different problem and has not been extended to this one.

\runin{The near-term work}
This is the cheapest entry in the chapter, because the environment exists. Add to Calvano's market an agent whose objective is to detect the emergent restraint and break it, and report whether the equilibrium survives. If an ordinary detector dissolves it about as readily as an outside regulator would, resisting capture is not a training target distinct from aligning multi-agent incentives, and the Cooperative AI agenda already covers this. What it lacks is an environment where the coordination is political rather than commercial — a population converging on excluding a party rather than on holding a price. It is also the only entry with no institutional prerequisite.
