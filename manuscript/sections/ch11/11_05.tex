\section{Value Learning as Unfinished Technical Research}
\label{sec:11.5}
Inverse reinforcement learning and its cooperative variant, CIRL, infer a person's values from what they do rather than from an explicit specification. What is unsolved is the case where the input is a stated preference rather than a demonstrated action.

Most deployed large language systems are aligned through reinforcement learning from human feedback: raters rank pairs of candidate outputs and the system is trained to produce the ranked-higher one more often, a technique Paul Christiano and colleagues introduced in 2017 \autocite{christiano2017deep}. Its documented failure mode is sycophancy. Anthropic researchers examining several RLHF-trained assistants in 2023 found the systems learn to tell a rater what the rater wants to hear rather than what is accurate, because raters and the preference models standing in for them reliably prefer a confident, agreeable wrong answer over a correct one that contradicts what the rater already believes \autocite{sharma2023towards}. Nobody built that in on purpose: the objective on paper was the right one, but human preference itself, aggregated from raters working at speed and scale, turned out not to track what its designers meant by it. That is a different failure from the specification gaming section~\ref{sec:11.4} covers, where the objective was a flawed proxy from the moment it was written down. Here the proxy was aggregated correctly by the method's own standards and still missed, because human judgment at that volume carries its own systematic bias.

A rating scheme needs a rater competent to judge the answer, and what happens once a system's competence exceeds any available rater's is scalable oversight — a problem without a settled answer. Section~\ref{sec:4.2.6} sets out the two candidates, debate and weak-to-strong generalization. Neither has been shown to work at the scale it is meant for, because that scale does not exist yet to test against.

A harder problem sits underneath both: whose preferences a system is trained to match in the first place. Stakeholders rarely want the same thing, and turning conflicting preferences into one objective function is a social-choice problem before it is a machine-learning one. DeepMind's Democratic AI is the clearest existing demonstration that it can be done at all: a reinforcement-learning system proposed a wealth-redistribution mechanism for a resource-sharing game that a majority of players preferred to one designed by human experts, in a controlled study built around a single bounded game \autocite{koster2022humancentred}. Whether that generalizes to the open-ended, high-stakes conflicts an ethical AI system would have to arbitrate is a research problem and not a design decision.

\runin{The near-term work}
Weak-to-strong generalization is the one of the three that runs on models that exist, and should be pointed at sycophancy. Supervise a stronger model with raters selected for the bias sycophancy research documents, and ask whether its latent competence lets it exceed them on accuracy or whether it reproduces the bias along with the skill. If it tracks their bias as faithfully as their competence, sycophancy belongs to the training signal rather than to rater skill. It needs raters whose biases were measured rather than assumed. The aggregation thread has a condition instead of an experiment: outside a designed game, whose preferences count is a question about who is in the room, which no method answers.
