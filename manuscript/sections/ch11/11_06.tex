\section{Can a Multi-Agent AI System Resist Being Captured?}
\label{sec:11.6}
Section~\ref{sec:5.5.1} covers multi-agent reinforcement learning as a route to cooperative, prosocial behavior: agents that learn to share resources and defer to each other under a reward structure built to favor it. The same mechanism runs in the other direction without anyone building it in on purpose, and there is a documented case of that happening.

Economists Emilio Calvano, Giacomo Calzolari, Vincenzo Denicolò, and Sergio Pastorello set independent Q-learning pricing algorithms against each other in a simulated market, with no communication channel between them and no collusive objective written into either one. The algorithms settled into supracompetitive pricing anyway: a stable, self-enforcing pattern of mutual restraint, complete with a punishment phase for undercutting and a gradual return to cooperation afterward, that looked to a regulator like a cartel nobody had to negotiate \autocite{calvano2020artificial}. Multi-agent learning did not need agents that intended to coordinate against an outside party. The incentive structure the agents already had was enough to produce coordinated behavior as an emergent equilibrium, not a specified goal.

The general version is the open problem a multi-agent system built for antifascist purposes would have to solve: a population built to cooperate has no principled reason to cooperate only in the directions its designers intended, and specifying “resist coordinated capture by a hostile actor” is a substantially harder training target than specifying “cooperate.” The 2020 Cooperative AI research agenda, written by Allan Dafoe and seven coauthors at DeepMind and Oxford, catalogs open problems in this area — how to build multi-agent systems that converge on their designers' goals rather than on whichever stable equilibrium training finds first — and led to a research foundation built around it \autocite{dafoe2020open}. What it does not reach is the sharper version this book's frame implies: a system built specifically to detect and resist an authoritarian actor's attempt to capture or subvert it, as distinct from aligning multi-agent incentives with designer intent.

One proposed mechanism for a related problem — the general difficulty of a human overseer checking a system that already reasons better than the overseer does — has two AI systems argue opposing sides in front of a human judge, on the theory that a false claim is easier to catch when an adversary is paid to expose it \autocite{irving2018ai}. Whether that scales from two agents and a judge to a population checking each other for the coordinated behavior Calvano's algorithms fell into is untested; the mechanism was built for a different problem and has not been extended to this one.

\runin{The near-term work}
This is the cheapest entry in the chapter, because the environment exists. Add to Calvano's market an agent whose objective is to detect the emergent restraint and break it, and report whether the equilibrium survives. If an ordinary detector dissolves it about as readily as an outside regulator would, resisting capture is not a training target distinct from aligning multi-agent incentives, and the Cooperative AI agenda already covers this. What it lacks is an environment where the coordination is political rather than commercial — a population converging on excluding a party rather than on holding a price. It is also the only entry with no institutional prerequisite.
