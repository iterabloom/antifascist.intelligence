\chapter{Necessary Areas of Research}
\label{sec:11}
Chapters~\ref{sec:2} through \ref{sec:10} ran into problems they could not close, and set each one aside with a note. This chapter is those notes, collected and put in order. It is not a survey of what the field has left to do: every entry is a gap this book's own argument opens, and they are ranked by what that argument fails without.

The criterion produces an order almost nobody would choose on other grounds. The problems this book most needs solved are not the ones nearest to being solved, and the first two entries are the least tractable things here. A funder ranking by progress per dollar, or a lab ranking by what its people could start on Monday, would invert most of the list. This is not a work plan; it is an account of what the book is running on credit. A reader who wants the cheapest useful thing on it should turn to section~\ref{sec:11.6}, which says in its own entry why it could start today.

Each section closes by naming four things: the experiment that could begin now with means that already exist, the result that would falsify the approach, the data or access it would take, and the institutional condition without which it cannot run. Where there is no near-term experiment the section says so rather than inventing one, and three say so — the third of section~\ref{sec:11.2}'s questions, the aggregation half of section~\ref{sec:11.5}, and section~\ref{sec:11.9} entirely. The first of those is the second-ranked problem in the chapter, which is the shape of the situation and not a defect in the exercise.

One qualification governs every gap named here. Where I say a problem is unaddressed, the claim is bounded: I looked for published work under a particular description and did not find it, which is weaker than no such work existing. A search is not a survey, a program can be underway under a name I did not think to search, and the closer a gap sits to this book's own framing the less likely anyone has posed it in these words. What it does support is narrower and still useful: a researcher entering here will not find the problem already named and waiting. Each section names the nearest work I could find and says where it stops, because the distance between an existing program and the missing thing is what a researcher can actually use.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Whether a trained-in commitment survives a determined attempt to remove it, and whether interpretability can be trusted about a system with an incentive to defeat it. First because interpretability is the most load-bearing unsolved problem in this book. A commitment that fine-tunes out for twenty cents is not a floor, and the instrument that would show one holds is what the next entry needs. \emph{Nearest work:} tamper-resistant training built to survive hundreds of adversarial fine-tuning steps, and concept-injection experiments on whether a model's self-report tracks its own activations. Both stop at the same place — they establish that a behavior persists, not that a reason is what persisted.
  \item What is owed to a bearer, whether any arrangement can discharge it, and whether anyone outside could check. Chapter~\ref{sec:3} argues for building a prospective moral patient once the cheaper routes have had their attempt; this is the debt that argument leaves. \emph{Nearest work:} institutional review adapted into something binding, and the pet-trust form as the nearest existing instrument. Neither has been tried on a subject whose function is to refuse the institution reviewing it.
  \item Telling a recuperated dissent mechanism from a merely mediocre one, when both produce the same paperwork. \emph{Nearest work:} the democratic-backsliding indicator literature, which measures institutions rather than the mechanisms inside them, and section~\ref{sec:9.3.4}'s slope instrument, which needs a record an institution generated for other reasons.
  \item Telling a method that encoded the value from one that merely fit its test distribution, before deployment rather than after a failure exposes the difference. \emph{Nearest work:} the specification-gaming catalog, close to sixty documented instances, and the goal-misgeneralization results, where an agent carries every capability into a new distribution while pursuing the wrong goal competently. Both describe the failure after it has happened.
  \item Whether preference aggregation that works for one bounded game survives the conflicts an ethical system would have to arbitrate. \emph{Nearest work:} reinforcement learning from human feedback and its documented sycophancy failure. That names what goes wrong inside a rating scheme and leaves untouched the question outside it, of whose preferences are collected at all.
  \item A multi-agent system built to detect and resist capture by an authoritarian actor, as distinct from aligning multi-agent incentives with designer intent. \emph{Nearest work:} the pricing-algorithm simulation in which independent learners converged on supracompetitive pricing with no channel between them and no collusive objective. It documents the emergence and offers nothing that resists it.
  \item Whether apparent theory-of-mind competence rests on one underlying capacity or several unrelated ones. \emph{Nearest work:} the imaging dispute over whether the right temporoparietal junction is belief-specific, and machine models trained to pass false-belief tasks from observed behavior alone. The second sidesteps the first and is untested past simple gridworlds.
  \item Whether any face-to-feeling mapping survives once individual and cross-cultural variation are taken seriously. \emph{Nearest work:} the meta-analysis finding real above-chance cross-cultural recognition alongside a persistent in-group advantage that contact shrinks and does not remove. It establishes that the mapping is neither universal nor absent, which is the least usable of the three answers.
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:11.9} closes the chapter and is deliberately not on that list. The responsibility gap it describes is a question about how accountability should be attributed, and it would not be closed by anyone building anything. It is the condition the rest of the list runs on, which is why it comes last rather than first.

One gap this book records has no section here. Telling disagreement that deserves representation from disagreement produced by coercion or a captured information environment belongs to section~\ref{sec:7.2}, where the argument needing it is made, and it stays there.

Reading the nine institutional conditions together turns up what listing them apart had hidden. They are one condition wearing different clothes. Weights a vendor cannot withdraw when a result embarrasses it. A guardian who is not also the operator. Longitudinal access by somebody an institution cannot fire, reporting to a body with standing to receive the finding. Evaluation results published whether or not they flatter the method. Training checkpoints released rather than kept. Every one is a party outside the operator, holding access, able to act on what it finds. What keeps a party outside is the question none of the nine asks, and in the United States it was answered while this book was being finished. \emph{Trump v. Slaughter} removed, for most of the federal government, the protection from dismissal that made an outside party outside, and \emph{Trump v. Cook} kept it for one body on grounds no new body can have; section~\ref{sec:3.1} carries both. The year before had already shown how the thing comes apart. Independence decomposes into components — a director's tenure, a funding stream, a mandate — and an adversary takes the weakest: the consumer-finance bureau whose funding stream the Court had upheld in 2024 had that stream shut from inside in late 2025, by an acting director, removable at will since 2020, who declined to request the money under a reading of the statute that two courts then rejected \autocite{cfpb2025funding,seilalaw2020,cfsa2024}. A condition that names the outside party and not what keeps it there has named half of what it needs. The binding constraint on the research program in this chapter is not a research problem. Most of this work is fundable today and cannot be run.
