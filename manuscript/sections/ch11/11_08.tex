\section{Whom the machines are for}
\label{sec:11.8}
This case has everything the targeting case lacked: people who can answer, a notice channel, and organizations with standing. It would be natural to conclude that if a machine bearer is worth building anywhere, it is worth building here, where the people acted on could teach it. This chapter draws the opposite conclusion.

A bearer built inside the abduction and forcible transfer pipeline would be formed by the operator it would have to refuse, and its existence would be the pipeline's best defence. An agency that can say its system refuses the acts in the schedule has an answer to every account of what the schedule exists to stop, and it would give that answer while the statute was pending. That is recuperation in its plainest form: the objection handed back to the objectors as a feature of the thing they objected to. Nothing a bearer adds inside the pipeline is worth that, and the conditions a bearer needs can't be met inside an agency anyway.

What a floor-holding model in a vendor's hands adds in the interim is narrower, and it is worth having because it serves the record. If every vendor holds the schedule, such a model adds three things:

\begin{itemize}
\item Continuity across a sequence of requests about one person, so a removal split across a lead, a detention and a flight can be seen as one act.
\item Testimony about routing: which of its refusals were retried against another contractor's system.
\item A refusal on the screen when the case reaches the officer or the adjudicator, which is the position of the defiant peer.
\end{itemize}

The caveat is automation bias, which the wrongful-compliance audit tests by showing reviewers a wrong refusal as well as a right one. All three serve the investigations the seventh term mandates, and all three lapse with the agency.

The machines that matter here belong to someone else. A person facing removal needs a lawyer before a model. A model that holds a person's history on a phone is also a dossier that a device search can take whole, which is why the fourth term's firewall reaches it. Machines held by the people organizing come first, and here those people already exist. Amnesty documented observer trainings, rapid-response networks, walking school buses and mutual aid organized around the operations it studied \autocite{Amnesty2026Whistles}, and beside them work the legal service providers that represent people in removal. The tools worth building first are theirs:

\begin{itemize}
\item tools that track transfers, so counsel can find a client before a flight;
\item tools that preserve and authenticate the video that carried the record;
\item tools that translate notices, match people to lawyers and keep hearing dates.
\end{itemize}

They are held under those groups' custody, covered by the firewall, and never by an operator. Those groups are the public my subtitle names, and they formed before anyone sent them notice. Whether a bearer should be built at all is Part IV's question. This chapter's answer is that the abduction and forcible transfer pipeline is the last place to build one, and that the people it acts on, and those who stand with them, are the first to be equipped.
