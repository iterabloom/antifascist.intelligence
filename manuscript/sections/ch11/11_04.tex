\section{The statute}
\label{sec:11.4}
The power to remove is statutory, but so are the agency that exercises it, the means it uses and the money it spends, and the body that granted each can withdraw it.\note{
Congress has done this once. The Homeland Security Act of 2002 abolished the Immigration and Naturalization Service once its functions had been transferred. It divided those functions between two bureaus and barred the executive from using its reorganization authority to recombine them \autocite[§§442, 451, 471]{hsa2002}. That was a separation of offices written into statute. The enforcement bureau became the Bureau of Immigration and Customs Enforcement under a reorganization plan that took effect on 1 March 2003 \autocite{hdoc108_32}.
}

So the removal counterpart of the domestic clause puts two questions to the legislature. The first is whether it has authorized this agency, by these means, at this scale. The second is whether the statute that ends the agency carries the floor into whatever receives its functions.\note{
The precedent is also the warning. The 2002 Act ended one immigration agency and rebuilt its enforcement half inside a department organized around security. Amnesty International traces ICE to the aftermath of 11 September and to “an extremely problematic security framework” adopted in the years that followed \autocite{Amnesty2026PressRelease}. It now asks Congress to defund ICE and rescind its current funding, and asks House members to cosponsor the Melt ICE Act as “a crucial first step” \autocite{AmnestyUSA2026Letter}. However, starting over without a floor produced ICE.
}

Two bills in the current Congress answer the first question. The Abolish ICE Act ends the agency by name \autocite{hr7123}. The Melt ICE Act works through its powers: it releases everyone held in DHS detention and bars federal funds for detention and monitoring \autocite{hr7190}. Two further drafts reach the schedule's bar on identifying people by their faces. None can pass a Congress that in June 2026 voted ICE and CBP \$70 billion more \autocite{GT2026SecureAmerica}, and since 2025 most of the pipeline's money has come by reconciliation and stays available through fiscal 2029, past the reach of next year's appropriations bill \autocite{obbba2025}. A Congress with the votes to end the agency will find the text already written. Appendix~\ref{sec:A} gives the drafts and the money in full.
