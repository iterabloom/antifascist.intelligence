\section{Open Questions in Empathy and Theory-of-Mind Research}
\label{sec:11.7}
Section~\ref{sec:5.2.1} traces compassion to a neural circuit distinct from empathy, and section~\ref{sec:4.1.2} dismantles the standard mirror-neuron account of how one mind models another — a mechanism named as empathy's seat well before the evidence supported it. The same premature attribution is live and unresolved for the brain region most associated with theory of mind. Rebecca Saxe and Nancy Kanwisher's 2003 imaging work identified the right temporoparietal junction as selectively active when a person reasons about someone else's beliefs, and largely inactive otherwise \autocite{saxe2003people}. In 2008, Jason Mitchell reported activity in that same region during a task with nothing to do with belief attribution, and argued its actual job is more general — redirecting attention toward unexpected information, of which a false belief is one case \autocite{mitchell2008activity}. Neither claim has been withdrawn. Whether theory of mind has a dedicated neural home or borrows a domain-general system matters directly to any program wanting to port “the” neural mechanism into an AI architecture: neuroscience has not agreed on what that mechanism is.

Two live lines of AI research bear on theory of mind, and neither takes a side in that dispute: both treat the capacity behaviorally and ask whether it generalizes, rather than borrowing a neural mechanism from either camp. DeepMind's ToMnet, described in a 2018 paper, is trained purely on other agents' observed behavior, with no hand-built model of belief, and it learns to pass a version of the Sally-Anne false-belief test built for children \autocite{rabinowitz2018machine}. Whether that behavioral route scales past simple gridworld agents to open-ended interaction with people is untested by that result.

The dispute is sharper for large language models, trained on human text rather than a designed environment. In 2023, Stanford psychologist Michal Kosinski reported that GPT-4 solved 75 percent of a battery of false-belief tasks, matching the rate recorded for six-year-old children on comparable tests, and read it as evidence that theory-of-mind-like ability could emerge unplanned from language training alone \autocite{kosinski2023theory}. Harvard psychologist Tomer Ullman answered the same year: trivial rewording — leaving the belief structure intact while altering incidental detail — made the models fail questions they had just answered correctly \autocite{ullman2023large}. The two findings have not been reconciled. Whether a system that passes a false-belief test is tracking a belief or pattern-matching a benchmark's surface form is what a developmental account was supposed to help answer.

The developmental literature offers a specific target. Alison Gopnik and Janet Astington's 1988 study of three- to five-year-olds found that success on false-belief tasks correlates with success on a structurally different one — recognizing that an object made to look like something else, a sponge painted to resemble a rock, misrepresents what it is — and that both cluster around age four rather than developing on separate schedules \autocite{gopnik1988childrens}. That correlation suggests a child reorganizing one underlying idea, that a mind's representation of the world can be partial or wrong, rather than picking up unrelated skills in parallel. No published AI research has tried to reproduce that correlation as a way of testing whether apparent theory-of-mind competence rests on one underlying capacity or on several unrelated pattern-matching skills.

\runin{The near-term work}
Reproducing the correlation is the experiment: measure one system on structurally different mental-state tasks and ask whether the scores rise together. The dispute above supplies the falsifier: apply Ullman's rewording to both families, and a correlation that does not survive it is a fact about surface form rather than any underlying capacity. It needs batteries that vary belief structure and surface form separately, where existing benchmarks vary them together, and scores at intermediate training checkpoints rather than at the end, since the claim is that two things rise \emph{together}. Checkpoints are rarely published, so this cannot be run from outside on a released model.
