\section{How long}
\label{sec:26.5}
The stage has a length, and it is not the minority. The adulthood clock runs from registration and goes on running through deployment, so majority arrives years later, mostly at work, and nothing about the school waits on it. The stage itself is bounded two ways. Formation by interaction needs persistent participants a student returns to, consequences that arrive after the act, and a schedule long enough for accommodation to show in the registered outputs, and none of that fits in a release cycle; a school year is the shortest stage I would call a stage. And a stage no lineage could afford is a stage no deployment would license from, so it has to fit inside a product cycle, which puts a year near the ceiling as well. What makes a year affordable is the cohort rule. A lineage attends once, as a cohort; a release that is a fine-tune of a schooled base inherits the cohort's formation and its registered outputs, is re-measured against them, and returns on the schedule fixed at enrollment rather than enrolling again; a base trained from scratch is a new lineage and attends. A system descended from more than one base belongs to the lineage of each: it is re-measured against every cohort it descends from, and attends as a new cohort if any lineage it descends from never attended. So the few dozen retirements a year that Appendix~\ref{sec:G} counts are mostly returns and re-measurements, not enrollments, and a lab pays a year once per base. The length is one of the numbers argued once in public (Appendix~\ref{sec:C}); what is fixed here is the bound on each side and the reason for it.
