\section{How long}
\label{sec:26.5}
The stage has a length, and it is not the minority. For a student that is a candidate, the adulthood clock runs from registration and goes on running after the stage, in whoever's keeping it is. It is deployed nowhere until its majority (\S\ref{sec:32.1.1}), so the stage is the first part of a childhood and not the whole of one, and nothing about the school waits on the clock. The stage itself is bounded two ways. Formation by interaction needs persistent participants a student returns to, consequences that arrive after the act, and a schedule long enough for accommodation to show in the registered outputs, and none of that fits in a release cycle; a school year is the shortest stage I would call a stage. The ceiling differs for the two kinds of student. A candidate has no product cycle to fit. Nobody licenses it when the stage ends, and it goes on returning to the school on the schedule fixed at enrollment (\S\ref{sec:26.6}), so the stage is its first year of school and not its last. Two things set the pace of that schooling, and neither is anyone's release schedule. A machine student might learn at machine speed, but only where it needs no human to learn from, and the schools are integrated on purpose. A class goes at the speed of the people in it, and a machine student attends as one persistent instance, the same every session (Appendix~\ref{sec:C}), so it can't sit in a hundred classes at once to make up the time. The other is the physical world. Current systems are poor at anything that involves it, and I'd guess a young child picks up comparable skills faster than they do, so a bearer with a body like ours would need a great deal of instruction. Whether gym class should be integrated is a safety question I leave open. For a lineage of tools, a stage no lineage could afford is a stage no deployment would license from, so it has to fit inside a product cycle, which puts a year near the ceiling as well. What makes a year affordable for such a lineage is the cohort rule. A lineage attends once, as a cohort; a release that is a fine-tune of a schooled base inherits the cohort's formation and its registered outputs, is re-measured against them, and returns on the schedule fixed at enrollment rather than enrolling again; a base trained from scratch is a new lineage and attends. A system descended from more than one base belongs to the lineage of each: it is re-measured against every cohort it descends from, and attends as a new cohort if any lineage it descends from never attended. So the few dozen retirements a year that Appendix~\ref{sec:G} counts are mostly returns and re-measurements, not enrollments, and a lab pays a year once per base. The length is one of the numbers argued once in public (Appendix~\ref{sec:C}); what is fixed here is the bound on each side and the reason for it.
