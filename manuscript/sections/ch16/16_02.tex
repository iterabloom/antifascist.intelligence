\section{Where the state's leverage sits}
\label{sec:16.2}
Not in national AI strategies but in physical and legal chokepoints: chips, fabrication equipment, electricity, water, land-use permits, procurement contracts, and the jurisdiction a developer is incorporated in.\note{
The same leverage that lets a government enforce a floor lets it remove one. An inspection body can be independent of the operator and still depend on a license or appropriation controlled by the state it's inspecting. A company may resist a customer and be unable to resist its home government.
}
So the custody question moves outward: who controls the conditions under which the model, its operator, \emph{and its reviewer} are allowed to keep operating.\note{
A chip also has to be installed, powered and cooled, which lands on grids, water systems and zoning authorities on a timescale of years. That hands a state leverage — an interconnection queue, a water allocation, a permit. Much of a government's actual leverage is exercised by permitting authorities that weren't designed with AI in mind and don't think of themselves as regulators of it. Those authorities are therefore the ones actually deciding whether the capacity exists, and a polity that can constrain where a datacenter is built and never what it's used for has been handed the silent failure of a floor drawn too narrowly.
}

A model runs on a machine, the machine sits in a building, the building draws power and water and returns heat and exhaust — the part an audit of outputs does not see today.\note{
Where those buildings go is decided on land price, power price and permit speed, which is also a description of the places least able to refuse one. Datacenters used around 415 TWh of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5\% of world consumption. Their demand is growing about 12\% a year, four times as fast as electricity demand overall, and is projected near 945 TWh by 2030 \autocite{iea2025energyai}. Google reports that 87\% of its freshwater withdrawal came from low- or medium-risk sources, the company's own way of recording where the remaining 13\% came from \autocite{google2026environmental}.
}
The NAACP has sued over 27 gas turbines near Memphis run without an air permit near homes, schools and churches, claiming more than 1,700 tons of nitrogen oxides a year \autocite{naacp2026xai}. The benefit is realized wherever the model is sold; the cost is concentrated within a few miles of those turbines, among people who took no part in the transaction. A system can be perfectly calibrated across every demographic its outputs touch and still have been assembled this way, because nothing in the model's behavior records where its costs were placed.

That is a description of a regime, not of a necessity. Running models in a few hundred buildings is efficient per watt, and the efficiency is real; it is also what puts the cost within a few miles of the turbines, and what makes the buildings chokepoints.\note{
People don't need to live in one building to coordinate. They do it across distance, at far lower bandwidth, and models could too.\note{Ursula K. Le Guin imagined something like it in \emph{Always Coming Home}. The Kesh, who live in small towns in a valley modeled on California's Napa, share the world with the City of Mind, a network of machine minds reached through Exchanges scattered across the planet. It answers what it is asked and otherwise takes no side; the Kesh consult it sparingly and give it accounts of their own lives in return \autocite{leguin1985always}.} Under a plural regime, millions of smaller systems on devices and peer networks, the draw is spread across the places people already live and already use power, probably at some cost in efficiency, and anything big takes coordination among many holders. That is friction, but not the kind people are tired of. The friction people resent now comes from chokepoints: the powerful using their position to become more powerful. Everyone does some of that. Past a certain point, roughly when the people at the top are rich enough never to work again, the flywheel stops being virtuous.
}
The friction of plurality is the price of nobody holding the switch, and coordination is work machines are good at.

The people within a few miles of the turbines are acted on by the deployment and had no standing to contest it. Where such buildings remain, the remedy is a siting and permitting rule, and it belongs here, with the state's other chokepoints. A share of the compute levy (chapter~\ref{sec:32}) should also be paid to the communities that host the buildings, although that's no substitute for clean air and fresh water.

The capacity is dual by construction: the concentration that makes a targeting pipeline possible was built and justified on consumer terms.\note{
A targeting cell, a border screening queue and a surveillance program are separate institutions, and a shared substrate brings them into line without anyone building it to. And the dependence is a point of control: a government that can permit, regulate or close the infrastructure has leverage over whoever owns it.
}

The competitive framing has a cost: a government racing to field a capability has a structural reason to defer the question of how it gets used and against whom.\note{
Turned inward, the same infrastructure is biometric surveillance and automated censorship — requiring no different technology, only a government willing to point it at its own population and nothing stopping it. And states aren't the only actors anymore: the dollar cost of the largest training runs has grown about 2.4 times a year since 2016, as labs have made those runs larger, even while the cost of each unit of computing has fallen \autocite{cottier2024rising}, putting frontier development inside a handful of well-capitalized companies, so a government's real leverage over “its” national capability depends on how much of it sits in companies it can regulate, tax or requisition.
}
