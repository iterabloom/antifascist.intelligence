\chapter{Custody, Compulsion, and the State}
\label{sec:16}
On 29 August 2012 a drone strike in the Yemeni village of Khashamir killed Salem bin Ali Jaber, an imam who had preached against al-Qaeda a few days before, and Waleed bin Ali Jaber, a policeman who had gone with him to meet three young men who wanted to discuss the sermon. Salem was Faisal bin Ali Jaber's brother-in-law and Waleed his nephew, and Faisal spent years asking the United States why. When the family sued in Washington, the court of appeals held the question nonjusticiable, and the judge who wrote its opinion added, separately, that the doctrine compelling that answer was inadequate to a standard operating procedure “that will be replicated hundreds if not thousands of times” \autocite{binalijaber2017}.

When the state operates the system, it also controls the rules, the evidence, the infrastructure, and the remedies meant to constrain it — and people outside its jurisdiction, like the bin Ali Jabers, may have no standing to contest anything.
