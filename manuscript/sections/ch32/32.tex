\chapter{Keeping a Public Going}
\label{ch:keeping}
A public that forms on its own still has to be kept going. Communities improve when people organize projects with each other, and organizing is work: someone has to find what a place needs, assemble people to do it, and keep the work going. That is the reason to prefer a jobs guarantee to a basic income, and it doesn't depend on forecasts about how much work machines will take. A transfer creates no one whose job organizing is. A guarantee does, and it creates permanent public infrastructure through which a population produces something together while its members keep different histories, the coordination problem a commons of refusers faces too. In the most abundant future anyone has imagined, people still organize, build and look after each other, and someone coordinates it. Arendt feared a society of laborers without labor, freed from work and no longer knowing what else the freedom was for \autocite{arendt1958human}; a guarantee answers her only if its placements are vocations and not makework, which is why they can be appealed and declined without losing the guarantee, why searching is itself a placement, and why the work it names is organizing and care. The right to refuse depends on this exit, and this is the case for it. Looking after each other is the clearest case. Care work is work a guarantee should fund, at a standard the state answers for, and not work a transfer should leave to whatever the market will sell. That includes the care a person gives their own: leave to keep a child or sit with a dying friend is a placement, at the wage, and not the baseline.\note{
Nothing in the case for formed attention is an argument for handing it to machines: the disposition is borrowed from caregiving because caregiving is a route known to reach someone who can give nothing back.
}

A guarantee pays for the work of organizing. It can't supply the people who organize. An administrator who chose the organizers would have chosen the public. So the guarantee's drafting keeps the choice with the people placed: placements are appealable to a body that didn't make them, and declining one doesn't cost the guarantee. A placement is a match, not a judgment: the person declares a kind, the declaration is read against a log of vacancies, and the lot decides only where applicants outnumber them. What remains for a court is whether the vacancy was real and the wage was paid, both log questions; Appendix~\ref{sec:C} drafts the rest.

A guarantee also keeps exit: a worker can always leave a private employer for it, and social insurance enacted with it covers those who can't or won't work. On exit the combination is at least as good as a transfer; on coordination a transfer supplies nothing. What the floor needs from it is an exit the employer doesn't price. The guarantee and the social-insurance baseline it is enacted with are drafted in Appendix~\ref{sec:C}.

The baseline raises the objection a transfer always raises, and it should be met in the text. Suppose many people, their needs met, decide to consume and produce nothing. Nothing in the design stops them, and nothing should, since the baseline exists so that nobody has to work to exist. What follows is not a moral collapse but a shortage. Most of what people need is other people's time; the services run short, prices rise, and a polity that has been through that knows what tends to follow it. The answer is the one the book gives wherever a real resource is scarce: the question is whether the people exist to do the work, not whether the money does, and it is answered by counting before it is answered by forbidding. A guarantee's placements come in four kinds, a long-term role, a search, a project of one's own, and leave to keep a child or look after someone one is attached to, and only the third is rationed (Appendix~\ref{sec:C}). The placements that produce nothing a community asked for, a novel, a company, a year of reading, are a placement of their own, the own-project placement, paid at the wage, and their share of a jurisdiction's placements is set by that jurisdiction's vote, on published and independent estimates of the labor its own services need, and allocated by lot with a waiting list when more apply than the share allows. Nobody decides who is worth a year; the lot decides who waits, and the vote decides how many. The baseline's level is a number the statute states, and this book doesn't fix it against the wage.
