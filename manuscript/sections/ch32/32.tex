\chapter{Keeping a Public Going}
\label{ch:keeping}
A public that forms on its own still has to be kept going. Communities improve when people organize projects with each other, and organizing is work: someone has to find what a place needs, assemble people to do it, and keep the work going. That is the reason a basic income isn't enough by itself, and it doesn't depend on forecasts about how much work machines will take. A transfer creates no one whose job organizing is. A jobs guarantee does, and it creates permanent public infrastructure through which a population produces something together while its members keep different histories, the coordination problem a commons of refusers faces too. In the most abundant future anyone has imagined, people still organize, build and look after each other, and someone coordinates it. Arendt feared a society of laborers without labor, freed from work and no longer knowing what else the freedom was for \autocite{arendt1958human}; a jobs guarantee answers her only if its placements are vocations and not makework, which is why they can be appealed and declined without losing the jobs guarantee, why searching is itself a placement, and why the work it names is organizing and care. The right to refuse depends on this exit, and this is the case for it. Looking after each other is the clearest case. Care work is work a jobs guarantee should fund, at a standard the state answers for, and not work a transfer should leave to whatever the market will sell. That includes the care a person gives their own: leave to keep a child or sit with a dying friend is a placement, at the wage, and not the basic income.\note{
Nothing in the case for formed attention is an argument for handing it to machines: the disposition is borrowed from caregiving because caregiving is a route known to reach someone who can give nothing back.
}

A jobs guarantee pays for the work of organizing. It can't supply the people who organize. An administrator who chose the organizers would have chosen the public. So the jobs guarantee's drafting keeps the choice with the people placed: placements are appealable to a body that didn't make them, and declining one doesn't cost the jobs guarantee. A placement is a match, not a judgment: the person declares a kind, the declaration is read against a log of vacancies, and the lot decides only where applicants outnumber them, whatever the role pays: a role may pay more than the jobs guarantee wage, within a band the statute sets, and none is ever awarded. Nor may a role turn away anyone willing to learn it; where the work needs a qualification, the placement is the paid training that leads to it. What remains for a court is whether the vacancy was real and the wage was paid, both log questions; Appendix~\ref{sec:C} drafts the rest.

A jobs guarantee also keeps exit: a worker can always leave a private employer for it, and a basic income enacted with it means that nobody has to work to exist. On exit the combination does everything a basic income alone would do; on coordination a basic income alone supplies nothing. What the floor needs from it is an exit the employer doesn't price. Where the statute sets its wage decides how well that works, in both directions: set high, refusing costs almost nobody anything, but the jobs guarantee becomes most of the economy and the state the one employer left to refuse; set low, other employers survive, and refusing costs whatever the refuser earned above it. The jobs guarantee and the basic income it is enacted with are drafted in Appendix~\ref{sec:C}.

So this book proposes both: a jobs guarantee at a living wage, and beneath it a \emph{basic income}, paid to everyone present in the jurisdiction, working or not, at a level the statute states below the jobs guarantee wage. It is paid on top of work and not instead of it, so a placement or a private job adds its whole wage to it and working always pays. It raises the objection a transfer always raises, and it should be met in the text. Suppose many people, their needs met, decide to consume and produce nothing. Nothing in the design stops them, and nothing should, since the basic income exists so that nobody has to work to exist. What follows is not a moral collapse but a shortage. Most of what people need is other people's time; the services run short, prices rise, and a polity that has been through that knows what tends to follow it. The answer is the one the book gives wherever a real resource is scarce: the question is whether the people exist to do the work, not whether the money does, and it is answered by counting before it is answered by forbidding. A jobs guarantee's placements come in four kinds, a long-term role, a search, a project of one's own, and leave to keep a child or look after someone one is attached to, and only the third is rationed (Appendix~\ref{sec:C}). The placements that produce nothing a community asked for, a novel, a company, a year of reading, are a placement of their own, the own-project placement, paid at the wage, and their share of a jurisdiction's placements is set by that jurisdiction's vote, on published and independent estimates of the labor its own services need, and allocated by lot with a waiting list when more apply than the share allows. Nobody decides who is worth a year; the lot decides who waits, and the vote decides how many.

That is also the answer to the demand, which many of the basic income's own advocates will make, that it be paid at the wage. Paid on top of work at parity, it would make every placement pay twice the floor. The wage sits above it because the work a community counts on has to get done, and the community's vote on how many paid years of one's own it can afford has to bind; a basic income at the wage would decide that vote for it.

The money people don't need to spend is the other half of the shortage. A basic income paid to everyone puts spending in every hand on the same day, and the spending nobody needed to do is what would press on prices. So it is paid by default into a \emph{U-bond}: a Treasury savings instrument of its own, indexed to inflation at a real return of zero, so that it protects what is parked there without competing for other savings, and redeemable at any time without penalty. That is where it differs from a Series~I bond, which can't be cashed in its first year and costs the last three months' interest if cashed before five \autocite{treasurydirect2026ibonds}; a floor someone lives on has to be spendable this month. The default does the restraining, not a lock. A person who needs the money spends it and the floor works; a person who doesn't leaves it where it is. Whether it restrains anything is a published trend, redemptions against payments month by month and by income class, and if redemptions track payments, the default isn't restraining anything and this paragraph's argument fails. The U-bond is covered by the firewall of chapter~\ref{sec:11}: it can't be garnished, and its records are never a lead. The basic income's level is a number the statute states, below the wage; this book fixes the relation and not the number.\note{
A universal payment is a large program on paper, and taxes on earnings bring much of it back from the people who work; the gross figure should be stated beside the net, not hidden behind it. Indexation also costs the Treasury most exactly when inflation is high, which is when the U-bond is doing its job.
}
