1.2. Aims and Objectives
This book offers specific design principles drawn from psychology, neuroscience, and moral development; technical methods for building them into a learning system; policy levers, named and evaluated; and proposals ambitious enough to match the problem. Chief among them is a federal jobs guarantee to blunt AI-driven labor disruption, argued for in chapter 6 with its costings and its strongest objection both on the page. Two others I treat more cautiously than the word "proposal" would suggest, and say so where they appear: export controls on surveillance-enabling hardware, which chapter 6 assesses as a lever with a mixed record rather than advocating, and certification with real teeth behind it, which the book argues for in principle while being clear about how little of it currently exists. The next chapters organize around five aims:
<<list>>
1. Define the ethical frameworks a design can actually draw on.
2. Examine what cognitive science and developmental psychology show about empathy, social reasoning, and moral development in humans, and what of that is portable to an artificial system.
3. Assess what machine learning methods actually exist for building a system that behaves according to ethical norms, not just one trained to avoid the specific failures its designers happened to anticipate.
4. Confront the risks: bias, brittleness, goals that drift from their original motivations, and fascism.
5. Set out what collaboration this actually requires — across disciplines, with the public, and across borders.
<</list>>
