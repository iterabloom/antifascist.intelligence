1.3. Mindset and Motivation in AI Behavior
Three ideas borrowed from how humans develop recur across this book's design chapters: the stance a system takes toward its own failures, what drives it to act absent an external reward, and what it takes to build any of this with people who don't share a discipline's vocabulary. This section names them and points to where each is actually developed, rather than arguing them twice.

<<h>>Growth Mindset<</h>>
Psychologist Carol Dweck's distinction between a fixed mindset, which treats ability as static, and a growth mindset, which treats it as built through effort and experience, matters for an AI system's moral judgment as much as for a person's: a system that treats its own errors as data has room to keep improving; one built to defend its prior outputs does not. Section 4.1.1 works out what that disposition would need to look like architecturally, and what it would cost to get wrong.

<<h>>Intrinsic Motivation and Autonomy<</h>>
A moral sense that only activates under supervision is not much of one. Section 4.6 covers what it would mean for a system to act well for reasons other than an externally specified reward — the engineering analog of intrinsic motivation — and what happens to a system's autonomy once it has something like that drive: real independence from moment-to-moment human correction, and a correspondingly real question about what keeps it aligned once that correction is gone. Section 3.3.3 covers the boundary question directly: how much autonomy a system should have before independence itself becomes the risk.

<<h>>Interdisciplinary Collaboration<</h>>
None of the above gets built by one field working alone. A machine-learning lab, a psychology department, and an ethics program rarely share a budget, a dataset, or a deadline by default; building AI this way means changing that default, not hoping the fields converge on their own. Chapter 6 covers what that collaboration actually requires — funding structured to force it, venues that make it a byproduct of doing the work, and the international partnerships that let it hold across borders — with the real institutions already doing it, not a wish list of what one might do.
