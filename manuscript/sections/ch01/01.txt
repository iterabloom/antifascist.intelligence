Intellect estranged from compassion is a description of a great deal of the twentieth century's worst actors, and a live risk for what gets built next: a machine intelligence smart enough to accomplish almost anything, indifferent to whether what it accomplishes helps or harms humanity. This book asks what it would take to build a superintelligent AI that is altruistic and antifascist by design — one that stands, by definition, for humanity and against concentrated, unaccountable power.

How most humans come to have that stance — empathy, altruism, a disposition to resist oppression rather than serve it — is itself an open question in psychology and neuroscience. Constructing anything like it means starting from uncertainty about the inherited case — the one that was four billion years in its making.

Chapter 2 takes up the design question directly: what compassion and empathy would need to look like in a system built to have them, and what a system's own self-model can and cannot be trusted to report. Chapter 3 is the pivot, and it was the last thing written: an unconditional floor of things a system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate preference, the three ways of building one, and what each costs. Chapters 4 and 5 are the learned half that sits above that floor — what cognitive science and machine learning offer a system perceiving and modeling other minds, and what moral psychology suggests about how ethical judgment develops past a fixed rulebook. Chapter 6 asks what goes wrong regardless — bias, brittleness, goals that drift from their original motivations, and deliberate misuse. Chapter 7 turns that question on the book itself, on the machinery that produces these systems. Chapter 8 covers everything outside the system: the people and institutions a project like this depends on, and then what makes a well-designed system the norm rather than the exception — economic incentive, policy, privacy, accountability, and the geopolitics of the technology. Chapter 9 names the specific research questions still open. Chapter 10 closes on what a system built this way would be for, and what would count as evidence the project is working.

Three claims in what follows are worth flagging in advance, because together they set the standard I am holding this book to. The first is that one hard question about machine subjects has an answer checkable from outside. Whether a system can be wronged in the way that suffering names turns on whether anything about it persists across a session — a fact about its architecture, settled by inspection, requiring nothing the system says about itself. Section 2.4.1 makes that argument, and section 2.4.4 follows it to the point where consent stops applying at all. The second is that the book's central term does not yet cash out as an instrument. I define fascism structurally so that a system could in principle be asked to detect it, and chapter 9 is where I account for how far that is from a detector anyone can currently build.

The third took me most of the book to see, and stating it here rather than where I found it is the difference between a commitment and a discovery. Almost everything this book proposes about accountability is machinery for aggregating what people want: consultations, citizen juries, participatory design, deliberative polling, feedback collected from raters at scale. All of it makes public preference more legible and more actionable, and none of it protects anybody from public preference. A perfectly deliberative, perfectly informed, perfectly representative process that concludes some group should be watched has produced a legitimate output by every criterion of aggregation there is. So what makes a system antifascist cannot be that it faithfully tracks what people want. It has to be that there are things it will not do to a person regardless of who wants them — a floor that holds against the aggregate, including a well-formed and popular aggregate. Chapter 3 works out what such a floor would be, who could hold it, and what each way of building one costs; section 8.2.2 shows the governance machinery running out at exactly this line, and section 8.7.7 shows what its absence does to the book's own account of democracy. A reader should carry all three through the design chapters, because the design chapters lean on all three.

Chapter 11 is a glossary. The book uses a fair number of terms in senses narrower than their ordinary ones — compassion as distinct from empathy, fascism as a structural signature, a floor, recuperation, alignment — and a reader who wants those pinned down before meeting them in an argument will find them there.
