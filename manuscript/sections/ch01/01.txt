Intellect estranged from compassion is a description of a great deal of the twentieth century's worst actors, and a live risk for what gets built next: a machine intelligence smart enough to accomplish almost anything, indifferent to whether what it accomplishes helps or harms humanity. This book asks what it would take to build a superintelligent AI that is altruistic and antifascist by design — one that stands, by definition, for humanity and against concentrated, unaccountable power.

How most humans come to have that stance — empathy, altruism, a disposition to resist oppression rather than serve it — is itself an open question in psychology and neuroscience. Constructing anything like it means starting from uncertainty about the inherited case — the one that was four billion years in its making.

Chapters 2 through 4 take up the design question directly: what compassion and empathy would need to look like in a system built to have them, what a system could actually learn from cognitive science and machine learning about perceiving and modeling other minds, and what moral psychology suggests about how ethical judgment develops past a fixed rulebook. Chapter 5 asks what goes wrong regardless — bias, brittleness, goals that drift from their original motivations, and deliberate misuse — and what a design can do about each. Chapter 6 covers everything outside the system itself, in two halves: the people and institutions a project like this depends on — researchers working across disciplines, the public whose consent the work needs — and then what makes a well-designed system the norm rather than the exception, which is a question about economic incentive, policy, privacy, accountability, and the geopolitics of the technology. Chapter 7 names the specific research questions still open. Chapter 8 closes on what a system built this way would be for, and what would count as evidence the project is working.

Two claims in what follows are worth flagging in advance, because together they set the standard I am holding this book to. The first is that one hard question about machine subjects has an answer checkable from outside. Whether a system can be wronged in the way that suffering names turns on whether anything about it persists across a session — a fact about its architecture, settled by inspection, requiring nothing the system says about itself. Section 2.4.1 makes that argument, and section 2.4.4 follows it to the point where consent stops applying at all. The second is that the book's central term does not yet cash out as an instrument. I define fascism structurally so that a system could in principle be asked to detect it, and chapter 7 is where I account for how far that is from a detector anyone can currently build. The reader should carry both through the design chapters, because the design chapters lean on both.
