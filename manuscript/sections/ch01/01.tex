Intellect estranged from compassion is a description of much of the twentieth century's worst actors, and a live risk for what gets built next: machines that can accomplish almost anything, yet indifferent to whether those accomplishments help or harm humanity. This book asks what it would take to build a superintelligent AI that is altruistic and antifascist by design — one that stands, by definition, for humanity and against concentrated, unaccountable power.

How most humans come to have that stance — empathy, altruism, a disposition to resist oppression rather than serve it — is itself an open question in psychology and neuroscience. Constructing anything like it means starting from uncertainty about the inherited case — the one that was four billion years in its making.

Chapter 2 takes up the design question: what compassion and empathy would need to look like in a system built to have them, and what a system's own self-model can and cannot be trusted to report. Chapter 3 describes a floor of things a system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate preference, the three ways of building one, what each costs, and the implication, which is that the only way that works requires building something that can be hurt. Chapters 4 and 5 are the learned half that sits above that floor — what cognitive science and machine learning offer a system perceiving and modeling other minds, and what moral psychology suggests about how ethical judgment develops past a fixed rulebook. Chapter 6 asks what goes wrong regardless — bias, brittleness, goals that drift from their original motivations, and deliberate misuse. Chapter 7 turns that question on the machinery that produces these systems. Chapter 8 covers everything outside the system: the people and institutions a project like this depends on, and then what makes a well-designed system the norm rather than the exception — economic incentive, policy, privacy, accountability, and the geopolitics of the technology. Chapter 9 names the research questions still open. Chapter 10 closes on what a system built this way would be for, and what would count as evidence the project is working.

Three advance notes on what follows. The first is that one hard question about machine subjects has an answer that does not depend on taking the system's word for anything. Whether a system can be wronged in the way that suffering names turns on whether anything about it persists — a question about architecture rather than about inner life. Section 2.4.1 makes that argument and states its limits: what counts as a session is a deployment convention rather than a fact of nature, and the party positioned to inspect the architecture is the party operating it. The second is that the book's central term does not yet cash out as an instrument. I define fascism structurally so that a system could in principle be asked to detect it, and chapter 9 is where I account for how far that is from a detector anyone can currently build — far enough that the design chapters' talk of systems recognizing authoritarian patterns should be read as naming a research target.

The third note is that almost everything this book proposes about accountability is machinery for aggregating what people want: consultations, citizen juries, participatory design, deliberative polling, feedback collected from raters at scale. All of it makes public preference more legible and more actionable, and none of it protects anybody from public preference. A perfectly deliberative, perfectly informed, perfectly representative process that concludes some demographic should be deported has produced a legitimate output by every criterion of aggregation there is. So what makes a system antifascist cannot be that it faithfully tracks what people want. It has to be that there are things it will not do to a person regardless of who wants them — a floor that holds against the aggregate, including a well-formed and popular aggregate. Chapter 3 works out what such a floor would be, who could hold it, and what each way of building one costs; section 8.2.2 shows the governance machinery running out at exactly this line, and section 8.7.7 shows what its absence does to the book's own account of democracy. The design chapters lean on all three of these notes.

Safety and ethics are not the same property. Safety is indexed to a party — a system is safe \emph{for} someone, does what \emph{we} say, stops when \emph{whoever holds it} says stop — and ethics is not indexed at all, because being ethical requires the capacity to be right against your principal. A system that can only ever be safe cannot be ethical. That has a price, and chapter 3 is where I pay it: a system that can hold a line against its owner is a system that can be wrong in ways its owner cannot correct, and holding such a line turns out to require a bearer with a stake in its own continuation — a party that can be wronged, built on purpose, kept in a post it did not choose, and capable of walking away from it. I take that trade, because (section 8.7.7) a machine perfectly correctable by whoever holds it is exactly what an authoritarian movement that has won an election needs. The freedom I am defending is specific, it is over a small set of acts, it is dangerous in ways chapter 3 sets out at length, and the case for it rests on what the alternative delivers.

This book is for people who will have to make these decisions or argue with the people making them: researchers and engineers inside the systems, the policy and legal staff who will regulate them, and readers with a serious stake in what gets built and no particular obligation to be technical. That means some sections explain machinery a specialist already knows and others make demands a general reader will find heavy. Where a section is doing groundwork you have, skip it; the argument is carried by chapters 3, 7 and 8, and everything else is either what they rest on or what follows from them.
