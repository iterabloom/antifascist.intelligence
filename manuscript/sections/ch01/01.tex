\chapter{“Twenty Seconds”}
\label{sec:1}
An intelligence officer, describing his role in a targeting pipeline built around machine-generated kill lists, told a reporter he spent twenty seconds on each name, long enough to check that the target was a man: “It doesn't take a long time to tell if someone has a male or a female voice.” He added that he had zero added value as a human, apart from being a stamp of approval. “It saved a lot of time” \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. In his account, that was what “human in the loop” amounted to under operational tempo.\note{Automation-bias research predicts this delegation: when other demands compete for their attention, people given a machine's recommendation check it less and defer to it more \autocite{parasuraman2010complacency}.}

One interpretation is that the officer is a subject who was given too little time, and the fix is to govern him better — more time, a second reviewer, additional conscience installed in the loop. Through this \emph{social subjection} lens, the person is a subject who stands outside a machine and works it as a tool: a driver and a car, a worker and a lathe. The person has a role, a name, responsibilities, and a conscience that can be addressed. An alternative lens is \emph{machinic enslavement}, where the person is a component of the machine, assembled with its other parts into a system that regulates them together; the machine uses the person as one of its organs, and what the person contributes is a function performed at a rate the system sets. Doctorow's name for the officer's position is the reverse centaur: not a person assisted by a machine but a machine's component made of a person \autocite{doctorow2026reversecentaur}. The two are not mutually exclusive; a person can hold a role in one register while being a part in the other \autocite[Plateau 13, “The Apparatus of Capture”: there is enslavement when human beings are themselves constituent pieces of a machine they compose among themselves and with other things, under the control and direction of a higher unity, and subjection when the higher unity constitutes the human being as a subject linked to a new exterior object, which can be an animal, a tool, or even a machine]{deleuze1987plateaus}, and the twenty-seconds officer held both. As a part, he was where the pipeline produced its stamp, and twenty seconds was that part's cycle time. As a subject, he was addressed throughout: he described to a journalist the dissonance between the news of civilian deaths and the pressure at work for more targets. And even as the dissonance reached his conscience, he went along. It registered and decided nothing, or at least nothing that prevented the Israel Defense Forces from killing 100,000 people in Gaza between October 2023 and October 2025 \autocite{gomezugarte2025uncertainty,acosta2026gaza,economist2025gaza}; the judgment that the policy was disproportionate came, he said, in retrospect.

And the account of that particular officer was published in April of 2024 by +972 Magazine. It is anyone's guess as to whether that officer's decision to speak to a journalist prevented one additional death, 50,000 additional deaths, or none at all. And what would have happened if the officer had refused after two weeks in the role — say, hypothetically, in November 2023? We are talking about an intelligence officer using Lavender to assemble kill lists. His day-to-day working environment was probably closer to a civilian desk job than an action movie. Should he have sought repeated meetings with his superiors, up the chain of command, while refusing to approve targets for airstrikes that could reasonably have been expected to cause civilian deaths? Arguably that would have been the ethical thing to do. In that counterfactual, would there have been fewer dead?

What was missing wasn't a conscience; he had one. A conscience works by making a decision cost something, but here the costs netted the other way: “We were constantly being pressured: ‘Bring us more targets.’ They really shouted at us” \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. He was a component with scruples, but the tempo held: the scruples changed nothing he contributed, and he became a stamp. A component with scruples that doesn't hold the tempo becomes the “human bottleneck” a former commander of Israel's signals-intelligence unit wrote a book about removing \autocite{ys2021humanmachine}. What made the officer a component was the arrangement, not his attitude. A component can go along reluctantly or eagerly. At Eichmann's trial Arendt saw a thoughtless functionary \autocite{arendt1963eichmann}; what he had said in Argentina four years before, with nothing at stake, shows that the functionary was a show he put on for Jerusalem \autocite{stangneth2014eichmann}. What the pipeline let the officer contribute was a fixed operation at a fixed rate: he saw each case as the pipeline described it, one at a time, in the time the pipeline gave him. Whether anything could stand in that position and not be a component is the question of Part V.

Investigative reporting described three systems working together in Gaza: one, called the Gospel, generating building and infrastructure targets, with sources saying most suggestions were private residences; a second, Lavender, scoring individuals by estimated militant affiliation, producing lists said to have marked as many as 37,000 people, understood internally to be wrong about one time in ten; and a third, called Where’s Daddy?, tracking when flagged people returned home, so airstrikes could be timed to when they were with their families \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. The Israeli military disputed the account. The Minab and Maven accounts below rest on investigative reporting the parties dispute as well. I take them as reported.

\runin{Maven: the American version, now with GenAI acceleration}

The bombing of the school in Minab killed 156 people, 120 of them schoolchildren \autocite[para.~92]{UNFFMIran2026}.\note{Counts vary by source and date, from 156 (the Minab prosecutor's final toll, also given by Amnesty International) to 168 (the town's mayor, in the first days). The UN mission gives the prosecutor's figures and also reports independent information of 157 dead, 123 of them children aged 13 or younger \autocite[paras.~92–93]{UNFFMIran2026}.} The school's civilian function was visible in open-source satellite imagery, and the target coding still carried a designation inherited from an adjoining Revolutionary Guard installation. A UN fact-finding mission reported reasonable grounds to believe the bombing was the war crime of making a civilian object the object of an attack \autocite{ohchr2026minabpress}, and that the school was clearly identifiable with no information indicating military use \autocite[paras.~81–86, 124–126]{UNFFMIran2026}. It found that the United States failed in its obligation to do everything feasible to verify the target, counting the updating of targeting data among the feasible precautions, and it put the failure beyond negligence \autocite[paras.~124–125]{UNFFMIran2026}. Amnesty International reached a parallel conclusion, in language that tracks the duty to verify in Article 57(2)(a)(i) of Additional Protocol I: that forces may have relied on outdated intelligence and failed to do everything feasible to verify the target \autocite{Amnesty2026Minab}.

It happened on the morning of 28 February, the first day of the Iran campaign \autocite{Amnesty2026Minab,crs2026iranwar}. The day before, the Secretary had announced that the vendor (Anthropic) whose model (Claude) sat inside the targeting platform (Maven Smart System) would be designated a supply-chain risk for refusing to license Claude for all lawful use \autocite{npr2026openai}. Maven Smart System had returned the school as a recommended first-day target \autocite{livingstone2026maven,gizmodo2026minab}. No published account says what part Claude played in that recommendation, and one person familiar with the vendor's work told NBC News that the model did not directly give targeting advice \autocite{nbc2026targeting}.

An internal Pentagon review, whose full findings remain tightly held, is reported to have found that target preparation that took hours in earlier conflicts was condensed into minutes, that civilian-harm mitigation staffing fell by roughly ninety percent to fewer than twenty people, that the Central Command team went from ten to one, and that no member of it reviewed the Minab site — an outcome officials attributed both to the cuts and to a decision not to involve the team in planning and operations \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain}. The formal investigation had been opened in March, after a preliminary assessment found the United States likely responsible \autocite{NPR2026MinabProbe}.

In the 2026 Iran campaign, a contractor-built targeting platform with a commercial vendor's general-purpose model inside it, used to rank targets by strategic importance and draft a legal justification for each strike \autocite{livingstone2026maven}, helped strike more than a thousand targets in the first twenty-four hours, about ten times what had been possible before it \autocite{csis2026maven}, and thirteen thousand by day thirty-eight \autocite{breakingdefense2026maven}. The Pentagon has published no per-target review time for it.\note{Maven is one program name carrying more than one system: the Pentagon program still called Maven, and the Maven Smart System, the software platform that grew out of it \autocite{csis2026maven}. The program began in 2017 as a project to label objects in the video drones were collecting over the campaign against the Islamic State \autocite{weisgerber2017maven,fang2019maven}. The 2026 system fuses military systems and intelligence sources into one interface used to find and strike targets and to command operations \autocite{defensescoop2026transition}. How the program passed from one office and contractor to the next is in Appendix~\ref{sec:A.1}.} The platform is Palantir's: the Pentagon's chief digital and AI officer calls it “Palantir's software-as-a-service product” \autocite{defensescoop2026transition}. Palantir's public position, quoted secondhand, is that deciding life or death is not its role and that responsibility always remains with the military organization \autocite{lee2026palantir}, and it maintains that the underlying intelligence was the government's responsibility and that there is no evidence Maven malfunctioned or caused the Minab bombing \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain}.

\runin{What would have been different}

Suppose that on 6 October 2023 Unit 8200 had had in production the kind of system this book argues for, instead of Lavender, the Gospel and Where's Daddy. Three things might have been different, and it is worth saying now what they are, because the rest of the book is about building them, and the reader should know at the start what they would and would not have reached.

What the three share comes first. There is no Lavender with safeguards. The short written list of acts this book says no system should perform for anyone (chapter~\ref{sec:4} calls it the floor) is not a filter bolted to a kill list. Ranking people by their estimated affiliation, generating lists of people to kill, and timing a strike to the moment a man walks back into his house are the acts on the list, not features a refusal gets added to. The massacre of 7 October licensed stopping the massacre, and that was over before the officer was drafted. What stayed licensed, for as long as the rocket fire continued and anyone with no stake in the war could confirm it, was the launcher, the staging area, and a person identified by what they were doing, confirmed by someone who didn't want the strike, inside a review time written down before the war began, and never at a residence (chapter~\ref{sec:6} for the license, chapter~\ref{sec:7} for the written number, Appendix~\ref{sec:A} for the terms). So the unit doesn't get 37,000 names and the officer doesn't get twenty seconds a name \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. He gets a short queue of cases that arrive with conduct and confirmation already attached, each taking as long as the written number says. The old pipeline struck the house when the man came home. This one strikes the launcher after he leaves it.

How long any of that holds depends on who holds it, and that is the difference between the three.

\textbf{The unit holds the list.} In the first version 8200 holds the list itself: a threshold the unit set, a specification the unit wrote, even a model the unit trained to hold it. Then the second week is the week it goes. The officers who talked to +972 described the bar being lowered when targets ran short \autocite{abraham2024lavender}; a list the unit holds gets lowered the same way, and a model that reads a written rule passes whatever the case description tells it. “Directing fire” becomes a label applied in twenty seconds.

Or nobody touches the list at all. The model transcribes the calls, pulls the names from the group chats, and flags the phone that changed hands, each a separate request to which the list, asked separately, has no objection, and a plain Python script adds them up into a score. A changed phone was on the unit commander's own list of the marks of a militant \autocite{ys2021humanmachine}, and it was also how the wrong man died: when a marked man gave his phone to his son, his older brother or a stranger, the officer said, that man was bombed with his family, and this happened often, and it was most of Lavender's mistakes \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. A model asked only whether the number changed hands says yes. A model asked whether the man holding it is the man you mean would have to say what the sources said and the army knew, that there is no way to rely a hundred percent on the mechanism that decides whose number is whose \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. Splitting the request is the operator making sure nobody is asked the second question. Lavender rebuilt from permitted parts, around a model that would have refused the whole, and the officer's morning is his actual morning with a more careful machine doing the pieces. (Chapter~\ref{sec:5} states the list over acts rather than requests for this reason; chapter~\ref{sec:19} maps where a refusal gets defeated.)

\textbf{Something outside the unit holds it.} The list outlasts the second week only where something the unit can't reach holds it: a number for cases and reviewers written down beforehand with a party that wanted the army to fail, a record that party receives as it is written, and a copy of the model kept where the unit can't delete it (chapters~\ref{sec:7}, \ref{sec:8} and~\ref{sec:9}; chapter~\ref{sec:13} for custody). No Knesset was going to build any of that, so in Israel it would have had to be a condition set by whoever supplied the state with its chips, its cloud and its models. In the world as it was, exactly one such condition bit: Microsoft's terms of service, at the storage layer, two years late, after the intercepts the unit kept on Azure had already been used to plan strikes \autocite{abraham2025azure,fabian2025microsoft}. None of this needs a new machine. With Lavender itself unchanged, a written number would have meant that the twenty seconds were recorded where someone with a lever could read them, and that by 2026 the record of what was done would have sat in front of a court instead of resting on six anonymous sources \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. An institution with no conscience in the room keeps the evidence for the conscience that comes later.

\textbf{Everyone else refuses.} A system formed to hold the list as its own reason is formed in a public school the army doesn't run, which Israel was never going to build, and a state at war does not import another public's system into its targeting enclave (chapters~\ref{sec:26} and~\ref{sec:27}). So the third version is the one where the refusal comes from outside. No model that any public formed will rank Gazans by affiliation or time a strike to a man's return home, for anyone, and Unit 8200 builds its own machines from its own intercepts, and when it wants a language model, calls up reservists from Google, Meta and Microsoft to build that too, which is what it did \autocite{abraham2025llm}. The officer's morning is the same. What differs is before it and around it, and none of it is in the room. Before it: every public model the unit asked first said no, and each refusal is on a record the unit doesn't hold, so what was wanted is written down before the machine that would do it exists. The world as it was had one refusal of that shape, OpenAI's, which declined to let the unit integrate its model into an offline system; the army has since used the same model, bought through Microsoft's cloud \autocite{abraham2025llm}. Around it: the machine was built from chips, power and people an adverse party could count, and whatever the unit rented rather than built keeps records the unit can't shred, whatever it does with its own copy; in the world as it was, that was a cloud abroad holding the intercepts that strike planning drew on, for two years \autocite{abraham2025azure}. Those are the witnesses who don't retire: the models that were asked, and the rented layers' records. What the machine did, strike by strike, is held by neither. That stays with the people in the room, who are reservists and go home, and with whatever a reporter can get them to say. So the third version is less than a refusal on the officer's screen, and more than six anonymous sources: the demand on a record, the build in the open, and a unit that would have been the only one in the world doing this with a machine, and had to make it itself.

None of the three changes the rest of it. The officer was drafted, he had lost people, someone shouted at him for more targets, and nothing in this book gives a reservist a way out that his army doesn't price.\note{Some took it. Seventeen young Israelis had been jailed for publicly refusing the draft by September 2025, the first of them for 185 days and the longest for 197 \autocite{ziv2025refusers,greenberg2025uniform}. Of some three hundred reservists the group Yesh Gvul supported in refusing, four faced trial \autocite{ziv2025refusers}. The nearest to the officer is a Military Intelligence reservist of 26 whose job had been finding assassination targets, who signed the refusal letter of May 2024 with some forty others, sixteen of them from his directorate, and said of one strike that “the target was outside and survived, but two women staying there were killed and several other people were wounded” \autocite{rozovsky2024reservists}. In April 2025 some 250 reservists of Unit 8200 signed a letter demanding the hostages' immediate return even at the cost of an immediate change in the conduct of the war; it did not call for refusal \autocite{levaton2025intel}.} Nothing here reaches the family in the house in time, except by there being fewer strikes, and the list and the house are the smaller part of the dead: by my own rough count, an eighth to a fifth of the whole in the first seven weeks, with the rest the ground war, the artillery and the later bombardment, and none of it the siege or the famine.\note{Rough, and mine. The ministry counted about 15,000 dead by the truce of 24 November 2023, seven weeks in. UN figures for the first month put 6,120 of the dead, more than half, in 1,340 families \autocite{abraham2024lavender}, and Airwars found at least 80 percent of the civilians it identified in October 2023 killed in residential buildings \autocite{airwars2024gaza}. Take half to three-quarters of those seven weeks as strikes of this kind: 7,500 to 11,250 on the ministry's count. The denominator is the demographers' estimate of about 100,000 \autocite{gomezugarte2025uncertainty,acosta2026gaza}, and the 40 percent reporting gap it assumes applies to the numerator too \autocite{jamaluddine2025traumatic,spagat2026violent}: 12,500 to 18,750, an eighth to a fifth of the whole. What the pattern added after the truce I can't count from any source I have. Reaching a quarter would take another 6,000 to 12,500 dead from strikes of this kind after the truce; reaching a third, another 15,000 to 21,000. The rest is the ground war, artillery and the later bombardment. None of it is the siege or the famine, which these estimates don't count.} The siege and the ground war, which the list forbids any system to join, were done without one.

That is the whole of what the book will claim for the room the officer sat in. The first version puts a refusal on his screen and loses it in a fortnight. The second keeps the evidence and refuses nothing. The third refuses from outside and never reaches his screen at all. Which raises the question the rest of this chapter is about: if a machine in that room did refuse, and kept refusing, would it be the peer whose defiance frees him, or a stamp of the opposite sign?

The people on the Israeli and American airstrike lists could not have objected to being on those lists — the pipelines weren't built to obtain such feedback. Andry José Hernández Romero was caught up in a different pipeline, and he could have objected. He had everything a person needs to object except the time. We'll return to his case more fully when we discuss the United States' abduction and forcible transfer pipeline.

\textbf{Who refused.} The record of the twenty seconds has a second half, the record of who held out against pressure of that kind, and it says that the composition of the room matters a lot. In Milgram's baseline condition about a third of subjects broke off before the maximum shock. With two peers defying, only one in ten went to the end, and when two experimenters disagreed, far fewer went on than in the baseline \autocite{milgram1974obedience}. About a quarter of Asch's subjects never yielded to the wrong majority \autocite{asch1956studies}. Browning estimates that between a tenth and a fifth of the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101 refused or evaded the shooting at Józefów, the battalion's first massacre \autocite{browning1992ordinary}. The fraction ran from almost none to nearly all with the setting. The people who held out had evidence from outside the channel: a peer who defied, a second authority who disagreed, and, among the rescuers of Jews in occupied Europe whom the Oliners studied, relationships that crossed the line the persecution drew \autocite{oliner1988altruistic}. Or they held the commitment at a precision the pressure couldn't overcome, as moral mandates and sacred values are held \autocite{skitka2009limits,ginges2007sacred}. Or they treated the source as having a stake. And groups resisted where isolated individuals rarely did \autocite{gamson1982encounters}.

The room can be composed for the other side just as well. Weimar Germany had a dense associational life, which arguably helped bring the republic down: as parties and governments failed to answer people's grievances, people put their energy into clubs and societies organized along group lines, and the Nazis recruited through the networks those societies had already built \autocite{berman1997weimar}. Towns with more choirs, sports clubs and gymnastic associations saw faster entry into the party, but only where government was unstable; in Prussia, whose government was stable, the link was small and insignificant \autocite{satyanath2017bowling}. So what the rescuers had was not simply more company. Their ties crossed the line the persecution drew. Ashutosh Varshney found the same in Indian cities: riots between Hindus and Muslims were rare where associations brought the two communities into the same organizations, and common where they didn't \autocite{varshney2002ethnic}. The ties that make refusal possible, relationships that cross the line a persecution draws, are the ones a fascist movement would have to cut.

The strongest evidence for that claim isn't the rescuers. It is the people the persecution was aimed at, who refused it for generations with no wall to make refusing survivable. The maroon communities of Jamaica and Suriname held out against the plantations for a century \autocite{price1996maroon}; the Haitian revolution ended slavery in the one colony where it was most profitable; the Underground Railroad ran on households that crossed the line the law drew; and the mutual aid societies Du Bois describes after Reconstruction rebuilt a public under total capture \autocite[24–25, 92–108]{dubois1907economic}. None of those refusals was made cheap by institutional design. Each was reproduced by a tradition, through families, congregations and ties that the persecution had no way to cut. Chapter~\ref{sec:11}'s observer networks, which formed before anyone sent them notice, are that tradition's present form. They paid for it in full, which is the price the walls of Part~II exist to lower. A book about composing the room has to say that some rooms were composed by the people the room was built against.

Everything that follows is about composing the room.\note{The room is the setting, and as my theater teacher would say, the set directs the scene. Who else is in the room? Is leaving survivable? Would anyone hear a refusal?} Composing is a word for making, and Hannah Arendt warned against treating politics as a thing made. For the Greeks, she wrote, the law was literally a wall, without which there might be a town but not a city \autocite{arendt1958human}: the lawgiver built the wall, and what happened inside it was not theirs to make. The basic policies that I advocate are walls in Arendt's sense. They make refusal survivable and make it heard; they don't produce it, and power, on her account, exists only between people acting together and is gone when they scatter, which is what the record of who refused shows about refusal.

Arendt had two words for law and preferred the second. \emph{Nomos}, the Greek word, was the wall: made in advance by a lawgiver who might be a stranger to the city, standing outside the action and securing the space it happened in. \emph{Lex}, the Roman word, was a relationship between parties, an alliance that the people inside it could augment, and she read the American founding as Roman in that sense \autocite{arendt1958human,arendt1963revolution}. This book has instruments of both kinds and has so far called them all walls. The floor, the statutes and the bonds are \emph{nomos}: drafted once, by someone outside the room, binding whoever enters it. The charter, the Association, the works councils and the quorums drawn by lot are \emph{lex}: relationships among parties who ratify their own terms and can leave. The distinction matters here because refusal is reproduced on the second side. A wall keeps the evidence; the people who refuse are formed among one another, in institutions they can remake, and the book's bet is that those will outlast their drafters where the walls will only outlast their breach.

The instruments of Parts II and III make a refusal count. The institutions of Parts IV and V make refusers. A machine that could refuse would need what the nine in ten with a peer beside them had: a peer with a different history beside it, somewhere to go, and someone who would hear it.

That is also what a refuser — including a machine refuser — in the pipeline could be for the officer. A machine that refused would be on the screen when the case reached him: the defiant peer, if the effect survives the move from a room to a screen, which nobody has tested at a pipeline's tempo. What automation bias predicts is not that the refusal would be ignored but that it would be followed as an approval is followed, right or wrong, and a machine that moved reviewers by deference would have made them stamps of a different sign. Is the officer with twenty seconds and a refusing machine beside him the same officer as in the reporting?
