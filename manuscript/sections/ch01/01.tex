An intelligence officer, describing his part in a targeting pipeline built
around machine-generated kill lists, gave a reporter the number this book
returns to. He spent twenty seconds on each name. What follows is the
reporting the number comes from, and then what the number measures.

\begin{esbox}
\boxtitle{AI targeting in the Israeli campaign in Gaza, as reported 2023–2026}
Investigative reporting in +972 Magazine and Local Call, drawing on interviews with serving and former intelligence officers, described three systems working together. Later accounts in the New York Review of Books and the Washington Post covered the same systems. How far their sourcing is independent of those interviews is not established here, so what follows rests on one body of reporting rather than on several.

Habsora — “The Gospel” — generated infrastructure and building targets. Sources told reporters that the majority of its suggestions were private residences.

Lavender scored individuals by estimated likelihood of militant affiliation and produced kill lists. The sources put the number marked at as many as 37,000, and described the outputs as understood inside the unit to be wrong about ten percent of the time — that is, roughly one in ten people it named was not a member of the armed group it was supposed to identify. Both figures come from those sources and have not been confirmed by any party with access to the system.

A third system tracked when flagged individuals returned home, so a strike could be timed to when they were with their families.

What matters is the human review these systems were paired with. One officer described the work: “I would invest twenty seconds for each target at this stage, and do dozens of them every day.” And then, without being asked: “I had zero added value as a human, apart from being a stamp of approval.” \autocite{abraham2024lavender}

The Israeli military disputed the account. Its response to +972 and Local Call denied using artificial intelligence to incriminate targets, describing the systems as “auxiliary tools that assist officers in the process of incrimination,” and stated that “in any case, an independent examination by an [intelligence] analyst is required, which verifies that the identified targets are legitimate targets for attack, in accordance with the conditions set forth in IDF directives and international law” \autocite{abraham2024lavender}. The denial goes to what the systems do and not to whether they exist. Nothing in this section's argument requires the response to be wrong. What carries the section is the officer's own description of what he contributed, which the response does not address.

\end{esbox}
Twenty seconds is what “a human makes the final call” measures out to under operational tempo. It is the whole of the safeguard, and I would ask any reader who finds the safeguards in this chapter reassuring to hold that number against them.

The American analog runs faster. During the 2026 Iran campaign a targeting
platform built by a defense contractor, with a commercial vendor's
general-purpose model inside it, was credited with thirteen thousand targets
in thirty-eight days, more than a thousand of them in the first twenty-four
hours, at roughly ten times the previous rate
\autocite{breakingdefense2026maven,csis2026maven}. No per-target review time
has been published for it. Chapter~\ref{sec:6} returns to both deployments
with the institutions around them. What they supply here is a shape.

Ask what anything standing between that pipeline and a person could have
done for the person.

A floor can do four things for the person it protects, and they need to be apart before anything else in this book. It can \emph{prevent} the act: the refusal occurs and reaches the person. It can \emph{arrest} it: the act is underway and is stopped before completion. It can \emph{detect} it: the act completed and left a record somebody outside the deployment can read. It can \emph{remedy} it: the party the act fell on gets disclosure, a finding and compensation. Only the first is what the word promises. Most of what follows is about the positions at which a deployment converts prevention into one of the other three, or into none of them, and about what it costs to make that conversion visible.

One term reaches the case as it happened, and it asks nothing of anything
inside the model. The officer was not overruled and did not refuse; the
pipeline removed the interval in which either could have occurred. A
deployment can be required to publish the ratio between the rate at which
its system generates candidates and the people available to examine them,
together with the review time per case that ratio implies. The number binds
the operator, it can be checked by a party outside the deployment without
inspecting an output, and it is the only thing in this book that reaches a
targeting pipeline. It is developed in section~\ref{sec:3.6} and measured
in section~\ref{sec:6.4.1}. Everything else here is about what it does not
reach.
