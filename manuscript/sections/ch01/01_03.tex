\section{Mindset and Motivation in AI Behavior}
\label{sec:1.3}
Three ideas borrowed from how humans develop recur across this book's design chapters: the stance a system takes toward its own failures, what drives it to act absent an external reward, and what it takes to build any of this with people from diverse disciplines.

\runin{Growth Mindset}
Psychologist Carol Dweck's distinction between a fixed mindset, which treats ability as static, and a growth mindset, which treats it as built through effort and experience, matters for an AI system's moral judgment as much as for a person's: a system that treats its own errors as data has room to keep improving; one built to defend its prior outputs does not. Section~\ref{sec:5.1.1} works out what that disposition would need to look like architecturally, and what it would cost to get wrong.

\runin{Intrinsic Motivation and Autonomy}
A moral sense that only activates under supervision is not much of one. Section~\ref{sec:5.6} covers what it would mean for a system to act well for reasons other than an externally specified reward — the engineering analog of intrinsic motivation — and what happens to a system's autonomy once it has something like that drive: independence from moment-to-moment human correction, and a corresponding question about what keeps it aligned absent human correction.

\runin{Interdisciplinary Collaboration}
A machine-learning lab, a psychology department, and an ethics program rarely share a budget, a dataset, or a deadline; building AI this way means changing that default, not hoping the fields converge on their own. Section~\ref{sec:8.1} covers what that collaboration requires — funding structured to force it, venues that make it a byproduct of doing the work — with the institutions already doing it, and chapter~\ref{sec:10} the international partnerships that let it hold across borders.
