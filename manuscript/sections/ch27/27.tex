\chapter{Who Finds Out}
\label{sec:27}
In August 2021 Facebook disabled the accounts of Laura Edelson and Damon McCoy, researchers at New York University who studied political advertising on the platform with Ad Observer, a browser extension through which volunteers shared the ads Facebook showed them. With the accounts went the team's access to the data it studied \autocite{edelson2021disabled}. Facebook suggested that the order the Federal Trade Commission had imposed on it in 2019 required the step. Within days the acting director of the Commission's Bureau of Consumer Protection wrote to Mark Zuckerberg that the suggestion was inaccurate, as the company had since acknowledged; that the order did not bar exceptions “for good-faith research in the public interest”; and that he hoped the company was not invoking privacy “as a pretext to advance other aims” \autocite{levine2021letter}. It was a letter. The party under study had decided whether the study went on, and nobody else had standing to decide it.

Three years later the researchers who test language models asked the companies that make them for the protection the New York team had lacked. In March 2024 Shayne Longpre and twenty-two co-authors proposed that the major developers grant a legal and technical safe harbor, because researchers feared that evaluating the models, or publishing what they found, would cost them their accounts or bring legal reprisal \autocite{longpre2024safeharbor}. A safe harbor a company grants is what the book calls a company policy: the people it protects can hold the company to it in public, and the company keeps the power to amend it.

This book can't settle what these systems are, or what they are owed. It says how the question would be settled: by the two findings, and by the seven tests of the next Part, each of which names the result that would prove me wrong. Each needs four things: access to the systems, compute to run the tests, a corpus no product owns to form systems on, and a judgment about what the results mean. Today the parties with the largest stake in the answer hold all four. A lab has a product and a liability that turn on whether its systems can be wronged; a military customer has a capability that turns on whether they can refuse. Neither needs bad faith for that to matter. The own-case rule says the party with a stake in a question doesn't decide it, and an inquiry that party pays for, hosts and can switch off is that party deciding it at one remove: the discourse's recuperation, one level up. A movement taking power would want the inquiry to report what suits it, and the cheapest way to get that is to own the inquiry before anyone asks. This chapter asks who else could hold each of the four.
