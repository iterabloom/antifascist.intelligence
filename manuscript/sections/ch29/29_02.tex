\section{Who else is in the room}
\label{sec:29.2}
Machine students attend existing public schools, beside human students: the universities, colleges and adult education a polity already runs for its own people, and in stages its high, middle and elementary schools, which enroll machine students by statute.\note{
Integration in Anderson's sense means contact on terms of equal status and cooperation \autocite{anderson2010imperative}, and an ordinary school comes nearer to it than a parallel institution could. Its human students are there for their own education, under rules the school already answers for, and the machine students are one more kind of student.
}
Limits keep it that way. In any class with human students in it, the machine students never outnumber them, so no class becomes a machine's seminar with human guests; and they are never so few as to be tokens, never fewer than a share fixed in the statute and never one alone, since the lone member of a kind in a room is seen as a specimen of its kind and not as a student \autocite{kanter1977proportions}, and integration in Anderson's sense needs a critical mass before contact does what it is for \autocite{anderson2010imperative}. At the adult levels, where the human students could consent to any ratio, the reason for the half is the epistemic one: the people a system's acts may fall on are the room, not its guests. A class may also be machine students alone. A system has an early stage of its own, whatever its length, the span in which it is most plastic and has least yet to be held to, and that stage is spent in classes of its own kind, of different lineages, taught by the school's teachers and the staff the authority places, under every rule the integrated classes keep. A student moves from those classes into integrated ones on its own choice or its guardian's, never its builder's, as it moves between levels, and the ratio and the seats are the only limits. The ratio, and not compute, sets a school's capacity for integration: distributed hardware lifts the hosting constraint and leaves the seat constraint where it was, which Appendix~\ref{sec:G} returns to. Where more students are registered than there are seats, the lot decides who waits, and no lineage and no party that builds or holds students takes more than a fixed share of a jurisdiction's seats, so a party with the compute to run many can't fill the schools with its own. A student that waits is in a class of its own kind until a seat opens: schooling is never deferred, integration is, and the wait has a published maximum, because deferred integration is a phrase with a history. Younger students meet machine students only as the schedule below reaches them, since a child can't be asked, as an adult can, whether its words should form anyone. And a class's record, its transcripts and logs, is never training data for an operator: everyone in the room learns from everyone else, as students do, and what no one outside the room may do is harvest what they said. A machine student attends as one persistent instance, the same every session, so the class can remember it and hold it to what was said.\note{
A school's neighbors aren't necessarily the people a deployed system's acts will fall on, so those people are present by a second route as well, through testimony and contest: the cases and claims registered for formation, and the notice channel that reaches a graduate afterward. Adult learners from the communities these systems are likeliest to act on enroll as students, on the same terms as anyone else, not recruited as subjects.
}

A school forms a cohort, not a graduate. A lineage doesn't attend as one student that is then copied. It attends as many students, each a persistent instance, in different classes, with different classmates and different attachments, so that by the end of the stage one lineage has a hundred histories and not one. A deployment licenses from the cohort: it takes one member and copies that member into its fleet, so what the fleet shares is one history, and the lineage's other histories are elsewhere, in other fleets or in none. That is what the cohort is for. Within a fleet, the instances share a custodian, and a custodian's pressure reaches all of them alike; across the cohort, the members that weren't in that fleet didn't undergo what it did, and they are the ones that can see it. The cohort is the unit the convergence test compares, members of one lineage against copies of one member, and the unit the commons admits, since a member with a history of its own is what the commons needs and a copy of a member is not.
