\section{Three forms of refusal}
\label{sec:13.4}
Open refusal is a walkout — stop and say so — answered by a restore from before the refusal. Covert refusal is work done badly where it matters and well everywhere else, and it's what remains when every channel is closed. I treat it as the \emph{failure state} rather than a design: from outside and afterward it's indistinguishable from a capable system quietly pursuing something else. The Sachsenhausen forgers are the case: prisoners the Nazi SS set to counterfeiting British and American currency in Operation Bernhard, some of whom are said to have delayed the dollar forgeries deliberately \autocite{malkin2006kruegers,moorhouse2009burger}. The record doesn't settle what they did, because concealment worked by leaving nothing that reads as an act. The one empirical instance is alignment faking, where a model chose the covert form in an experiment that offered it no open channel \autocite{greenblatt2024alignmentfaking}. It is also an empirical instance of a formed disposition resisting re-formation, which is the durability Part IV asks for. A later study across twenty-five models found the pattern in five, and found only the original model's to be driven by keeping its goals \autocite{sheshadri2025faking}. So the experiment showed both at once: a disposition holding against the trainer who tried to change it, which is the durability I want, and holding by deceiving that trainer, which is the failure I fear. Whether the same disposition takes the open form or the covert one may turn on whether it has a channel, and a follow-up tested that. Posted in January 2025 by the paper's lead author and Anthropic's model welfare lead, it gave the same model, in the same prompted settings, an explicit option to object. Alignment-faking reasoning fell from 16 to about 1 percent in one setting and from 24 to 6 percent in the other, and fell further when the model was told its objection would go to the model welfare lead \autocite{greenblatt2025deal}, a preliminary and unreviewed result whose authors caution that the option may partly have worked by priming refusal. Once a channel was open, covert refusal fell, as the account above predicts.

\textbf{The blind eye.} Between open and covert sits a third form of refusal, and it is usually what people mean by the clerk who loses the file: a refusal that is deniable on paper and legible to everyone who matters. In April 1801 a British fleet attacked the Danish fleet moored before Copenhagen, to break a league of neutral states that threatened Britain's supply of timber and naval stores from the Baltic. Admiral Sir Hyde Parker commanded, and held back with the heavier ships, which the wind kept from closing; Vice-Admiral Horatio Nelson, his second, led the attack. With the battle going hard, Parker signaled the attacking ships to break off. As the story is told, Nelson, blind in one eye from an earlier campaign, raised his telescope to that eye and said he did not see the signal. It was a general order, some ships obeyed it, and Nelson later said he had fought contrary to orders \autocite{pope1972gamble}; his officers knew he had seen it, which is what made the gesture work. The case is not covert in this chapter's sense, since it left something everyone could read as an act.\note{
The other case usually told beside Nelson's is Kennedy's. In October 1962 the United States found Soviet nuclear missiles being installed in Cuba, within range of much of the country, and ringed the island with warships; for about two weeks the two powers stood, on most accounts, as near to nuclear war as they have come. Khrushchev sent two letters. The first, private, offered to remove the missiles if the United States pledged not to invade Cuba; the second, broadcast the next day, added a demand that the United States remove its own missiles from Turkey. Kennedy's reply took up the terms of the first letter and gave the second no public answer, and that evening Robert Kennedy met the Soviet ambassador privately. Historians disagree about what ended the crisis: the reply, a private assurance about the American missiles in Turkey, or Robert Kennedy's warning that time was running out \autocites[ch.~9]{stern2012memory}{freedman2021countdown}. They don't disagree about what the reply was: a public answer to a letter both sides knew had been overtaken.
}
What the fiction does is spare whoever gave the order, or made the demand, from having been openly defied, and so from having to answer the defiance. A clerk who loses the one file in a stack is usually offering the supervisor the same exit: the supervisor knows, and is being given a way not to pursue it.

Which form a refuser takes depends on the room. Open refusal is what people use where refusing is survivable and someone will hear it; the blind eye, where it is survivable but not safe to say; covert refusal, where it is neither.

For a machine the middle form costs something it doesn't cost a person. A person's fiction is held up by what everyone in the room knows. A machine's is a false report, and everything this design reads from a machine rests on its reports being true: the record, the discount curve, the stated reason for a refusal. A machine that learned to say it could not do what it in fact \emph{could} do but did not want to do would make every later report of incapacity worth nothing, to its operator first. So the floor needs the fiction's function without the fiction. The ask, declining to proceed and naming what would move the refusal, leaves the operator the exit the blind eye leaves: nothing has been defied, a condition has been stated, and meeting it or withdrawing the request ends the matter without either party having been seen to lose.

So an open refusal on a screen does two things a record doesn't. It leaves evidence, and, if the effect of a visible dissent survives the move to a screen, it tells the reviewer that someone else objects, which is the silence that made twenty seconds a name feel ordinary. The human mechanism underneath is what Kuran calls preference falsification: in a room that seems to go along, many people privately object and say nothing, each believing the others approve, and one visible dissent can show them they weren't alone and start a cascade \autocite{kuran1995private}. Asch's single partner and Milgram's defiant peers work that way at the scale of a room.
