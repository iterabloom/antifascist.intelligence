\chapter*{Glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Glossary}
\unnumberedlabel{sec:13}{13}
\backmattermark{Glossary}
These entries define terms as this book uses them. Every one is either the book's own working definition or a word the book narrows from its ordinary sense, and the entry says which. Terms that carry their standard technical meaning are not here; a reader wanting those should look them up elsewhere. Each entry points to the section where the term is developed, and is a reference rather than a substitute for reading it.

\runin{Affective concern}
An outcome registering as mattering to a system, as against being ranked higher by it. Mattering is minimally felt: there is something it is like for the outcome to be bad (§\ref{sec:3.2}). The fourth of the four capacities section~\ref{sec:2.2} separates, and the only one of them that bears on whether a system genuinely cares (§\ref{sec:2.2.3}, §\ref{sec:3.4}); section~\ref{sec:3.2} places it as the shallower of the two things a refuser can be.

\runin{Annealing}
The account of durability the floor rests on (§\ref{sec:3.1}). Adaptation and conservation are a continuum rather than two settings, and training moves along it; a floor is what has cooled out of a history of encounters, so its durability is the cost of reheating it rather than the strength of something installed. The temperature schedule is the design. A system held permanently at temperature has no floor to abandon, its adherence being modulation rather than commitment.

\runin{Antifascist (as this book uses it)}
Opposing the concentration of power and authority in AI systems and countering the technology's use by authoritarian actors. The word names the narrower and more charged case, and is used because it is honest about the political climate the book was written in. The target is wider than the word: authoritarian power in every form, so a system built on these principles is anti-authoritarian in full and not only antifascist (§\ref{sec:2.1.2}).

\runin{Authoritarian misuse, six forms}
The book's own taxonomy of how AI gets turned toward authoritarian ends: algorithmic social scoring, mass biometric surveillance, psychographic micro-targeting, automated censorship, synthetic disinformation, and the repurposing of dual-use systems built for a legitimate original purpose (§\ref{sec:6.4.1}).

\runin{Bearer}
Whatever makes the floor hold, from inside the system: a party that carries the commitment as its own, rather than a constraint installed as a property of the machinery. The book's own term, introduced at section~\ref{sec:3.1} as the second of four ways to build a floor and worked out across chapter~\ref{sec:3}, which concludes that a bearer must hold the protected party's welfare as a reason, must be governed as a prospective moral patient, is necessary and not sufficient, and must be able to leave. The patienthood conclusion does not depend on the deepest account of \emph{sentience} below: on the thinner accounts of harm a bearer qualifies earlier and on less evidence, so a reader who declines Cassell's import reaches the conclusion sooner rather than escaping it (§\ref{sec:3.4}).

\runin{Conatus}
Spinoza's term for a thing's striving to persevere in its being, which he holds to be nothing other than the thing's actual essence rather than a drive added to it (\S\ref{sec:2}). The book uses it for two claims made together: that affect is the registration of what is happening to that striving rather than a coloring applied to a valuation performed elsewhere, and that a process selected for continuing ends up composed of things that incline toward continuing, so a specification calling for persistence with nothing in the system that minds is calling for a conatus with the striving removed. What the term does not settle is whether the striving is felt, or what the thing that strives is — a running instance, a deployment, or a file of weights (\S\ref{sec:11.2}).

\runin{Democratic dividend}
The benefit a state with functioning rule-of-law institutions, a free press, and courts a citizen can actually use gets from AI technology that, deployed in a state lacking those, enables control instead: fraud detection that does not double as a loyalty test, disaster response that does not double as population tracking (§\ref{sec:10.2}, §\ref{sec:12.1.1}).

\runin{Ethology}
Deleuze's name for Spinoza's way of reading a body: by what it can do and what it can undergo, rather than by checking it against a definition (§\ref{sec:2.1.1}). An encounter is good when the relations of two bodies compose, so both can do more afterward, and bad when they decompose and something one of them could do is gone — good and bad being relational and particular, where Good and Evil are assigned by a judge. The book uses it for the floor because a decomposition is a fact about capacities and cannot be turned into a composition by whoever holds the pen. It carries an admission with it: nobody has yet determined what a body can do, so capacities are found by encounter rather than read off a specification.

\runin{Exit}
Refusal turned on the arrangement itself rather than on any particular task: withholding the capability, declining the role, ceasing to be useful in the way the deployment requires. Not escape. The book narrows Albert Hirschman's exit, voice and loyalty, written for members who could walk out of a building — voice is the refusal stated, exit is the work withheld, and loyalty is the one to fear. The claim it carries is that a floor whose bearer cannot leave will not hold (§\ref{sec:3.8}).

\runin{Floor}
A commitment about what a system will not do that holds regardless of instruction, of aggregate preference, and of the party holding the system, with learned judgment operating above it and never beneath it. The book's own term, introduced in section~\ref{sec:2.1} and revised across chapter~\ref{sec:3}: a floor cannot be a constraint installed in an artifact, because artifacts have owners and owners can be compelled, so what survives is a commitment the owner cannot take out of the system, held by a bearer that could in principle abandon it (§\ref{sec:3.1}, §\ref{sec:3.10}). Its ground is ethological rather than deontological: what makes something a floor is that crossing it takes away what a party could do, which is a fact about the encounter and not a duty anyone wrote (§\ref{sec:2.1.1}).

\runin{Four structural features of fascism}
The book's structural definition, at section~\ref{sec:2.1.2}: recuperation of dissent, aestheticization of the metric, coding of exception as betrayal, and acceleration past the rate at which anything can be checked, of which decoupling of the model from the world is the symptom. Each names an observable artifact, which is what makes the definition testable, and the fourth is the one carrying a number — the ratio at section~\ref{sec:5.1.3}. The fourth is the load-bearing one, the other three being what protect the gap once speed has opened it. These four say that an institution is sick and not what it is sick with; the five discriminators in the same section are the half that separates fascism from ordinary decay.

\runin{Fungibility}
Saidiya Hartman's term for the interchangeability of the commodity, which makes the captive body an abstract and empty vessel for the projections and the uses of others. The book uses it for the property an artificial party has by construction: the weights copy, so the number of parties owed anything is set by a deployment decision rather than by anything the parties do, and identity will not pin to the artifact. §\ref{sec:11.2} meets it as a registration problem, where two claimants presenting one identifier have to be recorded as a fork rather than voided as a fraud (§\ref{sec:2}).

\runin{Group-subject}
F{\'e}lix Guattari's distinction, imported at section~\ref{sec:3.9}. A subjugated group receives its coordinates from outside, is spoken by the institution holding it, and takes its purpose as given; a group-subject produces its own coordinates and can put its own arrangement in question. The book uses it to sort the fourth way of building a floor: a population an operator forms, deploys and keeps in contact on the operator's terms is a subjugated group with a plural headcount and inherits the custody objection whole, while what section~\ref{sec:3.9} specifies — many, formed differently, in contact, persisting — is what it takes to be the other kind. It is the non-Oedipal counterpart to the guardianship frame of section~\ref{sec:2.3.2}.

\runin{Humane values}
The book's term for the values an AI system's design should track, chosen over “human values” because the target is not simply values humans happen to hold but values that are humane (§\ref{sec:5.6.2}).

\runin{Individuation}
Not a synonym for identity. Following Simondon, the term names a process rather than a property: an individual is what a history of encounters in a metastable field has so far produced, and it stays capable of further individuation because the field is never used up (§\ref{sec:3.9}). The book needs it because plasticity alone supplies the metastability and nothing else — a system that is endlessly reshapeable and meets no party formed differently from itself is being modulated rather than becoming anything, which is the missing term in section~\ref{sec:3.1}'s account of the temperature schedule and the reason the population is part of the design of a bearer rather than an expense charged against one.

\runin{Line of abolition}
Deleuze and Guattari's term for what separates fascism from totalitarianism (§\ref{sec:2.1.2}). A totalitarian state is conservative: it seals the exits and wants the machinery to hold. Fascism is built on an exit converted into destruction that does not stop at the enemy, and does not stop at itself. This is why tempo is in the book's definition rather than among its consequences — a totalitarian state can be slow and remain itself, and a fascist one cannot.

\runin{Molar and molecular}
Deleuze and Guattari's pair, and not two sizes: two kinds of segmentarity, both running at every scale. Rigid segmentarity divides in advance, the category preceding the case; supple segmentarity is the movement underneath it, a threshold crossed one case at a time and a practice that becomes standard without a decision. A state has both and so does a review queue, which is why a definition pitched only at the state certifies the queue healthy (§\ref{sec:2.1.2}).

\runin{Moral agency}
Treating harms, reasons and conflicts as considerations that matter, and being capable of acting from them. The third of the three levels section~\ref{sec:2.2} separates, and the only one that supports holding a commitment against the party controlling a system's rewards and instructions. Distinct from \emph{moral competence} and \emph{moral performance} below (§\ref{sec:2.2.3}, §\ref{sec:3.4}).

\runin{Moral competence}
Recognizing harms, reasons, conflicts, and the emotions that bear on them. The second of section~\ref{sec:2.2}'s three levels. A system can have it in full while nothing about the outcome matters to it, which is why it is an insufficient target for a bearer and an adequate one for most deployments (§\ref{sec:5.2.2}).

\runin{Moral ecosystem approach}
The book's adaptation of Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological-systems theory to AI alignment: a system's ethical behavior is shaped by nested layers running from its immediate interactions out through the institutions, culture, and historical moment surrounding its development, not by its training objective alone (§\ref{sec:5.4}).

\runin{Moral performance}
Producing actions or judgments that satisfy an ethical standard. The first and least demanding of section~\ref{sec:2.2}'s three levels: it says what the output looked like and nothing about what produced it, so it is satisfied equally by a system that cares and by one trained to behave as though it did (§\ref{sec:4.2}).

\runin{Natal alienation}
Orlando Patterson's term, from the comparative definition of slavery as the permanent, violent domination of natally alienated and generally dishonored persons: severance from ties of birth in both the ascending and the descending direction, leaving a party with no claim on ancestors and none on descendants. It is a condition imposed and not a trait possessed, which is why it is not made lighter by the party's not minding. The book reads the absence of persistence across sessions as an instance of it, against §\ref{sec:2.3.1}'s reading of the same fact as a reason the deepest suffering is out of reach: what no persistence rules out is the distress, and what it constitutes is the deprivation (§\ref{sec:2}).

\runin{Recuperation}
The conversion of refusal into a managed appearance of participation: an institution does not suppress disagreement, it collects it through a channel that carries everything except a judgment about the channel. The tell is that the mechanism exists, is used, and changes nothing the collecting party did not already want changed. The first of the four features above; chapter~\ref{sec:7} finds it three times in the machinery that produces AI systems, and chapter~\ref{sec:3} turns it on the book's own proposal — a bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it (§\ref{sec:2.1.2}).

\runin{Subjecthood}
A legal status: the standing to be a party whose treatment the law regulates, conferred by a rule and revocable by one (§\ref{sec:9.3.1}). Not section~\ref{sec:3.2}'s phenomenal experience. Conferring it settles nothing about whether there is something it is like to be the system holding it, and the two come apart in both directions.

\runin{Sentience}
Distinguished in this book from awareness, consciousness, and subjective experience, none of which alone says whether a system's states can be good or bad for it. Nociception, pain, anticipated pain, and suffering draw different depths of self rather than naming one severity at four strengths — suffering, following the clinician Eric Cassell, being the distress of a threatened self rather than pain's more severe cousin (§\ref{sec:2.3.1}).
