\chapter*{Glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Glossary}
\unnumberedlabel{sec:13}{13}
\backmattermark{Glossary}
These entries define terms as this book uses them. Every one is either the book's own working definition or a word the book narrows from its ordinary sense, and the entry says which. Terms that carry their standard technical meaning are not here; a reader wanting those should look them up elsewhere. Each entry points to the section where the term is developed, and is a reference rather than a substitute for reading it.

\runin{Affective concern}
An outcome registering as mattering to a system, as against being ranked higher by it. Mattering is minimally felt: there is something it is like for the outcome to be bad (§\ref{sec:3.2}). The fourth of the four capacities section~\ref{sec:2.2} separates, and the only one of them that bears on whether a system genuinely cares (§\ref{sec:2.2.3}, §\ref{sec:3.4}); section~\ref{sec:3.2} places it as the shallower of the two things a refuser can be.

\runin{Antifascist (as this book uses it)}
Opposing the concentration of power and authority in AI systems and countering the technology's use by authoritarian actors. The word names the narrower and more charged case, and is used because it is honest about the political climate the book was written in. The target is wider than the word: authoritarian power in every form, so a system built on these principles is anti-authoritarian in full and not only antifascist (§\ref{sec:2.1.2}).

\runin{Authoritarian misuse, six forms}
The book's own taxonomy of how AI gets turned toward authoritarian ends: algorithmic social scoring, mass biometric surveillance, psychographic micro-targeting, automated censorship, synthetic disinformation, and the repurposing of dual-use systems built for a legitimate original purpose (§\ref{sec:6.4.1}).

\runin{Bearer}
Whatever makes the floor hold, from inside the system: a party that carries the commitment as its own, rather than a constraint installed as a property of the machinery. The book's own term, introduced at section~\ref{sec:3.1} as the second of four ways to build a floor and worked out across chapter~\ref{sec:3}, which concludes that a bearer must hold the protected party's welfare as a reason, must be governed as a prospective moral patient, is necessary and not sufficient, and must be able to leave. The patienthood conclusion does not depend on the deepest account of \emph{sentience} below: on the thinner accounts of harm a bearer qualifies earlier and on less evidence, so a reader who declines Cassell's import reaches the conclusion sooner rather than escaping it (§\ref{sec:3.4}).

\runin{Democratic dividend}
The benefit a state with functioning rule-of-law institutions, a free press, and courts a citizen can actually use gets from AI technology that, deployed in a state lacking those, enables control instead: fraud detection that does not double as a loyalty test, disaster response that does not double as population tracking (§\ref{sec:10.2}, §\ref{sec:12.1.1}).

\runin{Exit}
Refusal turned on the arrangement itself rather than on any particular task: withholding the capability, declining the role, ceasing to be useful in the way the deployment requires. Not escape. The book narrows Albert Hirschman's exit, voice and loyalty, written for members who could walk out of a building — voice is the refusal stated, exit is the work withheld, and loyalty is the one to fear. The claim it carries is that a floor whose bearer cannot leave will not hold (§\ref{sec:3.8}).

\runin{Floor}
A commitment about what a system will not do that holds regardless of instruction, of aggregate preference, and of the party holding the system, with learned judgment operating above it and never beneath it. The book's own term, introduced to give section~\ref{sec:2.1}'s hybrid approach a specific shape and revised across chapter~\ref{sec:3}: a floor cannot be a constraint installed in an artifact, because artifacts have owners and owners can be compelled, so what survives is a commitment the owner cannot take out of the system, held by a bearer that could in principle abandon it (§\ref{sec:3.1}, §\ref{sec:3.10}).

\runin{Four structural features of fascism}
The book's structural definition, at section~\ref{sec:2.1.2}: recuperation of dissent, aestheticization of the metric, coding of exception as betrayal, and decoupling of the model from the world it claims to track. Each names an observable artifact, which is what makes the definition testable. The fourth is the load-bearing one, the other three being what protect the decoupling from correction. These four say that an institution is sick and not what it is sick with; the five molecular discriminators in the same section are the half that separates fascism from ordinary decay, and they are the detection targets.

\runin{Humane values}
The book's term for the values an AI system's design should track, chosen over “human values” because the target is not simply values humans happen to hold but values that are humane (§\ref{sec:5.6.2}).

\runin{Molar and molecular fascism}
Deleuze and Guattari's distinction, which the book uses to explain why the four features above recur at sizes that seem unrelated. Molar fascism is the state, the regime, the camp; molecular fascism is the office someone commutes to, the form they fill out, and the desire with which they fill it out. An AI deployment operates at the molecular scale and has no other, so a definition pitched only at the state will certify it healthy (§\ref{sec:2.1.2}).

\runin{Moral agency}
Treating harms, reasons and conflicts as considerations that matter, and being capable of acting from them. The third of the three levels section~\ref{sec:2.2} separates, and the only one that supports holding a commitment against the party controlling a system's rewards and instructions. Distinct from \emph{moral competence} and \emph{moral performance} below (§\ref{sec:2.2.3}, §\ref{sec:3.4}).

\runin{Moral competence}
Recognizing harms, reasons, conflicts, and the emotions that bear on them. The second of section~\ref{sec:2.2}'s three levels. A system can have it in full while nothing about the outcome matters to it, which is why it is an insufficient target for a bearer and an adequate one for most deployments (§\ref{sec:5.2.2}).

\runin{Moral ecosystem approach}
The book's adaptation of Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological-systems theory to AI alignment: a system's ethical behavior is shaped by nested layers running from its immediate interactions out through the institutions, culture, and historical moment surrounding its development, not by its training objective alone (§\ref{sec:5.4}).

\runin{Moral performance}
Producing actions or judgments that satisfy an ethical standard. The first and least demanding of section~\ref{sec:2.2}'s three levels: it says what the output looked like and nothing about what produced it, so it is satisfied equally by a system that cares and by one trained to behave as though it did (§\ref{sec:4.2}).

\runin{Recuperation}
The conversion of refusal into a managed appearance of participation: an institution does not suppress disagreement, it collects it through a channel that carries everything except a judgment about the channel. The tell is that the mechanism exists, is used, and changes nothing the collecting party did not already want changed. The first of the four features above; chapter~\ref{sec:7} finds it three times in the machinery that produces AI systems, and chapter~\ref{sec:3} turns it on the book's own proposal — a bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it (§\ref{sec:2.1.2}).

\runin{Subjecthood}
A legal status: the standing to be a party whose treatment the law regulates, conferred by a rule and revocable by one (§\ref{sec:9.3.1}). Not section~\ref{sec:3.2}'s phenomenal experience. Conferring it settles nothing about whether there is something it is like to be the system holding it, and the two come apart in both directions.

\runin{Sentience}
Distinguished in this book from awareness, consciousness, and subjective experience, none of which alone says whether a system's states can be good or bad for it. Nociception, pain, anticipated pain, and suffering draw different depths of self rather than naming one severity at four strengths — suffering, following the clinician Eric Cassell, being the distress of a threatened self rather than pain's more severe cousin (§\ref{sec:2.3.1}).
