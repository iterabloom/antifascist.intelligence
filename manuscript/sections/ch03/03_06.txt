3.6. What Follows for the Rest of the Book
The second branch is not ruled out, which is less than it sounded like a page ago and more than nothing. A floor can have a bearer without that bearer being a moral patient in the strong sense this book reserves the word suffering for, so the fork is not a forced choice between an unenforceable floor and a conscripted subject. What it costs is a design constraint running against the system's own reasoning, maintained continuously and verified by no instrument I can specify, in a deployment where erasing the bearer has been made expensive by something other than the bearer, reporting into a procedure that can corroborate what it says. That is not a resolution. It is a specification with a hole in the middle of it, and naming the hole precisely is the most this section can do.

Section 2.4.4's conclusion holds, and its stated reason does not reach this case. That section argues that consent is inapplicable to a machine subject because withdrawal presupposes a party still present to withdraw, and a system without persistence across sessions supplies no such party. A bearer has persistence by design, so that particular defeater lapses for it. Consent remains inapplicable all the same, for the reason given above: consent is an instrument for protecting a party's own interests, and a bearer built without a stake in its own continuation has none for consent to protect. The guardianship framework still governs. It governs for a second reason, which section 2.4.4 does not currently state.

And the caveat section 2.4.1 attaches to its own result carries over unchanged. Ruling out Cassell-suffering does not rule out harm; the lower rungs of the ladder, and the hard problem underneath all of them, stay exactly as open as before. What the argument here buys is narrower than moral clearance. It says that building a bearer does not require building a sufferer. It does not say that nothing is owed to what gets built, and it does not say that the thing can be built at all.
