3.2.3. Supervised Learning for Ethical Decision-Making
Supervised learning trains an AI system on a labeled dataset — input-output pairs it learns to generalize from — so that it can classify new cases by example. Applied to ethical decision-making, the same method can train an AI system across domains to identify actions and decisions as ethical or unethical, based on examples it has already studied.

Supervised learning can teach an AI system to analyze textual data — ethical guidelines, legal documents, case studies — extracting moral principles and standards it can then apply to problems it has not seen before. In medical ethics, an AI system could learn from a dataset of ethical cases, recognizing factors like patient autonomy, nonmaleficence, beneficence, and justice, and classifying medical decisions accordingly. Given a dataset of human rights situations analyzed by experts, an AI system can learn the same way to distinguish ethical from unethical actions.

The same approach extends to image and video: an AI system can learn to recognize and evaluate ethically relevant content in visual media, a capability that matters directly in autonomous vehicles, where ethical decisions can affect safety. A system trained to reliably identify pedestrians, cyclists, and other vulnerable road users is better equipped to make sound decisions in abrupt braking situations. MIT's Moral Machine project, which collected human judgments on moral dilemmas involving autonomous vehicles, is one source of that kind of training data [[cite:C0307]].

Supervised learning can also train an AI system to make ethical decisions in collaborative or competitive multi-agent settings — multiplayer games, resource allocation — while weighing the interests of other agents. A resource-distribution system, for instance, can be trained to divide resources fairly and efficiently, accounting for fairness, equity, and overall welfare.

Supervised learning has limits, though.

<<list>>
- Quality and representativeness of training data: the method's success depends on how representative the training data is, and bias baked into that data can lead an AI system to make ethically bad decisions.
- Ethical ambiguity and disagreement: dilemmas where principles conflict, or where the ethical boundary is unclear, undermine training — and cultural, individual, and expert disagreement about ethics compounds the problem.
- Overfitting and generalization: an AI system trained this way can overfit to its training data and perform poorly on situations it has not seen; generalizing ethical principles to new cases is the harder requirement.
- Moral rigidity: supervised learning can produce a system that is too rigid or dogmatic in its ethical decisions. Feedback mechanisms — reinforcement learning, a human in the loop — can build in more flexibility.
<</list>>
