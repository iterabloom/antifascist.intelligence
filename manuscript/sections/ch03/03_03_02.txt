3.3.2. Encouraging Curiosity and Creativity in AI Systems for Anti-Authoritarian Applications
Curiosity and creativity for anti-authoritarian applications start with intrinsic motivation. An AI system that seeks novel experiences and explores for the satisfaction of exploring — not only to complete an assigned task — develops skills and knowledge harder for an authoritarian actor to fully specify or constrain in advance. Section 3.2.6 works through intrinsic motivation and curiosity-driven learning as a general mechanism for balancing exploration against exploitation; here the same mechanism serves a specific end.

A curiosity-driven learning mechanism rewards a system for discovering new information on its own rather than only for completing a fixed task. Work in this vein — curiosity-driven exploration through self-supervised prediction, where an internal model's own prediction error becomes the reward signal for novelty [[cite:C0308]] — lets a system generalize to situations its training did not anticipate.

Training environments for play and exploration work best when they are interactive and diverse within safe, controlled boundaries — letting a system learn alongside human users, AI peers, or simulated characters, with rich feedback and multi-modal interaction driving experiential learning.

Anti-authoritarian design also needs practice at cooperative problem-solving: working through social problems from more than one viewpoint is what builds an appreciation for diversity and pluralism in a democratic society.

The moral side of play — how playful interaction refines ethical judgment and builds empathy — is section 4.7's territory. What curiosity and exploration contribute here is the raw material: a system has to be driven to seek out new situations in the first place before there is anything for that later moral practice to work on.

AI systems can also be given examples of resistance to authoritarian regimes and of democratic values holding under pressure, as material for exploration — building creativity and problem-solving skills that carry over directly to anti-authoritarian design.

Making curiosity-driven learning practical at scale means testing it for transfer: whether a system trained to be curious in one domain carries that disposition into a new one. Benchmarking against human exploratory behavior across domains — healthcare, education, environmental protection, human rights work — is one way to check whether curiosity and creative problem-solving generalize, rather than staying an artifact of one training environment.
