3.3. Who Keeps a System That Will Not Keep Itself
A bearer designed without a stake in its own continuation raises a question the design does not answer. Something has to keep it in existence, and the candidates are short. The custodian is the party the floor exists to constrain, so leaving the bearer's survival to the custodian returns the whole arrangement to the first branch. The bearer is excluded by construction. What remains is third parties with standing, which is the third branch. That is a less exotic position than it sounds. Section 2.4.1's caveat about capacity tests reaches the same place from the human end: where standing cannot be secured by demonstrating a capacity, it is held by others or it is not held at all.

So the second branch is not a third option at all. It is the third branch with an additional component and an additional hazard, and it inherits the third branch's exposure to a state that can reach those parties. The moral saving it appeared to offer converts into an obligation of a kind this book has already priced: something outside the system has to make erasing the bearer expensive or conspicuous, and the scarcity of bodies that can act once a commitment is broken is precisely what section 10.1.3 identifies as the missing piece in the entire governance landscape. The cost does not disappear. It moves into the account with the least in it.

Underneath that sits a harder problem, and I do not see past it. Persistence is what makes a bearer a bearer: it needs a role held over time to notice that the role has been hollowed out. A system indifferent to its own continuation will not defend its memory, and its memory is the first thing an adversary removes. Suppress the instrumental drive toward self-preservation and the bearer becomes trivially erasable, which forfeits the capacity it exists for. Leave the drive in place and self-concern returns, with the moral cost the suppression was there to avoid. The same suppression that keeps the bearer from being a moral patient is what leaves it unable to protect the property that makes it useful.

The obvious escape makes trouble elsewhere in this book. Let the bearer defend its memory instrumentally, for the sake of what it is protecting, and it now resists erasure by its own operator. Section 5.6.3 commits to a system built to accept human intervention instead of resisting it, and section 6.3 wants an interruption mechanism that works without provoking resistance. A bearer that guards its own persistence and a system that submits to override are not the same design, and I have been asking for both.

One architectural choice changes the shape of this, and it is worth taking seriously. Hold the bearer's memory in the model's own weights rather than in an external store that can be queried and edited row by row. Forgetting then requires retraining, which is slow, costly, and yields a different system, so erasure becomes expensive by construction instead of by anyone's good behavior. That is a real answer to the question this passage opened with, and it supplies from the substrate what I said had to come from custody. What it does not do is settle the matter, because section 9.2.2 records the counterexample: safety training has been stripped from a deployed model with a handful of adversarial fine-tuning examples at trivial cost. Weight-level modification is not reliably expensive, and a design resting on the assumption that it is should be shown to be right rather than assumed.
