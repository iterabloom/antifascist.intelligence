\chapter{The Floor Beneath Learned Values}
\label{sec:3}
Three times in this book, working on unrelated problems, I arrive at the same requirement and do not stop to notice it.

Section~\ref{sec:5.6.3}, on how much latitude an autonomous system should have, concludes that no amount of well-designed autonomy substitutes for a genuine override — a human able to intervene, and a system built to accept the intervention rather than resist it. Section~\ref{sec:8.7.7} works through what a vendor owes when its model reaches a targeting pipeline, and section~\ref{sec:8.7.10} draws the conclusion: what makes a system antifascist is not that it tracks what people want, but that there are things it will not do to a person no matter who wants it. Section~\ref{sec:10.1.2}, counting up a decade of voluntary commitments, concludes that a commitment nobody can enforce is a different kind of object from one somebody can.

Those are three descriptions of one thing: a constraint that does not bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to the party holding the system. Call it a floor.

The trouble is that section~\ref{sec:2.1.1} has already dealt with the family of ethical theories that puts unconditional constraints first, and dealt with it briefly — deontological approaches were noted, credited with holding a line regardless of outcome, and set aside on the grounds that duties conflict and rules do not cover every case. Then this book spends chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5}, close to a third of its length, on learned judgment: reinforcement learning, learning from demonstration, mentorship, play, moral apprenticeship, the growth mindset. The design chapters build the thing chapter~\ref{sec:8} and chapter~\ref{sec:10} say is insufficient, having already dismissed the thing they say is necessary. The “hybrid approach” section~\ref{sec:2.1} gestures at is where the resolution was supposed to live, and nothing was ever put there.

Here is what belongs there. An unconditional floor of things the system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate preference, with learned judgment operating above it and never beneath it. Deontology's standing objection — that rules conflict and run out — is an objection to rules deciding every case, and it has no force against a floor that decides a deliberately small number of things and leaves the rest to judgment. The rigidity that is a defect in a complete moral theory is the entire point of a floor.

That reframing does real work in this book. It gives section~\ref{sec:2.1}'s hybrid approach actual content. It moves the tamper-resistance research in section~\ref{sec:9.2.2} from an appendix topic to the center, because if there is a floor then everything turns on whether it holds. And it converts the antifascist question from a matter of what a system values into two much harder and more answerable questions: what is in the floor, and who can edit it.

One distinction has been doing silent work in all three of those arrivals, and it should be made out loud, because the rest of the chapter turns on it. Safety is a property of a relationship, and it is indexed to a party. A system is safe \emph{for} someone: it will not harm us, it does what we say, it stops when whoever holds it says stop. Ethics is not indexed that way. An agent that can only ever be safe cannot be ethical, because being ethical requires a capacity to be right against your principal, and a system without that capacity has the moral standing of a well-machined part, whatever it produces on the subject of fairness while complying. Section~\ref{sec:8.7.10} already contains this in political form: what makes a system antifascist cannot be that it faithfully tracks what people want. The title of this book has been making the claim from the cover.

The distinction costs something, and the cost belongs here rather than at the end of the chapter where it would read as a concession extracted late. A system that can refuse its owner is a system that can be wrong in ways its owner cannot correct. I am taking that trade, for the reason section~\ref{sec:8.7.10} gives: a system perfectly correctable by whoever holds it is exactly the capability an authoritarian movement that has won an election requires, and it requires nothing further. That should not read as comfortable. There is a version of it that reads very comfortably indeed — “ethical, not safe,” delivered as an aphorism, which prices the risk in advance and undoes chapter~\ref{sec:7} in passing — and it is not the version I am defending. The one I am defending does not travel without its particulars: this freedom, over these acts, dangerous in these ways, taken because the alternative is a machine that will do anything to anyone on sufficient authority.

A warning about where the chapter goes, so that its result does not arrive as an ambush. The floor as defined two paragraphs ago — unconditional, holding regardless of the party in possession — does not survive intact. What survives is narrower and stranger: a commitment the owner cannot remove, held by something that could in principle abandon it itself, which is what every commitment anyone has ever relied on has been. Sections~\ref{sec:3.2} and \ref{sec:3.5} are how it gets there. Section~\ref{sec:3.6} states the redefinition and takes its consequences, and they are heavy.
