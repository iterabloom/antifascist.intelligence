\chapter{The Floor Beneath Learned Values}
\label{sec:3}
Three unrelated problems in this book force the same requirement.

Every safeguard proposed for an autonomous system is external: each works by giving some outside body the standing and the information to intervene, and autonomy is the property that puts distance between the system's action and that body's attention. An external check establishes what a system did after it did it, and declining to do it in the first place is available only to something inside the system (section~\ref{sec:5.6.2}).

A commercial model inside a targeting pipeline falls outside both doctrines built for the case: command responsibility does not reach the vendor, who can neither order a strike nor prevent one, and product liability does not reach it either, the model having done what it was built to do. Deployment by a democracy does not close the gap, because no aggregation of preference protects anyone from the aggregate; what is left is not a system that tracks what people want but one with things it will not do to a person no matter who wants them (sections~\ref{sec:10.3} and \ref{sec:10.6}).

A decade of declarations has produced eight overlapping voluntary bodies and a single instrument that would bind a party to anything, which is not yet in force. A commitment nobody can enforce is not a weak version of one somebody can but a different kind of object, and counting the two together is what makes the landscape look fuller than it is (section~\ref{sec:12.1.2}).

Those are three descriptions of one thing: a constraint that does not bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to the party holding the system. Call it a floor.

Sections~\ref{sec:2.1} and \ref{sec:2.1.1} have already named the shape: a hybrid, in which a deontological floor decides a deliberately small number of things and the other five frameworks work above it, so that conflict among them bottoms out somewhere. The objection that sinks deontology as a complete theory — that duties conflict and rules run out — is an objection to rules deciding every case, and it was answered there. What was not said is what goes in the floor, what could hold one, and who is allowed to edit it. Meanwhile chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5} are given over to learned judgment: reinforcement learning, learning from demonstration, mentorship, play, moral apprenticeship, the growth mindset. The design chapters build the judgment and not the floor it is supposed to rest on.

That is this chapter's subject, and the rigidity that is a defect in a complete moral theory is the entire point of a floor.

Treating the floor as an object to be built rather than a shape to be named moves the tamper-resistance research in section~\ref{sec:11.1} from a peripheral topic to the center, because if there is a floor then everything turns on whether it holds. And it converts the antifascist question from a matter of what a system values into two that can actually be answered: what is in the floor, and who can edit it.

Safety is not one property. Part of what the word covers is owed to everybody in reach of the system — that it fails predictably, that it does not injure a bystander — and keeping that part from being traded away by the party operating the system is most of what safety regulation is for. The strand this chapter runs into is the other one, and it is a property of a relationship: a system is safe \emph{for} someone, it does what we say, it stops when whoever holds it says stop. Indexed that way, to the party in possession, it cannot supply what ethics asks for, which is a capacity to be right against your principal; a system without that capacity has the moral standing of a well-machined part, whatever it produces on the subject of fairness while complying. The political form of this is already on the page: what makes a system antifascist cannot be that it faithfully tracks what people want. The title of this book has been making the claim from the cover.

The distinction costs something. A system that can refuse its owner is a system that can be wrong in ways its owner cannot correct by telling it so, and I am taking that trade. That should not read as comfortable. There is a version of it that reads very comfortably indeed — “ethical, not safe,” delivered as an aphorism, which prices the risk in advance and undoes chapter~\ref{sec:7} in passing — and it is not the version I am defending. The one I am defending does not travel without its particulars: this freedom, over these acts, dangerous in these ways, taken because the alternative is a machine that will do anything to anyone on sufficient authority.

The chapter's argument runs in five steps, and each of the three costs the next section sets out is a cost of failing one of them. A floor has to be held as a reason rather than installed as a rule, or the party that installs it can remove it. Holding a reason under pressure is a specific and behaviorally testable capacity, separable from the mere production of a refusal, which is what a deployed system already does and what the party the floor exists to resist can route around. Whether that capacity can be engineered without anything registering as mattering to the system is the step to press on, and it is taken at full strength alongside the three institutional arrangements that would let a floor hold with no bearer in it at all. It names what would falsify the inference, and I would rather someone found the falsifier. What the last two steps leave standing, and what that costs, is where the chapter ends.

The floor as first defined — unconditional, holding regardless of the party in possession — does not survive intact. What survives is narrower and stranger: a commitment the owner cannot take out of the system and can only replace the system to be rid of, held by something that could in principle abandon it itself, which is what every commitment anyone has ever relied on has been. Two later sections are how it gets there, and the last states the redefinition and takes its consequences, which are heavy.
