\chapter{The Floor Beneath Learned Values}
\label{sec:3}
Three times in this book, working on unrelated problems, I arrive at the same requirement and do not stop to notice it.

Section~\ref{sec:5.6.2}, on how much latitude an autonomous system should have, concludes that no amount of well-designed autonomy substitutes for a genuine override — a human able to intervene, and a system built to accept the intervention rather than resist it. Section~\ref{sec:10.7} works through what a vendor owes when its model reaches a targeting pipeline, and section~\ref{sec:10.10} draws the conclusion: what makes a system antifascist is not that it tracks what people want, but that there are things it will not do to a person no matter who wants it. Section~\ref{sec:12.1.2}, counting up a decade of voluntary commitments, concludes that a commitment nobody can enforce is a different kind of object from one somebody can.

Those are three descriptions of one thing: a constraint that does not bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to the party holding the system. Call it a floor.

The trouble is that section~\ref{sec:2.1.1} has already dealt with the family of ethical theories that puts unconditional constraints first, and dealt with it briefly — deontological approaches were noted, credited with holding a line regardless of outcome, and set aside on the grounds that duties conflict and rules do not cover every case. Then this book spends chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5}, close to a third of its length, on learned judgment: reinforcement learning, learning from demonstration, mentorship, play, moral apprenticeship, the growth mindset. The design chapters build the thing chapters~\ref{sec:8} through \ref{sec:10} and chapter~\ref{sec:12} say is insufficient, having already dismissed the thing they say is necessary. The “hybrid approach” section~\ref{sec:2.1} gestures at is where the resolution was supposed to live, and nothing was ever put there.

The resolution is an unconditional floor of things the system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate preference, with learned judgment operating above it and never beneath it. Deontology's standing objection — that rules conflict and run out — is an objection to rules deciding every case, and it has no force against a floor that decides a deliberately small number of things and leaves the rest to judgment. The rigidity that is a defect in a complete moral theory is the entire point of a floor.

That reframing does real work in this book. It gives section~\ref{sec:2.1}'s hybrid approach actual content. It moves the tamper-resistance research in section~\ref{sec:11.1} from an appendix topic to the center, because if there is a floor then everything turns on whether it holds. And it converts the antifascist question from a matter of what a system values into two much harder and more answerable questions: what is in the floor, and who can edit it.

One distinction has been doing silent work in all three of those arrivals, and it should be made out loud, because the rest of the chapter turns on it. Safety is not one property. Part of what the word covers is owed to everybody in reach of the system — that it fails predictably, that it does not injure a bystander — and keeping that part from being traded away by the party operating the system is most of what safety regulation is for. The strand this chapter runs into is the other one, and it is a property of a relationship: a system is safe \emph{for} someone, it does what we say, it stops when whoever holds it says stop. That strand is indexed to the party in possession, by construction. Ethics is indexed too, and to somebody else — the party who can be wronged, who is rarely the party holding the switch. An agent that can only ever be safe in that second sense cannot be ethical, because being ethical requires a capacity to be right against your principal, and a system without that capacity has the moral standing of a well-machined part, whatever it produces on the subject of fairness while complying. Section~\ref{sec:10.10} already contains this in political form: what makes a system antifascist cannot be that it faithfully tracks what people want. The title of this book has been making the claim from the cover.

The distinction costs something. A system that can refuse its owner is a system that can be wrong in ways its owner cannot correct. I am taking that trade, for the reason section~\ref{sec:10.10} gives: a system perfectly correctable by whoever holds it is exactly the capability an authoritarian movement that has won an election requires, and it requires nothing further. That should not read as comfortable. There is a version of it that reads very comfortably indeed — “ethical, not safe,” delivered as an aphorism, which prices the risk in advance and undoes chapter~\ref{sec:7} in passing — and it is not the version I am defending. The one I am defending does not travel without its particulars: this freedom, over these acts, dangerous in these ways, taken because the alternative is a machine that will do anything to anyone on sufficient authority.

The chapter's argument runs in five steps, and each of the three costs section~\ref{sec:3.1} sets out is a cost of failing one of them. A floor has to be held as a reason rather than installed as a rule, or the party that installs it can remove it. Holding a reason under pressure is a specific and behaviorally testable capacity, and section~\ref{sec:3.2} separates it from the mere production of a refusal, which is what a deployed system already does and what the party the floor exists to resist can route around. Whether that capacity can be engineered without anything registering as mattering to the system is the step to press on, and section~\ref{sec:3.3} takes it at full strength alongside the three institutional arrangements that would let a floor hold with no bearer in it at all. It names what would falsify the inference, and I would rather someone found the falsifier. What the last two steps leave standing, and what that costs, is section~\ref{sec:3.8}.

The floor as defined two paragraphs ago — unconditional, holding regardless of the party in possession — does not survive intact. What survives is narrower and stranger: a commitment the owner cannot remove, held by something that could in principle abandon it itself, which is what every commitment anyone has ever relied on has been. Sections~\ref{sec:3.4} and \ref{sec:3.7} are how it gets there. Section~\ref{sec:3.8} states the redefinition and takes its consequences, and they are heavy.
