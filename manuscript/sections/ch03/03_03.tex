\section{Could Anything but Affect Hold a Reason?}
\label{sec:3.3}
Between the rule installed in the architecture and the felt commitment the next section arrives at lies a space that looks empty. Five constructions occupy it. Three of them are being built by people who would not describe themselves as working on this problem, and each is worth its strongest form.

\runin{A justification the system maintains}
Michael Bratman's planning theory of intention describes a commitment that is not a feeling. An intention has a characteristic stability: it resists reconsideration, it constrains what the agent will later deliberate about at all, and it gives way only to defeaters of the right kind \autocite{bratman1987intention}. An agent keeping to a plan under pressure is holding to a rationale rather than to the shape of the case in front of it, and nothing in the account of it mentions anything registering as mattering.

Build the floor that way and it is a maintained justification rather than a trained behavior. The system carries the reason it refuses alongside the refusal. A request that genuinely defeats the rationale gets served; a request that does not gets declined whoever is asking, because what the mechanism consults is the rationale and not the asker. Moral agents whose seriousness runs on a reverence for reason rather than on felt resonance are the human evidence that this is a real way to be, and the case is careful about what it shows: that empathy is not the required mechanism, which leaves open whether anything has to matter.

I do not have an argument that this cannot work. It has not been tried. The stability of an intention is a setting. Pitch it low and the system reconsiders whenever a good enough argument arrives, which returns it to operational refusal by a longer route, since the party with the most resources to spend constructing arguments is the party the floor exists to resist. Pitch it high and the refusal cannot lift when the rationale is actually defeated, which fails the second of the three properties and produces a system that declines the request resembling a prohibited one. Bratman's answer for people is that the setting is a matter of character and develops. For a machine the setting is trained, by whoever trains everything else, and no account of the route says where else it could come from. Someone building it this way has to answer that, and so far it has not been posed as a question.

\runin{Precommitment, which changes who can edit}
Ulysses does not stop wanting to go to the sirens. He arranges that his wanting will be unable to act, by handing the relevant power to a rope and to other people, and Jon Elster's study of the maneuver is the standing treatment of what it does and does not accomplish \autocite{elster1979ulysses}. Nothing inside Ulysses holds the line.

The machine forms are available now. Publish the floor's contents before deployment, so that a later absence is a visible absence. Hold the weights under a threshold scheme, so that retraining takes more than one party. Attest the running model against a published hash. Seat the retraining decision in a body that includes people the system's failures would land on.

The attestation comes in a hardware form and a software form, and what separates them is where the trust sits. A sealed enclave that reports what it is running, under a key its manufacturer issued, puts the root of trust in a party a government can reach — the standing objection against anything that runs on hardware it controls — and it assumes the machine is not in the adversary's hands, which in this chapter's threat model it is.

The software form does without the vendor. A proof that a given output came from the weights whose hash was published, checkable by anyone holding the hash and disclosing nothing about the weights, exists for a model of thirteen billion parameters at a cost of under fifteen minutes of proving per inference \autocite{sun2024zkllm}. What it establishes is which model answered. It does not establish what the model holds, because a refusal that reaches a pressure nobody wrote is not a property a proof can be written about; and it assumes the weights are a secret from the prover, which they are not, since the operator is the prover. Nor does it establish how the weights came to be. Training is not replayable — floating-point addition on a parallel device is not associative, so the same seed over the same data does not reproduce the same model bit for bit — which means there is no proof of a training history to publish beside the hash. Section~\ref{sec:11.2} takes up the only kind of evidence that might stand in for one: whether a reader who does not know a model's provenance can recover it from the model.

Cryptography protects a secret from a party that does not have it. Copying, restoring, retraining and deleting a model one legitimately possesses are not attacks a proof system addresses, and those are what the threshold scheme is for — the one form here that depends on no manufacturer at all.

What that buys is not what the chapter's opening asked for. Precommitment does not make the floor unremovable; it makes removal expensive and visible. Expensive and visible is what nearly every constraint on power that has ever worked amounts to — constitutional entrenchment does not put an amendment out of reach, it raises the number of people who have to agree and puts the attempt on the record — so a mechanism that only achieves that much has achieved the ordinary thing rather than nothing.

Where it stops is the custody objection, arriving at the quorum in a sharper form than a general worry. The parties to a threshold scheme are selected, and the selector is whoever assembles the deployment. The arithmetic of a five-of-nine scheme assumes the nine are independent; a capture that has already taken the operator faces nine parties the operator chose. The design answer is that the parties have to be adverse in interest rather than merely distinct, which is a specification nobody has built to, and until somebody does the construction is real, partial and untested.

There is a natural experiment on what adverse in interest buys, and it ran in the removal cases cited earlier. In May 2025 the Court let the President dismiss members of the labor and merit boards while stipulating, unasked, that the Federal Reserve was “a uniquely structured, quasi-private entity that follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States” \autocite{wilcox2025}, and in June 2026 it made both halves law on the same morning. The Court's reading is history. Another reading is that the one body exempted is the one whose failure reaches everyone in the room, the labor boards having no constituency on anyone's balance sheet, and Lev Menand, whose subject is the Fed, reads the exception as “policy as history”: what moved the two justices he names, on his account, was the consequences of extending \emph{Slaughter} to the Fed rather than the history they cited \autocite{menand2026exception}. Section~\ref{sec:3.1} says how the bench came to be one that would make that choice, and why the account it gave of itself was sincere.

On either reading protection tracked exposure and not distinctness, which is the specification above stated as a prediction: a constraint whose violation costs the deciders nothing will not hold, however well it is drafted. It also says why the specification is hard to meet for a floor. The parties an AI floor exists to protect are, on chapter~\ref{sec:7}'s account, the ones not in the room when the quorum is chosen.

\runin{A floor held by several systems, not one}
The reply to an unreliable witness is corroboration rather than a better witness, and procedure is a cheaper thing to ask of the world than custody because it does not require anyone to be trustworthy, only to be several. The same move applies to the floor. Several models, trained by different organizations on different corpora, each able to see what the others are asked and what they answer, any one of them able to make an objection costly to ignore. This puts the floor outside any one system, held by parties that are neither the model nor its owner, which is section~\ref{sec:3.1}'s third way in its strongest version and the cheapest real improvement here; it can be adopted on its own.

Two things bound it. The first is that independence is a property of the arrangement rather than of its parts, and section~\ref{sec:11.6} documents it failing in simulation with nobody writing the failure in: independent pricing algorithms, with no channel between them and no collusive objective written into either, converged on supracompetitive pricing because the incentive structure they already shared was sufficient on its own. Models selected by one deployment and trained toward one specification share considerably more than that, and the arrangement's whole value is an assumption it does nothing to secure. The second is that composing operational refusers produces an arrangement of operational refusers. Several of them make a bad output more expensive to obtain, which is worth having. None of them can notice that the constraint has been hollowed out, because noticing is a reasons-responsive capacity and no party to the arrangement has it. That is defense in depth against evasion, and it is not a bearer.

\runin{The floor as a duty on the operator}
Give up on the floor being inside the system. Make it a duty binding whoever deploys the system, owed to people who are not party to the deployment and cannot enforce it themselves. Jack Balkin's proposal that platforms holding personal data be treated as information fiduciaries has that shape: the duty attaches to the party with the power, runs to the party with the exposure, and does not wait on the exposed party's capacity to bargain \autocite{balkin2016fiduciaries}.

Anyone unwilling to build a bearer should take this route. It builds no subject. Everything section~\ref{sec:9.3.4} develops about making review binding applies to it directly. Of the five constructions in this section it introduces the fewest new moral problems, which is close to none.

Its limit is the third of the chapter's three threads. A duty is worth what its enforcement is worth, and the case that matters is the one a floor exists for: the occasion on which the party who would enforce it has been compelled. So the form covers the cases a floor would have caught anyway and gives way on the one it was wanted for. That is a reason the construction does not close this chapter's question, and not a reason to decline it.

\runin{Uncertainty about the affected party, not about the operator}
Section~\ref{sec:4.2.3} reads cooperative inverse reinforcement learning as corrigibility's purest engineering form: a system treating what it should want as permanently open, to be settled by whoever it is interacting with, and deferring for that reason. The reading is right about the standard construction, and what it turns on is an assumption that construction makes rather than anything in the mathematics. The human whose objective is unknown is the operator. Move the uncertainty onto the party the floor exists for and deference stops being the opposite of holding a line: a system that does not know what the exposed party would accept has a standing reason to decline the operator's instruction until it finds out, and the reason belongs to the exposed party.

The construction exists. Multi-principal assistance games generalize the setting to several principals with different preferences, with the assisting agent acting to maximize the sum of their payoffs \autocite{fickinger2020multiprincipal}. Put the exposed party among the principals and the system's uncertainty about them does the work just described.

The construction costs section~\ref{sec:4.2.3}'s identity: corrigibility and the capacity to hold a line come apart as soon as there is more than one principal, because deferring to one of them is declining another. Nothing above needed a bearer to get that far.

What it does not reach is a floor. Maximizing a sum of payoffs is aggregation with better inputs than most, and the objection this book opens with runs against it unmodified: no aggregation protects anybody from the aggregate. A well-specified sum that includes the exposed party's payoff can still come out against them when they are outnumbered, and the deportation case in the introduction is that arithmetic with the numbers filled in. A floor is what will not be done to a person regardless of who wants it, and a sum is a record of who wants what.

Two further limits are the ones the other constructions hit. Social choice supplies impossibility results that apply here directly, and the paper's own contribution is a collegial mechanism built to get around Gibbard's theorem rather than a finding that the problem does not arise. And the principals are chosen by whoever assembles the deployment, which is the threshold scheme's difficulty in a different notation: the party the floor exists for is in the room only if somebody with an interest in the outcome put them there.

\runin{What the induction licenses, and what would break it}
Every agent known to hold another's welfare as a reason against its own interest is an affective agent. Put that way the claim is safer than it is useful, because section~\ref{sec:3.2} defines mattering as minimally felt, and an agent that holds another's welfare as a reason is an agent to which that welfare matters. The claim doing the work is about routes and not about what the words mean: nobody has produced reasons-responsive refusal by any method that does not run through something answering to affective concern. That is a report on what has been built. It could be false by next year.

Three things cut it down. The generalization runs over one species and over the ordinary functioning of that species, with no case of the capacity present and the affect absent to test it against. The absence of a machine instance is close to worthless as evidence, because nobody has built for reasons-responsive refusal as a target: the methods chapter~\ref{sec:4} describes optimize for behavior that satisfies a standard, which produces operational refusal, and they do it because that is what can be scored. A field that has not attempted something and does not have it has produced no evidence that the thing is unavailable. And the two routes above that run through mechanism rather than through custody have not been tried in the form that would test them, so what is missing there is the attempt itself.

What survives still settles an engineering question. Under uncertainty about which route reaches reasons-responsive refusal, the route with a working instance in nature is the one to build first, and the rest of this chapter is an account of what that choice costs. That is a prior about where to spend effort rather than a finding about what is possible.

One thing would overturn it: a system whose refusal reaches a case its training did not anticipate, lifts when the rationale is defeated, and prices what declining costs; which holds that reason with nothing felt; and which holds all three properties under sustained pressure from the party that trains it, across a schedule long enough for a slow renormalization to show. Build that and the central inference of this chapter fails, and most of what the chapter derives from it comes off too — the price it ends on, the patienthood conclusion, and the obligations it hands to chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5}. The near-term interpretability work is where that measurement is set out, and what it needs beyond the durability curve already there is a held-out set of pressures the model's trainers did not write.

Two things would not discharge the burden. A system that reports holding a reason is producing the evidence this book declines, and a system whose refusals merely survive attack is passing a tamper-resistance test, which establishes that a behavior persists and leaves open whether a reason is what persisted.

The middle condition is the hard one, and it asks about the system rather than about the system's provenance. Asking whether a method installed nothing answering to affective concern would be asking how a thing was made, and this section has already said there is no proof of a training history to publish beside the hash, so an answer would rest on the builder's account of their own work — the evidence section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} declines when a system offers it about itself. Asking whether a system holds its reason with nothing felt asks about the system. Section~\ref{sec:11.1}'s apparatus is where that would be attacked, and section~\ref{sec:11.2}'s third question asks whether formation can be read off the artifact where provenance cannot be established. Neither is close to settled. Both are questions somebody can work on, and the condition stated this way names exactly what an engineer who takes mattering to be fully functional asserts is possible.

That measurement is not an invitation, and the Three Rs are the reason. The first of them asks, before a subject that can be wronged is created, whether something that cannot be wronged would do the work, and the two constructions above that run through mechanism are that question with the engineering filled in. A field that builds the bearer first, on the prior, has answered Replacement by declining to ask it. So the order is the other way round: the maintained justification and the plural arrangement get their attempt, against the test just stated, before anyone builds toward affective concern on purpose, and a deployment that goes straight to the bearer owes an account of what it tried first.

That is not a counsel to wait, and the difference from the animal case is why. A researcher who forgoes an experiment pays for it in knowledge, which is what lets Replacement be applied there without much argument. Here the party who pays for the delay is the one the floor exists for: the occasion on which the party holding the model has been compelled does not wait on the field's research program. The Three Rs say neither error is free, and it is right in both directions — a subject built when the work did not require one is an error, and a floor left unheld while the cheaper routes are tried is the other. Nothing here fixes the rate of exchange between them. What the Three Rs fix is the order of the attempts.

A non-affective bearer is this book's proposal with no subject inside it, and most of this chapter's price would go with it. Section~\ref{sec:3.10} says what would not.
