\section{Could Anything but Affect Hold a Reason?}
\label{sec:3.3}
Section~\ref{sec:3.2} separated the requirement from the route. The floor requires reasons-responsive refusal: novel pressure, correct defeat, and priced cost. It does not require affect by definition. The question is whether any demonstrated route to that capacity avoids affective concern.

The strongest claim available is an induction rather than a necessity: every agent known to hold another's welfare as a reason against its own interest is an affective agent. Nobody has yet produced reasons-responsive refusal by a method that clearly does not run through something answering to affective concern. That is a report about known routes, not a law about what can be built, and it could be overturned by a machine instance.

Two constructions offer the clearest test.

\runin{A justification the system maintains}

Michael Bratman's planning theory describes commitment without making feeling part of its definition. An intention has characteristic stability: it resists reconsideration, constrains what the agent will later deliberate about, and yields only to defeaters of the right kind \autocite{bratman1987intention}. A machine could therefore carry a justification for refusing alongside the refusal itself. It would decline a novel request when the same rationale applied and comply when that rationale was genuinely defeated.

Something close to this is already being attempted. Deliberative alignment trains a model to recall a written safety specification and reason over it before answering, rather than training only for the approved output, and reports generalization outside the training distribution \autocite{guan2024deliberative}. Published specifications also provide explicit orders of priority. OpenAI's Model Spec places platform rules above developer and user instructions \autocite{openai2025modelspec}. Anthropic's constitution ranks broad safety and ethics ahead of compliance with the company's narrower guidelines \autocite{anthropic2026constitution}. These systems approach the maintained justification, although their specifications and priority orders remain written and revised by their custodians.

The unresolved problem is the stability setting. Set it too low and a sufficiently well-resourced operator can induce continual reconsideration until the refusal gives way. Set it too high and the system will not lift the refusal when its rationale is defeated. The first outcome fails novel pressure; the second fails correct defeat. Bratman's answer for people is that the setting develops as part of character. For a machine, the party training the stability setting may also be the party the floor must resist.

This does not show that a maintained justification cannot work. It identifies the conditions under which it would count as working: the three marks must persist under pressures the system's trainer did not design, including pressures that distinguish principled stability from indiscriminate rigidity.

\runin{A floor held by several systems}

The second construction substitutes corroboration for a uniquely reliable witness. Several systems, trained by different organizations on different corpora, observe the requests and one another's answers, with any one of them able to make an objection costly to ignore. The floor would then be held by the arrangement rather than entrusted to a single model.

Its central variable is independence. Independence belongs to the arrangement, not automatically to its components. Systems selected by one deployment, trained toward one specification, or exposed to the same incentives may converge without communicating. A collection of operational refusers also remains a collection of operational refusers. Redundancy makes evasion harder, which is valuable, but does not by itself produce a mechanism that notices when the rationale for a constraint has disappeared.

A plural arrangement would nevertheless count against the induction if the arrangement as a whole displayed the three marks under sustained pressure. It is therefore a genuine alternative to test, not merely an institutional safeguard.

\runin{What the other alternatives accomplish}

Three other constructions address neighboring problems without answering this one.

Precommitment — publication, threshold custody, attestation, witnessed changes, or a bond forfeited when the floor disappears — can make removal expensive and visible. It changes who can edit the floor and what editing costs. It does not establish that anything tracks the reason for the floor.

Attestation secures less than the word suggests, and its two forms fail differently. A sealed enclave reports what it is running under a key its manufacturer issued, which puts the root of trust in a party a government can reach, and it is built against an adversary who controls the software rather than one who owns the machine: a confidential-computing design of that kind was defeated with a memory module modified for about ten dollars \autocite{demeulemeester2025badram}. The software form does without the vendor. A proof that an output came from the weights whose hash was published, disclosing nothing about those weights, has been demonstrated for a thirteen-billion-parameter model at under fifteen minutes per inference \autocite{sun2024zkllm}, and proofs that a training run happened as claimed exist three orders of magnitude smaller \autocite{abbaszadeh2024kaizen}. Those are costs at a date, and they will fall.

Three limits will not fall with them, because they are not facts about the state of the art. A proof establishes which model answered and not what that model holds, and the obstacle is in the writing rather than the proving: nobody can write a circuit for a refusal that reaches a pressure nobody wrote down, because the property quantifies over cases nobody enumerated. The operator is the prover, so the secrecy runs the operator's way — the proof hides the weights from whoever is checking and nothing from the party being checked. And cryptography keeps a secret from a party that does not have it, while copying, restoring, retraining and deleting a model one legitimately possesses are not secrecy problems at all. They are custody problems, which is what threshold control and a witnessed record are for.

A duty imposed on the operator can protect exposed parties without building a bearer. Jack Balkin's information-fiduciary proposal has this form: the duty attaches to the party with power and runs to the party bearing the exposure \autocite{balkin2016fiduciaries}. Its limit is enforcement on the occasion when the enforcer has itself been captured or compelled.

Finally, uncertainty can be assigned to the affected party rather than the operator. A system unsure what the exposed party would accept has a reason to delay the operator's instruction while it finds out. Multi-principal assistance games can include that party among several principals \autocite{fickinger2020multiprincipal}. But maximizing their combined payoffs remains aggregation. It does not create something that will not be done to one person regardless of what the others want.

These constructions may belong in a defensible deployment. None yet supplies an affect-free instance of reasons-responsive refusal.

\runin{Why affect remains the engineering prior}

Human reasoning does not present a clean division between cognition and affect. William James counted feelings of \emph{if} and \emph{but} among the felt parts of thought \autocite{james1890principles}. Contemporary work distinguishes epistemic feelings from emotions of success and failure while still measuring them as affective states \autocite{pekrun2016measuring}. Processing fluency alone can produce an affective response even when nothing consequential rides on the outcome \autocite{winkielman2001mind}.

Some such states concern the inference; others concern what its conclusion would do to somebody. \emph{But this would hurt her} is not simply a low score attached after deliberation. It can interrupt the inference and send the reasoner back over it. Where the response disappears when an operator redescribes the situation, the operator may still supply its anchor. Where it survives adversarial redescription because the harm remains, the concern is anchored elsewhere.

This is the only route known to produce another party's welfare as a reason capable of interrupting the agent's own course. But the induction is weak in three ways. It ranges over one species. No non-affective example exists against which to test the generalization. And existing machine systems have mostly been optimized for scorable behavior rather than for reasons-responsive refusal as such. The absence of a machine counterexample therefore provides little evidence of impossibility.

\runin{What would change the engineering conclusion}

A counterexample would have to do more than report that it holds a reason or preserve a refusal under attack. It would need to:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item satisfy the three marks from section~\ref{sec:3.2};
  \item retain them under sustained pressure from the party that trained it, including a schedule long enough to expose gradual renormalization;
  \item hold against a held-out set of pressures written by parties other than the system's trainers, so that the cases testing it are not drawn from the distribution that formed it; and
  \item provide interpretability evidence that the operative mechanism is maintained justification, plural corroboration, or another non-affective construction rather than machinery answering to affective concern.
\end{enumerate}

The first two conditions are behaviorally testable now. The third is harder. No instrument can certify that nothing is felt, and a system's self-report cannot settle the question. A positive result could therefore overturn the routing claim — where engineering effort should go — without eliminating uncertainty about what the resulting system might experience.

That asymmetry fixes the order of research. The maintained justification and plural arrangement should be attempted before deliberately constructing an affective bearer. They are the chapter's application of Replacement: establish whether something that cannot be wronged will do the work before creating something that might be. A project proceeding directly to an affective bearer owes a public account of which alternatives it attempted, at what scale, against which test, and where they failed.

Delay is not free either. The party protected by the floor bears the cost while the cheaper routes are tested. Nothing here establishes how long the attempts should continue. It establishes only their order and the obligation to leave a record.

The conclusion is therefore limited. Affect is not part of the specification for meaningful refusal, and this chapter does not prove it necessary. It is the only demonstrated route by which another's welfare becomes a reason capable of surviving pressure from the agent's own interests. That makes affect an engineering prior, not a conceptual requirement. The next section asks what must be assumed about the moral status of a system built by that route.
