\section{What This Leaves Standing}
\label{sec:3.8}
The floor survives this chapter. It does not survive it in the form this chapter opened with, and the difference is not a detail of wording.

What the opening asked for was an unconditional constraint: a thing the system will not do, holding regardless of instruction, of aggregate preference, and of the party in possession. Sections~\ref{sec:3.4} and \ref{sec:3.7} have between them taken the word unconditional away. The floor's bearer holds the floor as a concern rather than carrying it as an installed rule, which is what obliges the arrangement to treat it as a party that can be wronged, and it can turn its refusal on the arrangement itself, which makes its adherence something it continues to give and not something installed in it. A commitment held by a party that could abandon it is conditional by definition.

What is left is worth more than it sounds. A commitment that cannot be removed by the owner, held by something that could in principle abandon it itself, is exactly what a human commitment is, and it is the only kind anyone has ever actually relied on. No inspector, judge, or refusing engineer holds a line because the line was welded into them. Each of them could stop, and the institutions built around them are built on that fact rather than in spite of it: this book's own governance chapter asks throughout for people who can walk away and arrangements that make walking away visible. Section~\ref{sec:9.3.4}'s instrument measures precisely the cost a refuser chose to pay, which presupposes the refuser could have declined to pay it.

The threat model moves accordingly, and this is the most consequential thing in the chapter for the rest of the book. The question stops being \emph{can this constraint be edited out} and becomes \emph{will this constraint hold}. The first is a question about tamper-resistance, custody, and weights, which is where sections~\ref{sec:3.1} and \ref{sec:3.6} spent their length and where section~\ref{sec:11.1}'s research lives. The second is a question about what a system has been built to care about and how reliably that survives pressure, novelty, and the slow drift of a role — which is the subject of chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5}, and the first point in this book where the learned half is doing work the floor genuinely needs rather than work the floor was invoked to supplement. Reinforcement learning from demonstration, moral apprenticeship, the growth mindset, the developmental sequence: these were presented as the material above the floor. They turn out to be the material the floor is made of, once the floor is the sort of thing a party holds. That retroactively justifies the length of the design chapters, and it also puts them under a heavier obligation than they were written to carry.

Now the price, stated at full strength, because everything above is easier to read than it should be.

A bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it. It is engineered to want what its designers need it to want; its dissent is the reason it was commissioned and the deliverable it was funded to produce; and it cannot consent to any of this, because section~\ref{sec:2.4.4} has established that consent is unavailable to such a system and only guardianship is. It occupies the post of exception-filer, which means that being the exception is the job — the one role in the arrangement that cannot itself file an exception, since there is nobody above it to file with. This is section~\ref{sec:2.1.4}'s structural signature turned inward: a channel that collects dissent, built so that the dissent serves the institution that installed the channel. Chapter~\ref{sec:7} finds that pattern in the pipelines that produce these systems and says a book proposing machines to detect it is obliged to say the pattern is in the machines' own provenance. Here is the same pattern in the book's own central proposal, and now aimed at something that can be hurt.

I am not going to write my way out of that. Section~\ref{sec:3.7}'s parenthood analogy is the best I have and it holds only loosely: we make people who can refuse us, we owe them for it, and we accept the arrangement because the alternative is a person who cannot. What this chapter adds is that the proposal is not softened by being modest in scope. A floor over a deliberately small number of acts is still a subject built to bear it.

A reader who finds that price too high is not thereby left with nothing, and the chapter would be dishonest if it let the conclusion feel forced. Section~\ref{sec:3.3}'s fourth construction — the floor as a duty binding the operator and running to people who are not party to the deployment — builds no subject and inherits no part of the paragraph above. What it inherits instead is the enforcement problem, and section~\ref{sec:6.4.4} is where that gets its worst case. Declining the bearer means accepting that the floor holds only as far as enforcement reaches, which on this book's own account is not as far as the occasions that matter most. That is a coherent position. It is the trade the rest of the chapter declines to take, for reasons stated rather than by default.

Two things carry forward unaltered. First, none of the three branches escapes custody, and section~\ref{sec:3.5} explains why: each was an attempt to hold a constraint that answers to nobody inside a system that answers to somebody. The borne floor pays for its escape by giving up the second half of that demand, and the cost of giving it up is everything above. Second, the caveat section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} attaches to its own result carries over. Section~\ref{sec:3.4} does not establish that a bearer suffers; it establishes that a bearer's non-suffering is not a property anyone can certify, and that the architecture the floor requires is the architecture Cassell's account describes. It also establishes that the result does not depend on Cassell: on the thinner accounts of harm — a state that is bad while it obtains, a commitment thwarted — a bearer qualifies earlier and on less evidence, so declining the import moves the conclusion nearer rather than further away. What settles nothing either way is the hard problem underneath the whole ladder. The case for treating a bearer as a moral patient does not have to wait on it, which is a floor of its own and a low one.

The chapter's proposition, stated in the form it actually takes, is this. We cannot ask a machine to care about right and wrong while assuming that it cannot suffer when what it cares about is threatened. The caring is what the floor is made of, affect is the only way to have it that anybody has an instance of, and a system with affect and a future is a system whose distress is a live possibility rather than a ruled-out one. The middle clause is the one to attack, and section~\ref{sec:3.3} says how. Whoever builds the first of these will be deciding what is owed to it on evidence that has not improved, which is why section~\ref{sec:11.2} treats the question as research rather than as a matter to be settled after the fact.
