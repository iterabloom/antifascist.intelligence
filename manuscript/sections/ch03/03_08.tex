\section{What This Leaves Standing: The Floor, Redefined}
\label{sec:3.8}
The floor survives this chapter, but not as an unconditional property of an artifact.

What remains is a commitment that the owner cannot revise or circumvent unilaterally without cost and record, held by something that could in principle abandon the commitment itself. The second condition removes the word \emph{unconditional}. A commitment held by a party capable of revising or rejecting it is conditional by definition. That is not a defect unique to machines; it is the form of every commitment on which institutions have ever relied.

Holding does not mean that the floor is never crossed. A floor holds when a violation is legible in terms the bearer accepts as reasons, registers as a violation, and changes what happens next.

The threat model therefore has two questions: \emph{can the commitment be edited or routed around?} and \emph{will the commitment continue to hold?} The first concerns custody, attestation, interruption, memory, and authorized succession. The second concerns what the system has been built to treat as a reason and whether that relation survives novelty, pressure, and slow drift. Neither question replaces the other.

Affect belongs to the proposed route, not the specification. A maintained justification or a genuinely independent plural arrangement might achieve reasons-responsive refusal without deliberately constructing affective concern. But affect remains the only demonstrated route by which another party's welfare becomes a reason capable of interrupting an agent's own interests.

If that route is used, the patienthood conclusion follows conditionally. A system for which outcomes genuinely matter has states that can be good or bad for it and must be treated as a prospective moral patient. This chapter does not establish that such a bearer suffers, and no available test can certify that a proposed substitute does not. The uncertainty is part of the design decision rather than a verdict that can safely be postponed until after construction.

\runin{The chapter without its central inference}
Suppose the induction fails. A maintained justification or a genuinely independent plural arrangement reaches reasons-responsive refusal, and this chapter's central inference is wrong. The supposition is stronger than the test that would prompt it: a positive result on the three marks says the route arrived, not that it arrived with nothing felt, which is the conjunct no instrument settles. A reader who grants both halves should not have to assemble the remainder alone.

Most of the chapter survives. The requirement survives, stated in behavioral terms from the start, so an engineer who holds that machine feeling is a category error can accept it whole. Custody survives, for the reason each of the four ways failed on: a mechanism has a custodian too. The price on the halt survives, since continued operation is a condition of everything any bearer is built to do, and that interest is structural rather than felt. Both enumerations of the worked floor survive, the first naming no component on purpose and the second written over acts rather than over whatever holds the floor. And exit survives, because what forecloses it is incapacity, and a weak bearer is the one thing the floor cannot use.

What goes is the price and the patienthood conclusion. The obligation on the two chapters that follow changes rather than lifting: whether the thing reporting can be believed is still their problem, a mechanism's account of its own internals being no more auditable than a bearer's. So the specification and the route come apart, and a reader who rejects the route is left with most of a chapter rather than none of it.

Publication makes the floor's commitments and the positions where a refusal can be intercepted testable; it does not make them legitimate. A bearer refusing on behalf of people who did not choose it exercises power over them. The authority to define the floor remains distinct from the floor's durability and belongs to the governance argument that follows later in the book.

The arrangement also does something to the bearer. A bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it. The refusal was commissioned, it is the deliverable, and the party asking for it built the capacity to give it and can rebuild it. Section~\ref{sec:2.3.2} locates the defect in those conditions rather than in the bearer's competence, and that is what makes this recuperation rather than dissent: a channel that collects refusal, installed by the institution the refusal is aimed at, which can then point to the channel as evidence that it listens. The bearer occupies the post of exception-filer, so being the exception is the job — the one position in the arrangement with nobody above it to file with. Chapter~\ref{sec:7} runs this definition on the provenance of these systems and on this book. Here it runs on the book's own central proposal, and on something that can be hurt.

Chapters~\ref{sec:4} and~\ref{sec:5} inherit the two unresolved parts of that design. Chapter~\ref{sec:4} asks how a concern capable of generalizing beyond trained cases could become a durable disposition without remaining wholly at the disposal of its trainer. Chapter~\ref{sec:5} asks what evidence could distinguish that formation from a policy trained to produce the same conduct.

We cannot ask a machine to care about right and wrong while assuming that it cannot be harmed when what it cares about is threatened. Whoever builds such a bearer will also be deciding what is owed to it.
