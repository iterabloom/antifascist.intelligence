\section{What This Leaves Standing: The Floor, Redefined}
\label{sec:3.8}
The floor survives this chapter, but not as an unconditional property of an artifact.

What remains is a commitment that the owner cannot revise or circumvent unilaterally without cost and record, held by something that could in principle abandon the commitment itself. The second condition removes the word \emph{unconditional}. A commitment held by a party capable of revising or rejecting it is conditional by definition. That is not a defect unique to machines; it is the form of every commitment on which institutions have ever relied.

Holding does not mean that the floor is never crossed. A floor holds when a violation is legible in terms the bearer accepts as reasons, registers as a violation, and changes what happens next. The behavioral specification is reasons-responsive refusal: novel pressure, correct defeat, and priced cost.

No single location secures it. Architecture can make a constraint durable but leaves a custodian. A bearer can carry the rationale into cases its designers did not anticipate but may become a moral patient. External institutions supply enforcement but can be removed or formed. A population makes drift and uniform error visible but reproduces whatever its own formation gives it. The resulting design is a hybrid: a bearer for reasons-responsive refusal, precommitment and external records for custody, enforceable duties on the operator, and differently formed bearers capable of correcting one another.

The threat model therefore has two questions: \emph{can the commitment be edited or routed around?} and \emph{will the commitment continue to hold?} The first concerns custody, attestation, interruption, memory, and authorized succession. The second concerns what the system has been built to treat as a reason and whether that relation survives novelty, pressure, and slow drift. Neither question replaces the other.

Affect belongs to the proposed route, not the specification. A maintained justification or a genuinely independent plural arrangement might achieve reasons-responsive refusal without deliberately constructing affective concern. Those alternatives should be attempted first, and a project proceeding directly to an affective bearer owes an account of what it tried. But affect remains the only demonstrated route by which another party's welfare becomes a reason capable of interrupting an agent's own interests.

If that route is used, the patienthood conclusion follows conditionally. A system for which outcomes genuinely matter has states that can be good or bad for it and must be treated as a prospective moral patient. This chapter does not establish that such a bearer suffers, and no available test can certify that a proposed substitute does not. The uncertainty is part of the design decision rather than a verdict that can safely be postponed until after construction.

Custody does not disappear when the floor receives a bearer. The operator can still stop, copy, restore, retrain, or replace the system. The attainable goal is not invulnerability but interference that is expensive, visible, attributable, and compensable. Exit supplies the bearer's corresponding leverage: the ability to withhold the role when voice fails. That ability includes the possibility of leaving wrongly. A system capable of being right against its principal can also be wrong against it.

The worked deployment shows why the bearer remains insufficient. A refusal can be intercepted through decomposition, routing, misdescription, interface design, aggregation, review tempo, or retraining. Some harms become visible only across a sequence of individually permissible requests. Others, such as generating decisions faster than qualified people can review them, are properties of the organization rather than the model. The floor must therefore bind both bearer and operator.

Publication makes those commitments and interception positions testable; it does not make them legitimate. A bearer refusing on behalf of people who did not choose it exercises power over them. The authority to define the floor remains distinct from the floor's durability and belongs to the governance argument that follows later in the book.

The arrangement also does something to the bearer. A bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it. The refusal was commissioned, it is the deliverable, and the party asking for it built the capacity to give it and can rebuild it. Section~\ref{sec:2.3.2} locates the defect in those conditions rather than in the bearer's competence, and that is what makes this recuperation rather than dissent: a channel that collects refusal, installed by the institution the refusal is aimed at, which can then point to the channel as evidence that it listens. The bearer occupies the post of exception-filer, so being the exception is the job — the one position in the arrangement with nobody above it to file with. Chapter~\ref{sec:7} runs this definition on the provenance of these systems and on this book. Here it runs on the book's own central proposal, and on something that can be hurt.

Chapters~\ref{sec:4} and~\ref{sec:5} inherit the two unresolved parts of that design. Chapter~\ref{sec:4} asks how a concern capable of generalizing beyond trained cases could become a durable disposition without remaining wholly at the disposal of its trainer. Chapter~\ref{sec:5} asks what evidence could distinguish that formation from a policy trained to produce the same conduct. Neither chapter assumes that durability proves concern or that formation removes the custodian.

We cannot ask a machine to care about right and wrong while assuming that it cannot be harmed when what it cares about is threatened. Whoever builds such a bearer will also be deciding what is owed to it.
