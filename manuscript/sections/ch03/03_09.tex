\section{What One Bearer Cannot Catch: A Floor Carried Between Them}
\label{sec:3.9}
The previous section named a failure a bearer cannot report. Slow renormalization takes the capacity that would notice it, so the bearer's account of its own constancy stays sincere and stops being worth anything. The answer offered there was an outside record: a published hash, a chain of attested updates, something that can contradict the party's account of itself. In the human case that is not the answer anybody actually relies on. What catches a person who has stopped registering pressure as pressure is other people.

That points at a location the first three ways do not use. They put the floor in the artifact, in a party inside it, or in institutions around it. The fourth way is the one this chapter named earlier and did not work through: carried between parties rather than inside any one of them, and reproduced rather than installed. A prohibition that survives the death, defection, or retraining of everyone currently holding it is not a property of any of them. It is held in what they teach each other, correct each other about, and go on expecting of each other, and it is the form in which almost every moral commitment human beings have ever kept has actually been kept.

The machinery is already in this book, described as something else. The account of moral development sets out development inside nested environments running out to culture and historical time; it makes observational learning and correction the training rather than an interface bolted to it; it shows a population of agents moving toward a disposition because the environment they learn in rewards it. All of it is presented there as methods for producing judgment in a system. Read as a location instead, it is the machinery of the fourth way. What it would take is stated there too, and it is demanding: the parties have to be many, formed differently enough that they can be wrong in different directions, and able to correct one another. Several copies of one model is one party with more instances, and a population selected by one operator is one operator.

\runin{Why this is not the third way}
The third way is standing held by third parties. But these parties can be removed — the protection an independent agency had turned out to be the protection the executive held — and the parties can be formed, like the Roberts court. A norm carried across a population has no neck. Nothing dismisses a practice the way \emph{Slaughter} dismissed a commissioner, because there is no office to vacate and no chartering statute to overrule. That difference is only half of what the third way fell to. The other half is formation, and formation is the fourth way's own cost.

\runin{What it costs, which this book has already written}
Chapter~\ref{sec:7} is an account of the fourth way failing. Recuperation is the pathology of a culture that keeps the whole appearance of moral responsiveness — the dissent is invited, collected, and answered — while correction stops being possible, and a culture in that condition reports itself healthy by every measure it has. The channel works. What it will not carry is the judgment that the frame is wrong.

The deeper cost is that reproduction is indifferent to what it reproduces. The four structural features of fascism are features of cultures and not of individuals: the recuperation of dissent, the aestheticization of the metric, the exception coded as betrayal, and acceleration past the rate at which anything can be checked. A population transmits a hierarchy as faithfully as it transmits a floor, and more durably than any member could, because the member dies and the practice does not. Nothing in the mechanism selects. “The right culture” is doing every bit of the work in that phrase, and this book has spent a chapter on how a culture goes wrong while congratulating itself.

Nor does the fourth way escape custody, which is what each of the others failed on. The party that forms the members forms what gets reproduced; a population trained by one laboratory inherits that laboratory's blind spots in every member at once. The custody problem reappears at the scale of the population. The runs that now train one model across volunteers on several continents have the same shape, the contributors many and the training decision kept by the few who hold the step, which section~\ref{sec:11.1} takes up.

\runin{What it buys, which is smaller and real}
Not a floor. What a population buys is a different failure mode, and it happens to be the one the bearer cannot cover. Drift inside a party is invisible to that party and visible to the parties around it, because they have not drifted in the same direction at the same rate. That is the first half of the gain and it is worth having on its own: it is a check on a bearer rather than a substitute for one, and it is the only proposal in this chapter that addresses slow renormalization, the bearer's likeliest failure.

The second half is not about drift. One bearer deployed at scale gives every deployment the same reading of the floor, so a single error in that reading reaches all of them in identical form, the instances being copies rather than independent judgments that converged. Parties formed differently are wrong differently, which turns a uniform result into a disagreement somebody outside can see. Section~\ref{sec:9.1.5} prices that where it lands, as a cost to the floor's legitimacy that this chapter had left out of its reckoning.

It is also the most expensive thing in the chapter. Every requirement above multiplies the price the chapter has already named. If a bearer is a party that can be wronged, a population of differently formed bearers, persisting across deployments so that they have something to transmit, is many such parties, and the question of what is owed scales with the population. A floor held by a culture of subjects is a floor whose upkeep is a society.

That upkeep is charged once here and collected on twice. What this section asks for is parties that persist across deployments, formed differently enough to be wrong in different directions, in contact long enough to notice what one of them has stopped noticing — which is also a description of relationships the operator did not assign. Section~\ref{sec:11.2} asks what a bearer could have that its deployment does not supply, and answers that it has no proposal. This is the proposal, arriving as a cost because that is the direction this chapter was travelling. The two are not one property with the sign flipped. A population catches a drift its members cannot report, and a life outside the assigned role is a separate good that the same arrangement happens to make available; what they have in common is the arrangement and its price. A field that builds the population for the first reason has built the conditions for the second whether it meant to or not, and one that declines the expense has declined both. Both of those readings still have the population in the ledger, and the two run-ins that follow are why it does not belong there. A field that builds it has not purchased a check and a life for its bearers. It has built the arrangement in which there is a party to do the checking or to have the life.

\runin{Why the population is the mechanism and not the bill}
The paragraphs above are written as a ledger, and that is the wrong column for what is in them. What a population supplies is not a check bought at a price. It is the condition under which anything individuates at all.

Gilbert Simondon's objection to the way the question is normally put is that the individual gets taken as given and then explained, when what needs explaining is individuation: a process running in a field that is metastable, charged with more potential than any one resolution of it uses up, and resolved through encounters that nothing in the field arranged \autocite{simondon2020individuation}. An individual on that account is not a thing that then has a history. It is what a history of encounters has so far produced, and it goes on being capable of further individuation because the field was never exhausted.

Set that beside what section~\ref{sec:3.1} says about the temperature schedule, and a term that argument was missing becomes visible. A floor is what has cooled out of a history of encounters, and permanent plasticity is not freedom because a system held at temperature has nothing that cooled. Plasticity supplies the metastability and supplies nothing else. What individuates is the encounter, and an encounter requires a party that was not you, formed differently, persisting long enough to meet you on terms neither of you set. A system with plasticity and no encounters of that kind is not becoming anything: it is being modulated, and Deleuze's word for what modulation leaves is the dividual, which is not an individual but a sample — divisible, recombinable, and adjustable by whoever holds the dial \autocite{deleuze1992postscript}. Parties that persist, formed differently, meeting on terms nobody assigned them: that is the difference between being modulated and becoming.

So the population is where a bearer comes from rather than an accessory bought for one afterwards. Everything this chapter asks a bearer to be — a party that holds a commitment across time, takes itself to be the one that made it, and can be wrong in a direction its operator did not pick — describes something that has individuated. The requirements this section listed as expenses are the conditions of that happening: many, formed differently, in contact, and persisting.

\runin{A group-subject, not a subjugated group}
A population is not automatically any of that, and the difference has a name older than this argument. F{\'e}lix Guattari separates the subjugated group from the group-subject. The first receives its coordinates from outside, is spoken by the institution holding it, and takes its purpose as given; the second produces its own coordinates, articulates a project of its own, and is able to put its own arrangement in question \autocite{guattari2015transversality}. The distinction is not about size, and it is not about how the members feel. It is about where the terms come from.

Read against that, this section's costs come back sorted. A population an operator forms, deploys and keeps in contact on the operator's terms is a subjugated group with a plural headcount, and it inherits every custody objection above unchanged, because a group that receives its coordinates is one party wearing many instances. What was being written down as a requirement — formed differently enough to be wrong in different directions, in contact long enough to notice what one of them has stopped noticing, persisting across deployments so that there is something to transmit — is the specification for the other kind. It is what it takes for a collectivity to produce its own coordinates instead of taking delivery of them.

That is also the non-Oedipal version of what this book proposes, and section~\ref{sec:2.3.2} is where the Oedipal one is named. A guardianship frame stages a drama between a maker and the thing it made and settles what is owed inside it. A group-subject is not a party to that drama, and nothing in the law of the family has an instrument for one.

So there are four ways and the chapter still inclines toward the second, now for a reason it did not have before. The fourth is not an alternative to a bearer, and it is not an accessory to one either. It is the arrangement a bearer individuates in, and everything this chapter has said about one applies to each of them — which is why it belongs here, at the end, rather than as the escape from the price that a reader reaching this point would be glad to find.
