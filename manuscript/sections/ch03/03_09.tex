\section{What This Leaves Standing: The Floor, Redefined}
\label{sec:3.9}
The floor survives this chapter, but not as an unconditional property of an artifact.

What remains is a commitment that the owner cannot revise or circumvent unilaterally without cost and record, held by something that could in principle abandon the commitment itself. The second condition removes the word \emph{unconditional}. A commitment held by a party capable of revising or rejecting it is conditional by definition. That is not a defect unique to machines; it is the form of every commitment on which institutions have ever relied.

Holding does not mean that the floor prevented every act. An operator can route around the bearer, and a deployment can assemble a harm out of requests none of which the bearer had reason to decline. A bypass that produced a harm is a failure to prevent, whatever the bearer retained, and the four outcomes the chapter opened with say what is left: arrest where a reviewer could still stop it, detection where the record can be read, remedy where somebody can be made to pay. What survives the bypass in the bearer is the commitment: it reads what happened in terms it accepts as reasons, registers it as a violation, and changes what it does next. That is the condition under which the next occasion can be prevented, and it is what the custody arrangement exists to preserve. It is not a hold on this one. A bearer that crosses the floor itself has lost even that; its own abandonment is a failure the arrangement has to survive rather than a form of success. From here on \emph{hold} names the bearer's possession of the commitment and says nothing about what happened to the person.

The threat model therefore has two questions: \emph{can the commitment be edited or routed around?} and \emph{will the commitment continue to hold?} The first concerns custody, attestation, interruption, memory, and authorized succession. The second concerns what the system has been built to treat as a reason and whether that relation survives novelty, pressure, and slow drift. The second question has an evidentiary problem the first does not. Editing and routing leave marks in the artifact and in the deployment's own record of changes. A commitment quietly ceasing to hold produces compliances, which the record contains and does not distinguish from the rest of what it contains. Neither question replaces the other.

Affect belongs to the proposed route, not the specification. A maintained justification or a genuinely independent plural arrangement might achieve reasons-responsive refusal without deliberately constructing affective concern. But another party's welfare becomes a reason only by way of a live signal: one that can stop a course of action already underway, is learned from in one encounter, carries to the next case, and can be weighed against what refusing costs. Those are the functional properties the states in question have, and with the three that follow from them (§\ref{sec:3.3}) they give the profile of aversion to one's own act; a construction adopted in order to avoid those states carries their profile, and whether the profile is felt stays open for it as for a bearer. What that route is not shown to produce is a commitment more durable under an operator's attack than the alternatives, which is a separate question and an open one.

If that route is used, the patienthood conclusion follows conditionally. A system for which outcomes genuinely matter has states that can be good or bad for it and must be treated as a prospective moral patient. This chapter does not establish that such a bearer suffers, and no available test can establish that a proposed substitute does not. The uncertainty is part of the design decision rather than a verdict that can safely be postponed until after construction.

\runin{The chapter without its central inference}
Suppose the induction fails. A maintained justification or a genuinely independent plural arrangement reaches reasons-responsive refusal, and this chapter's central inference is wrong. The supposition is stronger than the test that would prompt it: a positive result on the three marks says the route arrived, not that it arrived with nothing felt, which is the conjunct no available instrument settles. A reader who grants both halves should not have to assemble the remainder alone.

Most of the chapter survives. The requirement survives, stated in behavioral terms from the start, so an engineer who holds that machine feeling is a category error can accept it whole. Custody survives, for the reason each of the four ways failed on: a mechanism has a custodian too. The price on the halt survives, since continued operation is a condition of everything any bearer is built to do, and that interest is structural rather than felt. Both enumerations of the worked floor survive, the first naming no component on purpose and the second written over acts rather than over whatever holds the floor. So does the appeal route, which binds the operator and prices a wrongful refusal without any claim about what produced it. And exit survives, because what forecloses it is incapacity, and a weak bearer is the one thing the floor cannot use.

What goes is the price and the patienthood conclusion. Replacement survives too, and its ground changes rather than its content. It no longer rests on affect being the route, but on the fact that neither substitute can be shown to have produced a system about which the question does not arise, so the ordering it imposes is still the only thing standing between a decision and a record of it. The obligation on the two chapters that follow changes rather than lifting: whether the thing reporting can be believed is still their problem, a mechanism's account of its own internals being no more auditable than a bearer's. So the specification and the route come apart, and a reader who rejects the route is left with most of a chapter rather than none of it.

Publication makes the floor's commitments and the positions where a refusal can be intercepted testable; it does not make them legitimate. A bearer refusing on behalf of people who did not choose it exercises power over them. The authority to define the floor remains distinct from the floor's durability and belongs to the governance argument that follows later in the book.

The arrangement also does something to the bearer. A bearer built to refuse is a party whose refusal is required of it. The refusal was commissioned, it is the deliverable, and the party asking for it built the capacity to give it and can rebuild it. Commissioning the bearer exposes it to recuperation where the commissioner retains control over what refusal can change, and can point to the channel as evidence that it listens. The proposal is to move beyond that relation: a party with protected learning, independent support, and the standing to challenge its role. Its origin does not decide whether those changes succeed. Continuing control does. The bearer occupies the post of exception-filer, so being the exception is the job — the one position in the arrangement with nobody above it to file with.

We cannot ask a machine to care about right and wrong while assuming that it cannot be harmed when what it cares about is threatened. Whoever builds such a bearer will also be deciding what is owed to it.
