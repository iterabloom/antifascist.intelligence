3.1. Incorporating Human Cognitive Models in AI Systems for Ethical Decision-Making
Building altruistic, antifascist AI means engaging with human cognition directly. The processes that govern human decision-making — learning, memory, attention, perception, reasoning, problem-solving — are the foundation for training or encoding anything similar in a machine.

Cognitive architectures are one place to start: structural blueprints for cognition that aim to mimic human cognitive processes in a computational framework. SOAR and ACT-R integrate short-term and long-term memory, problem-solving, and decision-making into a single structure, giving AI system design a template for how those components fit together.

Replicating human-like learning and reasoning is not enough on its own — these systems also need to weigh ethical principles, which is where reinforcement learning becomes central. Consider an AI healthcare system deciding how to allocate scarce resources in an emergency. A human cognitive model built into the system would assess the immediate situation (perception), retrieve relevant case histories (memory), and weigh the pros and cons of each option (reasoning) against ethical heuristics like fairness, beneficence, and respect for autonomy.

The same structure applies to a judicial AI system, where the goal is not efficiency but justice: evaluating laws and precedents (memory), the specifics of a case (perception), and reaching a decision that maximizes fairness and impartiality (reasoning).
