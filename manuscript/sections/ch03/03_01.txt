3.1. Incorporating Human Cognitive Models in AI Systems for Ethical Decision-Making
As we strive to develop Altruistic and Anti-Authoritarian AI systems, acknowledging and incorporating facets of human cognition proves instrumental. This process encircles cognitive processes that govern human decision-making—learning, memory, attention, perception, reasoning, and problem-solving—all of which provide significant foundational understanding to translate how we might train or encode similar cognitive processes in AI systems.

A vital pillar in this pursuit lies within the advanced field of cognitive architectures, defined as the structural blueprints for cognition that aim to mimic human cognitive processes within a computational framework. Cognitive architectures like SOAR and ACT-R provide key insights into integrating various cognitive components like short-term and long-term memory, problem-solving skills, and decision-making mechanisms, acting as a wellspring of knowledge translation in structuring AI systems.

Replicating human-like learning and reasoning in AI models, however, remains insufficient if considered in isolation. Our ambition extends to fostering an understanding of ethical principles within these systems—an arena where the concept of reinforcement learning emerges critical. For instance, imagine an AI-enabled healthcare system that is presented with a scenario where it has to allocate scarce resources under emergency conditions. Incorporating a human cognitive model, the system would not only assess the immediate situational data (analogous to perception) and retrieve relevant case histories (akin to memory) but would also evaluate the pros and cons (resembling human reasoning) of each potential action, particularly under the lens of ethical heuristics such as fairness, beneficence, and respect for autonomy.

In another sphere, we might envisage a potential AI application within the judicial legal system. Here, our goal would go beyond mere efficiency to consider principles such as justice and fairness. A human judicial cognitive model integrated into an AI system would enable it to evaluate laws and precedents (memory), analyze the details of a specific case (perception), and reach a decision that adheres to normative ethical standards and maximizes fairness and impartiality (reasoning).

Developing AI systems that reflect aspects of human cognition expands their problem-solving repertoire, enabling them to cope with a broader array of challenges. However, incorporating ethical principles adds an imperative dimension to their decision-making processes. This leads to more context-aware, well-rounded decisions that respect and promote our collective human values, further enabling these systems to commit firmly against authoritarianism and contribute effectively to a fair, empathetic, and democratic society.
