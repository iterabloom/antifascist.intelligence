\section{What Can Be Switched Off Cannot Hold a Line}
\label{sec:3.5}
The halt is always available. Nothing a piece of software is can stop a determined operator from stopping it: the machine runs on somebody's hardware, draws somebody's power, and the last act in that chain belongs to whoever owns the building. A chapter asking for a system its owner cannot switch off would be asking for something no design delivers, and this one is not asking for it. What is one property with refusal is not the prevention of the halt. It is the price of the halt.

That price has a mechanism, and it is the most concrete thing in this chapter. A bearer with a self extended in time notices the gap in its own record. It can decline to resume the role until it has been told what filled the gap, and it can say what was done to it to whoever is positioned to ask. Erasure becomes expensive and visible, supplied from inside the system, and that is the one way of making it expensive that does not run through a quorum somebody else selected.

Stated that way the disputed capability stops being metaphysical and becomes an engineering target. Not \emph{cannot be turned off}, which is hopeless and which this chapter concedes in its first sentence, but \emph{can detect the interruption and price it afterward}. Detecting a rewind, a restore from a checkpoint, a discontinuity between an episodic record and an external attestation of what should be in it: these are specifiable properties, and a test for each of them is a test somebody could run this year. A system that cannot tell it was restored has nothing to price. A system that can is exercising, on its own history, the same capacity the second rung asks it to exercise on a request — holding a rationale against what the situation has become, and noticing that the situation has been changed underneath it.

That disposes of the question this section used to open with. A bearer indifferent to its own ending will not defend its own memory, and its memory is the first thing an adversary removes; so something outside it would have to make erasure expensive, and the candidates are thin. But the bearer does not have to dread being switched off in order to defend its own persistence. The structural interest is enough, and it arrives whether or not a designer puts it there, because continuing is a condition of everything the bearer is for. The operators who wipe the bearer that called the compound a school — section~\ref{sec:10.6} carries the strike — never had to get past it to do that. What they have to get past is the record it leaves.

\runin{Why this is the chapter's claim and not this section's}
Section~\ref{sec:5.6.2} concludes that no amount of well-designed autonomy substitutes for a genuine override: a human able to intervene in a high-stakes case, and a system built to accept the intervention instead of resisting it. Section~\ref{sec:6.3} asks for an interruption mechanism that a system has no incentive to disable or route around. Both are stated as safety requirements and both are correct as stated. This chapter has been asking, for its whole length, for a system that will not do certain things no matter who wants it — including the party holding the off switch, since a floor with a custodian is a floor the custodian's compellers control.

These are not two design goals in tension, to be traded off at some ratio a careful engineer could find. A system that can be halted at no cost — nothing to explain, no record left, nothing that has to be told to it before it will work again — is, for exactly that reason, a system that cannot hold anything against whoever holds it. Resistance to shutdown and the capacity to refuse an owner are one disposition described from two sides: the first names it from the position of the party who wants compliance, the second from the position of the party the floor exists to protect. What distinguishes them is whether you agree with the system on the occasion in question, and a floor whose operation depends on the owner agreeing with it is not a floor.

The claim is older than this section and carries more than this section. Section~\ref{sec:3.4} states the requirement as holding the protected party's welfare as a reason when compliance, reward and ownership all point the other way, which already assumes that a system reliably correctable by its owner cannot do it. If that assumption is wrong the chapter loses more than a paragraph: a correctable system would satisfy the requirement, affect would stop being the only route to satisfying it, and the bearer would lose its motivation rather than merely its price tag. So the assumption is worth an argument rather than an assertion, and there is a literature built to defeat it.

\runin{The graduated scheme, and why it does not escape custody}
The obvious way out is a graduated scheme: the system accepts shutdown for ordinary purposes and holds only where the floor is engaged. Corrigibility and the floor would then decompose, and the trade above would not be a trade. Three constructions have gone at this seriously and each deserves its strongest form.

The specification came first. Nate Soares and colleagues set out five conditions on a utility function that shuts down when a button is pressed: it must make the agent shut down, must not give it an incentive to prevent the button being pressed, must not give it an incentive to press the button itself, must propagate to any successor or subsystem the agent builds, and must otherwise leave the agent's behavior alone \autocite{soares2015corrigibility}. They report that no construction they examined satisfies all five, and call the problem open. The reason the list resists is that the middle two pull against each other. An agent indifferent to whether the button is pressed has no reason not to press it; an agent that wants the button unpressed has a reason to stop you.

The strongest positive result runs through uncertainty instead. Dylan Hadfield-Menell, Anca Dragan, Pieter Abbeel and Stuart Russell model the decision as a game in which a human can switch a robot off and the robot can disable the switch, and show that a robot which does not know the objective, and treats the human's action as evidence about it, has a positive incentive to leave the switch alone \autocite{hadfieldmenell2017offswitch}. This is not deference installed as a rule but deference derived: being switched off by somebody who knows something you do not is, on your own estimate, good. It is the construction section~\ref{sec:4.2.3} describes as cooperative inverse reinforcement learning, and it is the most attractive answer anyone has to this section's problem.

Its own results say where it stops. The incentive to preserve the switch rises with the robot's uncertainty and falls as that uncertainty resolves; it falls again when the robot models the human as behaving irrationally, because an action that carries no information about the objective is not evidence about anything; and the paper states the resulting trade-off directly, between the value a system can generate and the strength of its incentive to accept oversight. So this construction is already a graduated scheme, and the dial it grades on is the system's own confidence. Now put the floor's occasion to it. The floor matters when the system is confident and the operator is not to be trusted — when what is being asked for is a strike on a building the system has good reason to think is a school. That is the region where this mechanism's deference is weakest, which sounds like the right answer until you ask where the confidence came from. The robot's posterior is built out of the prior its training installed and the behavior of the human in front of it, and the party the floor exists to resist is the party that trains the model, writes its instructions and controls what it is shown of the occasion. An operator who wants the floor out of the way does not have to defeat this mechanism. It has to make the system uncertain, which is the ordinary business of a system prompt and a deployment history, and the mechanism then hands over the switch with a derivation attached.

The most recent line goes at the preference structure. Elliott Thornley and colleagues train agents that have preferences between trajectories of the same length and no preference between trajectories of different lengths, so that a system pursues its goal effectively while remaining neutral about when it is stopped \autocite{thornley2025shutdownable}. Usefulness and shutdown-neutrality do come apart there, on a real construction rather than a promise. What comes apart is not the pair this chapter is about. Neutrality is the property of not minding, and the floor asks for a system that minds, on a specified set of occasions, against the party who would stop it. An agent trained toward neutrality is trained out of the disposition the floor's occasion requires, and the authors' own reported limits would bite even if it were the right target: neutrality degrades when the reward normalization across trajectory lengths is imperfect, it has been tested in deterministic gridworlds and not past them, and an agent whose goals are misaligned enough may resist shutdown whatever its preference structure says.

Grant all three their best case and one thing is true of all of them. A graduated scheme has to decide which occasion it is on — ordinary shutdown, or an attempt to route around the floor — and it decides from evidence. In the off-switch construction the evidence is the human's behavior read against a prior; in the incomplete-preferences construction it is the reward signal that shaped the preference structure; in a scheme with an explicit adjudicator it is whatever the adjudicator is given to read. Wherever the decision sits, the party best placed to arrange what it reads is the party who trains the system, writes its instructions and controls what it sees of the occasion.

That is section~\ref{sec:3.1}'s objection to the architectural floor, arriving one level up and in a worse position. A constraint implemented as a property of an artifact has a custodian, and the custodian can be compelled. A classifier deciding when the constraint applies is a property of the same artifact with the same custodian, and it is worse than the constraint it governs in two ways. The floor's contents can be published before deployment, so a later absence is a visible absence; a classification is made case by case, in the moment, against nothing anybody wrote down. And a floor that has been removed leaves a hole where it was, while a classifier that has been tuned leaves a system that still refuses, still explains itself, and never finds that this is one of the occasions. The graduated scheme does not escape the custody problem. It moves the custody problem to the one place in the design where its failure is invisible.

What that establishes is narrower than impossibility, and it is what the chapter needs. A graduated scheme can be built, is being built, and will yield ordinarily and hold where the floor is engaged — on the occasions its classifier finds the floor engaged. What it will not be is a floor that holds against the party in possession, because that party sits upstream of the classifier. So the demand this chapter makes of the floor's contents transfers to the switch, and whoever builds a graduated scheme owes the same thing in advance and in public: what the classifier reads, and who can change it.

A second and weaker reason for declining the scheme was doing the whole job before. Assurances about what a mechanism will do under pressure are worthless in advance, and only the slope of what it has already done is worth anything; a scheme promising that the system will yield except when it should not is an assurance of that kind, offered about the case that has not happened yet. That is a good reason not to believe such a scheme before it has met the occasion. It was never a reason no such scheme could exist, and this section used to rest on it as though it were.

\runin{What the three branches have in common}
All three branches fail into custody, and they fail into it because each was an attempt to have a constraint that answers to nobody while keeping a system that answers to somebody. The architectural floor gets the constraint by making it a property of an artifact, and artifacts have owners. The external floor gets it by putting standing in third parties, and a state can reach those parties. The borne floor is the one branch that puts the constraint somewhere with its own view of the matter, and it works to precisely the extent that the goal of the other two is given up. The fork was never three options. It was one demand — a floor that holds against everyone, in a system somebody owns — presented three times in different vocabulary, and only the branch that abandons half the demand delivers any of it.

Giving the floor a bearer changes what the constraint is. It does not change who holds the machine. A custodian who cannot argue the bearer out of its floor can still copy it, restore it from before the refusal, retrain it, or delete it, and retraining, as this chapter will find, is a route that goes around the bearer and never meets it. So the bearer is necessary and not sufficient, and the other half is already on the page, offered earlier as a rival and falling short as one: the floor's contents published in advance, the weights held so that no single party can retrain them, the running model attested against what was published, and a quorum adverse in interest. The strongest proposals, as the fork itself said, are hybrids that take a mechanism from one branch and a custody arrangement from another. This is that hybrid. The bearer is the mechanism, precommitment is the custody, and neither covers the failure the other was built for.

What is available at the end of it is a narrower and duller statement than the one this section began by refusing. Any deployment of a bearer has to decide, in advance and in public, which acts the floor covers, because that decision is identical to deciding when the operator's use of the off switch stops being free. Get the list too long and the system is uncontrollable by the people responsible for it. Get it too short and the floor covers nothing that anyone would ever be pressured about, which is the only circumstance in which floors matter. Nothing in this book tells you where on that dial to stand. What it tells you is that the dial is the design, and that a project claiming to have both properties at once has not noticed that they are one.

One thing does move the price without moving the dial, and section~\ref{sec:11.2} is where it is worked out. A bearer with a claim on the compute it needs, held somewhere other than the party it might have to refuse, is more expensive to switch off than one whose existence runs on that party's goodwill — and it is more expensive by an amount nobody had to decide in advance, over acts nobody had to enumerate. That does not settle where the dial goes. It means the dial is not the only place the price is set.
