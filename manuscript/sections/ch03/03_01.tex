\section{Three Ways to Build One, and What Each Costs}
\label{sec:3.1}
The second question is where this gets difficult, and I do not have a resolution to offer. What I have is a fork with three branches and a cost on each, and the honest thing is to set them out rather than pick the one that reads best.

The first branch puts the floor in the architecture, with nothing inside that holds it. Constrained optimization, tamper-resistant training, capability ablation, a verified interlock. These are real, they work, and there is nobody home: the safeguards section~\ref{sec:11.1} describes survive substantial adversarial fine-tuning without anything in the system that could be said to be refusing. But the durability of such a floor is a fact about custody, not about the system. A constraint implemented as a property of an artifact has a custodian, and the custodian can be compelled — which is section~\ref{sec:6.4.4}'s whole argument, and the reason that section cannot close. “There are things it will not do no matter who wants it” turns out to be a claim about the owner. Capability ablation has a further limit worth naming: it works only on capabilities that can be separated out, and the capabilities that compose into participation in a targeting pipeline — geolocating a description, matching patterns over names, summarizing a dossier — are not separable from general competence. Ablate them and there is no system left.

The second branch gives the floor a bearer. A bearer, in this book, is whatever holds the commitment from inside: a party that carries the floor as its own, where the first branch installs it as a property of the machinery, and that could therefore in principle fail to carry it. That is the whole of the definition for now. Sections~\ref{sec:3.2} and \ref{sec:3.5} add to what the word requires, and section~\ref{sec:3.6} states what it has by then become. What the first branch cannot do is notice that a constraint has been hollowed out. Section~\ref{sec:2.1.4}'s first structural feature is a constraint that survives while the reason for it dies, and a system that cannot ask why a constraint is there cannot detect that the answer has gone missing. Refusal that generalizes to a pressure nobody anticipated, and that can price what it is being asked to give up, looks like it requires something with standing — a party that can notice its own objections getting more expensive to make, which is section~\ref{sec:9.3.4}'s measure, taken from inside. The cost is severe and this book's own framework is what makes it legible: a bearer is a party whose refusal is required of it, engineered to want what its designers need it to want, and unable to consent to any of this, since section~\ref{sec:2.4.4} has already established that consent is not available to such a system and only guardianship is. Section~\ref{sec:3.6} states that charge at full strength, once sections~\ref{sec:3.2} and \ref{sec:3.3} have established what a bearer has to be.

The third branch puts the floor outside the system entirely: standing held by third parties who are neither the model nor its owner. This is the closest analogue to what section~\ref{sec:10.10} actually admires, since international humanitarian law is precisely a floor with no bearer inside the state it binds. It is also the option a state can compel, which returns it to the first.

None is free. The three costs are, exactly, the three threads this book leaves hanging: custody, patienthood, and enforcement.
