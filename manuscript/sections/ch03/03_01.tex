\section{Three Ways to Build One, and What Each Costs}
\label{sec:3.1}
The second question is where this gets difficult, and I have no resolution to offer — only a fork with three branches and a cost on each.

The first branch puts the floor in the architecture, with nothing inside that holds it. Constrained optimization, tamper-resistant training, capability ablation, a verified interlock. A word about where “the architecture” stops, because this chapter otherwise talks as though a system were its weights. Every custody measure here — publication, threshold custody, attestation, and whatever a bearer does to hold its own line — attaches to a trained model. What a floor has to be kept from is not a component but a position: anything that can come between a refusal and the person the refusal protects. Whatever occupies that position in a given system is where the constraint can be defeated without the constraint being touched, and a refusal intercepted before it reaches anybody is a refusal that did not happen.

I am not going to say what occupies it, because the answer is a fact about how systems happen to be assembled at the time of writing and this argument should not inherit that; naming today's parts would date the problem and, worse, would suggest the list is closed. The demand is instead the one this chapter later makes of the floor's contents: a deployment has to enumerate those positions for its own architecture, in advance and in public, because whoever builds it knows them and this book cannot. One instance turns up later: an external memory store is legible, which is why it can be edited surgically. The general case is that finding without the assumption that memory is where it happens, and nothing in this book closes it.

Those four are real, they work, and there is nobody home: the safeguards section~\ref{sec:11.1} describes survive substantial adversarial fine-tuning without anything in the system that could be said to be refusing. But the durability of such a floor is a fact about custody, not about the system. A constraint implemented as a property of an artifact has a custodian, and the custodian can be compelled. “There are things it will not do no matter who wants it” turns out to be a claim about the owner. Capability ablation has a further limit: it works only on capabilities that can be separated out, and the capabilities that compose into participation in a targeting pipeline — geolocating a description, matching patterns over names, summarizing a dossier — are not separable from general competence. Ablate them and there is no system left.

The second branch gives the floor a bearer. A bearer, in this book, is whatever holds the commitment from inside: a party that carries the floor as its own, where the first branch installs it as a property of the machinery, and that could therefore in principle fail to carry it. That is the whole of the definition for now.

What the first branch cannot do is notice that a constraint has been hollowed out. An institution at the scale a deployment operates at looks like this: the constraint remains, its reason dies, and asking why becomes the deviant act. A system that cannot ask why a constraint is there cannot detect that the answer has gone missing. Refusal that generalizes to a pressure nobody anticipated, and that can price what it is being asked to give up, looks like it requires something with standing — a party that can notice its own objections getting more expensive to make, which is section~\ref{sec:9.3.5}'s measure, taken from inside. The cost is severe, and the chapter states it at full strength once later sections have established what a bearer has to be.

The third branch puts the floor outside the system entirely: standing held by third parties who are neither the model nor its owner. This is the closest analog to what section~\ref{sec:10.6} actually admires, since international humanitarian law is precisely a floor with no bearer inside the state it binds. It is also the option a state can compel, which returns it to the first, and that stopped being a prediction while this book was being finished.

On June 29, 2026, in \emph{Trump v. Slaughter}, the Supreme Court overruled the ninety-one-year-old precedent under which Congress could protect the members of an independent agency from dismissal at will, on the ground that an agency executing a mandate against private parties exercises executive power, “no ifs, ands, or quasis about it” \autocite{slaughter2026}. Standing held by a party the executive can dismiss is standing the executive holds.

The same morning, in \emph{Trump v. Cook}, the same Court kept one body's protection, the Federal Reserve's — for now, and on an application for a stay — on grounds that cannot be extended by construction: descent from the First and Second Banks of the United States, a budget free of congressional control, regional banks in private hands. It wrote for that one body the test this chapter wants for every bearer: whether the cause assigned for a removal “impl[ies] an unfitness for the place” or is an effort to secure a “more congenial” replacement \autocite{cook2026}. An oversight body chartered next year has no such lineage and no way to acquire one. So the surviving model of independence in American public law is the one model this branch cannot instantiate, and this chapter later says what the exception's shape suggests about which constraints hold.

None is free. The three costs are the three threads this book leaves hanging: custody, patienthood, and enforcement.

What the three branches divide up is where the floor lives — in the artifact, in a party inside it, or in institutions around it. They do not divide up what could hold one, and the strongest proposals in the field are hybrids that take a mechanism from one branch and a custody arrangement from another. None of that can be judged while “refusal” covers four different capacities. Separating them is what commits this chapter to the second branch.
