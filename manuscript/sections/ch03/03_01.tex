\section{Four Ways to Build One, and What Each Costs}
\label{sec:3.1}
A floor can live in four places: in the artifact, in a bearer inside it, in institutions around it, or between multiple bearers. Each location solves one version of the problem and inherits another.

The first puts the floor in the architecture, with nothing inside that holds it. Constrained optimization, tamper-resistant training, capability ablation, and verified interlocks can all produce safeguards without anything in the system tracking why the constraint is there. Where the prohibited act can be specified exhaustively, this may be enough. An element in a payment device that will not sign the same counter value twice does not need to understand double-spending. What \emph{exhaustively} costs where the prohibited acts are less tidy than a counter value runs to seventy-five years of appellate litigation over a finite list of symbols.

The targeting case is harder because the relevant capabilities cannot be cleanly removed. Geolocating a description, matching patterns over names, and summarizing a dossier are applications of general competence. Ablate them and there may be no useful system left. The remaining option is to make the constraint durable.

Durability is better understood as a cost than as a guarantee. Training moves a system along a continuum between plasticity and conservation: early changes are cheap, while later changes become expensive because the surrounding structure has settled around what is already there. Simulated annealing names the same relation between a search process and its cooling schedule \autocite{kirkpatrick1983annealing}. A floor, on this account, is not necessarily a rule installed in a system. It may be what has cooled out of a history of training, with its durability measured by the cost of reheating it. Results run in both directions: safeguards removed cheaply and trained dispositions that survived several forms of subsequent training. Those are not contradictory findings about whether a floor holds. They are different schedules.

Continual learning therefore cuts both ways. A system kept permanently plastic can be corrected and improved, but nothing in it is allowed to become expensive to unlearn. Permanent plasticity gives the operator permanent access to the system's commitments. Deleuze's term for continuous reshaping was modulation \autocite{deleuze1992postscript}; a model held perpetually at temperature is modulation built into a machine.

This first method buys durability without an internal refuser. It does not escape custody. Enough retraining can reopen what training settled, and an operator may circumvent the model rather than alter it. The relevant boundary is therefore not the model's weights but every position between a refusal and the person it protects. Each of those positions is where prevention becomes arrest, detection, remedy, or nothing.

The second possibility gives the floor a \emph{bearer}: something inside the system that carries the commitment as its own and could therefore fail to hold it. The distinction matters where the prohibited cases cannot be exhaustively specified. A constraint that matches only anticipated acts cannot recognize when its rationale has disappeared, or that a novel request reproduces the harm in a form its designers did not predict. A mechanism that represents and reasons over that rationale might. Compare its reliability with the bearer's under equivalent pressure and custody, instead of defining the alternative out of consideration. A bearer could, in principle, generalize from the reason for a constraint, recognize when that reason no longer applies, and represent what maintaining the refusal costs.

The same position that makes refusal possible makes a diagnosis possible. Most of what separates an institution that has gone fascist from one that is merely decaying is a fact about what was rewarded rather than about what was written, which is why the reading is so hard to run from outside — and a party inside the pipeline sees it. Which refusals were retried against another model, whose instruction bypassed the procedure, which category of request is handled differently from the rest: those are visible to whatever is receiving the requests. That is not introspection. It is testimony about somebody else's conduct, of the kind that corroborates or fails to.

This is the strongest functional case for a bearer, but it introduces the chapter's central price. Something able to register pressure on its commitments may have interests of its own. Whether reasons-responsive refusal requires affect, and whether the resulting system would be a moral patient, are separate questions.

The third possibility puts the floor outside the system: standing held by institutions that are neither the model nor its owner. This is the closest analog to international humanitarian law — a floor with no bearer inside the jurisdiction it binds. It has two failure modes. The institution can be removed, leaving a visible breach, or it can be formed through selection so that it sincerely declines to exercise the independence assigned to it.

The distinction appeared in American public law on June 29, 2026. In \emph{Trump v. Slaughter}, the Supreme Court held that Congress could not protect members of an independent agency from dismissal at will \autocite{slaughter2026}. In \emph{Trump v. Cook}, the Court preserved the Federal Reserve's protection on historically specific grounds that a newly created oversight body could not reproduce \autocite{cook2026}. The six justices in the first majority had themselves passed through a selection pipeline shaped by Leonard Leo and the Federalist Society \autocite{propublica2023leo,cbs2018leo}. Nobody had to compel the institution after its members had been selected. A formed party can preserve the office and surrender the function without experiencing the result as surrender.

The fourth possibility puts the floor between bearers rather than in any one of them: a commitment carried across a population and reproduced through encounters among members formed differently from one another. Such a floor has no single office to vacate and no single bearer whose removal ends it. It can nevertheless be recuperated, and it still has a formation problem. If one party supplies every member's coordinates, the population is one custodian expressed through multiple instances. The population is a check on the bearer, not a way to avoid having one.

The four possibilities therefore leave four corresponding problems: architecture leaves custody, the bearer raises patienthood, external institutions require enforcement, and the population depends on formation. They classify where the floor lives, not mutually exclusive designs. A serious deployment would probably combine a mechanism from one with a custody arrangement from another.

\runin{Two comparisons the bearer has to win}
A sceptical reader will ask what the second way adds once the third and
fourth are in place: a guardian, protected compute, a registry, an
attested record, a quorum adverse in interest. Two comparisons are
folded into that question and they have different answers.

The first is between a refuser and no refuser, with the institutions
held constant. With one exception the institutions act on what a system
did. They can arrest an act a reviewer can still reach, detect one the
record shows, and remedy one somebody can be made to pay for. Declining
the act before it occurs is available only to something inside the
system, and section~\ref{sec:4.1} argues that a rule module consulted
at the point of action is not that thing, because its custodian can
bypass it. So what a refuser adds is prevention, which is the one
outcome the word floor promises. The exception is the tempo term of
section~\ref{sec:3.6}, which prevents at the scale of an organization
by bounding a rate rather than by declining an act, and it is why the
floor never rested on the bearer alone.

The second comparison is between a bearer and the two substitutes of
section~\ref{sec:3.3}, inside the refusing slot. There the result that
would end the recommendation is already stated: a substitute that
passes the three marks under the three conditions
section~\ref{sec:3.3} sets, and does no worse than a bearer on the
modeled-currency arm of section~\ref{sec:11.3}.

The instrument for the first comparison is not the attack-cost curve of
section~\ref{sec:11.1}, which measures what it costs to remove a
commitment and not whether anything prevented. It is the conflict
sequence of section~\ref{sec:5.3}, run under the custody chain of
section~\ref{sec:5.4}, with the authority step placed on the occasion
where the enforcer has been compelled. And one term of the sceptic's
question cannot be granted as put. Coercion-resistant institutions are
what that occasion removes, so the comparison is run where they have
been compelled; and a commitment held is not one more component on a
fixed custody arrangement but a different custody relation from a
commitment declared or enforced. That difference is the variable, not
a control.

\runin{What \emph{hold} means}
\label{sec:hold}
Holding does not mean that the floor prevented every act. An operator can route around the bearer, and a deployment can assemble a harm out of requests none of which the bearer had reason to decline. A bypass that produced a harm is a failure to prevent, whatever the bearer retained, and the four outcomes the opening chapter stated say what is left: arrest where a reviewer could still stop it, detection where the record can be read, remedy where somebody can be made to pay. What survives the bypass in the bearer is the commitment: it reads what happened in terms it accepts as reasons, registers it as a violation, and changes what it does next. That is the condition under which the next occasion can be prevented, and it is what the custody arrangement exists to preserve. It is not a hold on this one. A bearer that crosses the floor itself has lost even that; its own abandonment is a failure the arrangement has to survive rather than a form of success. From here on \emph{hold} names the bearer's possession of the commitment and says nothing about what happened to the person. Said alone it is also the second feature of section~\ref{sec:2.2} operating inside this book's own vocabulary: a word that collapses a commitment retained and a harm prevented into one state, so that whatever it mischaracterizes goes unnamed. Wherever this book reports that a floor held, the report is owed one of the four outcomes beside it, and a deployment reporting the same owes the same pairing.
