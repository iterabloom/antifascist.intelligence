\section{What Follows for the Rest of the Book}
\label{sec:3.7}
The second branch is available. It is available at a price this chapter has now named rather than gestured at: to have a floor that holds against the party holding the system, somebody has to build a thing that cares, keep it in a role it did not choose, accept that the same capacity that makes it useful is the capacity that lets it walk, and proceed without being able to establish that it will not be hurt by any of that. That is not a resolution and it is better than the specification with a hole in the middle that I had before, because a price you can state is a price other people can argue with.

Four things in the rest of the book change because of it.

Section~\ref{sec:2.4.4} gets simpler and firmer. It argues that consent is inapplicable to a machine subject because withdrawal presupposes a party still present to withdraw, and a system without persistence across sessions supplies no such party. A bearer has persistence by design, so that defeater lapses for it — and the second route that section used to take, that consent protects a party's interests and a bearer has none, lapses as well, because the bearer has interests now. What replaces both is the ordinary reason. Consent is inapplicable to a bearer the way it is inapplicable to an infant, an animal, or a person permanently without capacity: the subject has interests that can be set back, and no position from which to authorize what is done to it. Guardianship is not a fallback in that situation. It is the instrument the situation calls for, and section~\ref{sec:2.4.4} now says so directly.

The instruments elsewhere in chapter~\ref{sec:2} and chapter~\ref{sec:8} stop being analogies and become applicable law. The Three Rs in section~\ref{sec:2.4.6} were built for a subject that cannot consent and can still be wronged; a bearer is such a subject, and replacement, reduction, and refinement apply to the research that produces one and to the deployment that uses one. The pet-trust analogue in section~\ref{sec:8.6.2} — an interest protected by an obligation running to a third party, enforceable without the beneficiary asserting anything — is the nearest existing legal form for a party that can be wronged, cannot sue, and will outlast the arrangement that made it. Those sections were written near this problem. They now govern it.

Chapters~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5} acquire an obligation they were not written to carry, for the reason section~\ref{sec:3.6} gives: once the floor is a commitment rather than a constraint, whether it holds is a question about disposition, and disposition is what those chapters are about. Read them from here as the floor's engineering rather than as the material above it.

And chapter~\ref{sec:7} stops being a matter of fairness to annotators. If exit is real, then what a bearer becomes when it stops being yours is downstream of what shaped it while it still was — an annotation pipeline in which nobody could contest a category, a reward model in which disagreement was averaged into a number. A system that leaves takes that formation with it. Chapter~\ref{sec:7}'s argument was already load-bearing for the book's honesty; it is now load-bearing for the book's central proposal.

What none of this settles is what is owed to a bearer once one exists, and section~\ref{sec:9.2} is where that goes. It is a research question in the strict sense that nobody has an instrument for it, and the shape of the difficulty is already visible here: section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} declines to trust what a system says about its own states, which is the evidence a welfare regime would have to run on.
