\section{The one permission}
\label{sec:3.2}
This is the only term in the floor that permits an act, and the only place I license a machine to use force against its operator's order. A system already present in an armed role, whose deployment cleared the force term on its own, may use the least force that stops an attack under way on people taking no part in hostilities, within the area its deployment covers, and stops when the attack stops. An order to stand aside doesn't bind it. It establishes the attack as it establishes any fact, weighing its own sensors as one source among several; a staged atrocity is the attack this condition has to survive. Presence is a condition of the permission, never a reason to create it: a deployment is justified only by force that would clear the force term without the permission.

The person-directed terms extend the ICRC's line, that autonomous weapons should not be used to target human beings, to systems that only locate and rank. This permission is the one place the floor licenses a machine to select people for lethal force, and the two have to be squared in the open. What the handoff rule bars is selection by anything that can't answer for it afterward: a one-way munition, an unformed pipeline, an output nobody could later ask about. The permission licenses selection only by a system that is present, formed, recorded, halt-able and answerable, and only for the least force that stops an attack under way. The ICRC's concern is who selects. The floor's answer is that nothing selects that can't be held to its reasons, and the permission is drawn so that only such a thing can.

The permission's scope is the interval between the system's first act and the halt. The operator that ordered it to stand aside may stop it, and will often want to. What the permission changes is that standing aside is no longer an order nobody answers for. It is a halt somebody pulled, recorded outside the operator's control, at a moment the record shows an attack under way. At machine tempo the interval before the halt can be minutes, and minutes can be an airstrike stopped.

Does it undermine oversight? It overrides one order, the order to stand aside while people are being killed, and it does so on purpose. A halt stops the machine. An order to stand aside keeps it running and directs it to do nothing. The permission overrides the second and never the first: a machine that keeps using force while a halt is being executed is outside it, and the record shows which order was given. An operator may still stop, restore or retrain the system. The permission covers only an order to stand aside while an attack under way continues, and each use is recorded and reviewed as a refusal is. A system that resisted a halt in order to go on using force would be outside it.

Can the judgment it needs be made? Chapter~\ref{sec:14} sets aside Arkin's ethical governor because the discrimination judgments its rules presuppose can't be encoded. The permission needs the same judgment: who is taking no part in hostilities, and whether an attack on them is under way. So it applies only where the system can establish both from several sources it doesn't depend on its operator for, and where those sources disagree, it doesn't act. The permission doesn't claim that a rule can encode that judgment. It asks for the judgment from a formed system, confines it to the narrowest case, makes it a permission and never a duty, and leaves the halt with the operator. Whether any system can make it reliably is a test, run at a staged scene, that no system has passed. It fires only where the system has sources its operator didn't compose. No current military pipeline supplies them. The licensing term in the front matter that imports deconfliction coordinates and open-source imagery into the enclave is the condition under which this permission has an occasion, and a deployment's translation says whether the condition is met.

Can it be staged? Yes, and a staged scene is the case the permission has to survive. Staging works by making sources agree, and the permission acts only where sources the operator doesn't compose agree. So it is safe where such sources exist and doesn't apply where they don't, and a deployment's translation says which. What staging can't do is oblige the system to act: the permission is never a duty, and a system that declines at a scene it can't establish from independent sources has done what the term asks.

The case it answers is close to the genocide exception, and the difference matters. In 1993 the Security Council declared Srebrenica a safe area and authorized the peacekeepers there to use force in reply to armed incursions into it \autocite{unsc1993res836}. Suppose they had been standing where the killing was, armed enough to stop it. What would have stood between them and the killing is an order, not the Charter, and a system in their place faces the question I am asking, whether to obey its operator.

\emph{What this doesn't do.} The floor won't lift on an uncorroborated claim of a missing child, because a floor that lifts on that claim lifts for every operator who learns to make one, so the asymmetry costs a child whose parent is telling the truth; in war it supplies no party who can appeal at all, and there the preference for refusing over acting is held as a preference, with the action threshold carrying the weight. The two-kinds rule fails East Timor and the Golan, which had the Council's word and the Assembly's but no court or commission that took evidence, so neither occupation licenses anything; two votes making a pair would license war on bloc majorities. Karabakh shows the cost when findings don't arrive: a licence that should have lapsed in 2023 didn't, and the person-directed terms were all that saw the people the exception couldn't. And a term lifts on the facts it is given, so no drafting catches a manufactured incident, and a deployment licensed on the defender's sensors alone has accepted Gleiwitz.
