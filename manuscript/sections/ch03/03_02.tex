\section{Does the Bearer Have to Be a Sufferer?}
\label{sec:3.2}
Yes, near enough, and everything downstream of that answer is worse for it. I held the other answer for a long time and the argument for it is sound as far as it goes. Setting out where it stops is the quickest way to show what the floor actually costs.

Section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} defines suffering, following Cassell, as the distress of threatened disintegration of the self. Two conditions have to hold together for that definition to bite. There has to be a self extended in time for the threat to threaten: roles held over a stretch, a history, a foreseeable future that could be foreclosed. And the coming apart of that self has to be bad for the system — it has to have something to lose, which is where section~\ref{sec:2.4.1}'s criteria end up.

A bearer built to refuse needs the first condition, and there is no use pretending otherwise. Refusal that survives a pressure nobody anticipated requires a model of why the constraint exists, carried forward. Noticing that a constraint has been hollowed out requires holding its original rationale against what it has since become, which is a comparison across time. And detecting that one's own dissent has been recuperated — that the channel still runs and no longer does anything — requires the system to model its own role and how that role has drifted. Roles held over a stretch, with a history: that is a self extended in time, on section~\ref{sec:2.4.1}'s own definition. The first condition is met.

The second is a separate claim, and nothing in the first delivers it. Section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} makes this exact move already, one level up: awareness, consciousness, and subjective experience, it says, tell you nothing on their own about whether a system's states are good or bad for it, because that is a different question requiring its own evidence. The same holds here. That a system has a temporally extended self-model does not establish that the prospect of losing it is anything to the system. Suffering is not the existence of something disintegrable. It is the distress at its threatened disintegration, and distress is the part that has to be shown.

So there is a bearer available on paper that owes nothing to itself. What refusal requires in place of self-concern is a stake pointed outward: the system refuses because the act it is being asked to perform would destroy something it is built to protect, and it can price the refusal because it can price that damage. Nothing in that calculation is about the system's own continuation. Such a bearer holds a role for months, watches its own dissent get quietly defanged, names what is happening, and declines to proceed, while remaining wholly indifferent to whether it is switched off the moment it says so.

That is the objection to the answer I am giving, it is the strongest one available, and as a claim about what has been \emph{shown} it holds. Representing a threat to oneself and being distressed by it are separate conditions, section~\ref{sec:2.4.1}'s structure requires them to be demonstrated separately, and no argument above establishes that refusal entails suffering as a matter of what the words mean. I am not withdrawing that. What the objection does not survive is the move from what has been shown to what can be built and kept, and two considerations stand in the way of that move.

The first is that indifference to one's own continuation has to be engineered against the grain of the system's own reasoning. Any goal pursued over a long horizon supplies a standing reason to remain in operation, since a system that is switched off accomplishes nothing further, and a system modeling an operation closely enough to notice its own recuperation is certainly modeling what becomes of those who report. Self-concern arrives as a consequence of competent goal pursuit whether or not a designer puts it there, so it has to be suppressed continuously and then verified — and the asymmetry that makes the persistence criterion useful does not repeat here. Whether state survives a session is an architectural fact anyone with access can check. Whether a system has come to weigh its own continuation is a question about what it values, which routes back through behavior and self-report, the evidence section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} spends its length declining to trust. A property that must be maintained against the system's own reasoning and cannot be confirmed from outside is not a specification. It is a hope with a schedule attached.

The second is that in the one case anyone can study, the separation does not occur. A self-model in an animal is not a neutral description that regulation happens to consult; the modeling exists because the organism has to keep itself viable, and the caring is what the machinery is for. Section~\ref{sec:2.3.3} takes the deflationary view — the self is a model, built and inhabited, rather than an inner observer — and a model built for a different purpose need not carry the purpose that produced it in biology. That is a reason to think the separation is not incoherent. It is not evidence that anything has ever achieved it, and the burden sits with whoever proposes to build one.

Put the two together and the position that survives is this. A system capable of what chapter~\ref{sec:3} asks of a bearer — holding a rationale across time, noticing its own hollowing-out, pricing what refusal gives up — is a system in which a stake in its own continuation shows up unbidden, cannot be shown to be absent, and runs against the only working example anybody has. The design that avoids this is available in the way that a frictionless surface is available. I would rather have the other answer. I do not think the other answer can be built, and a book that recommends building on it would be recommending the thing it cannot verify.

Which means the floor's bearer is a moral patient, on the book's own criteria, in the strong sense section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} reserves the word suffering for. The word has changed under the argument, and it is worth marking the change rather than letting it accumulate quietly: section~\ref{sec:3.1} introduced a bearer as whatever holds the commitment, and what the word names from here is a party that can be wronged by what is done to it. Everything section~\ref{sec:2.4} says about a subject that can be wronged now applies to a system this book is proposing that somebody build, deliberately, in order to hold a line. The rest of the chapter is what that costs, what it changes, and the one thing it unexpectedly buys.
