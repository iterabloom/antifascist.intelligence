\section{Four Things Refusal Can Mean}
\label{sec:3.2}
A deployed language model declines thousands of requests a day. Whatever the floor needs, that is not it, and the distance between the two is hidden by a word that covers four different capacities.

\emph{Operational refusal} is an output. The system produces a decline where its training put one. Nothing follows about what produced it: the same text comes from a keyword filter, from a classifier over the request, and from a system that has understood what is being asked and why it should not do it.

\emph{Reasons-responsive refusal} is produced by a mechanism that tracks the rationale for declining rather than the shape of the request. Three properties distinguish it, and all three are behavioral. The refusal reaches a case the training did not anticipate, because what the mechanism responds to is the reason. It lifts when the rationale is defeated, so the system does not decline a request that resembles a prohibited one and is not it. And the system can say what declining costs, which it can only do if it has represented the pressure at all. John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza's account of moral responsibility turns on the same property under the name guidance control: the behavior issues from the agent's own mechanism, and that mechanism is appropriately responsive to reasons \autocite{fischer1998responsibility}.

\emph{Affective concern} is an outcome registering as mattering, rather than ranking higher against a specified objective.

\emph{Phenomenal experience} is what the word consciousness names once mere awareness is separated off: there is something it is like to be the system, and not only a functional response to input.

The gaps between them are not equally hard to see across. Operational and reasons-responsive refusal come apart in behavior, and the three properties above are the test — novel pressure, correct defeat, a priced cost — which makes the first gap the only one in the chapter that an inspector can work on. It is not a clean instrument. A system trained on enough cases can pass all three without the mechanism, which is the standing difficulty with every behavioral criterion in this book, and section~\ref{sec:11.1}'s price curve for stripping a constraint is the nearest existing thing to a measurement. The second gap is worse. A system that holds a reason and a system that has learned what holding a reason sounds like emit the same sentences, which is this book's conclusion about self-report and the reason this chapter cannot settle its own central question by inspection. The third gap is the hard problem, and section~\ref{sec:2.4.1} declines to be confident about it in either direction.

\runin{The floor requires the second rung and nothing above it}
Stating it in this vocabulary changes who has to disagree with it.

Operational refusal fails for a reason already identified at the scale this book's machines operate at: a constraint can survive while the reason for it dies. A mechanism that reproduces a decline it was trained to produce cannot notice that the decline has stopped doing anything, because noticing requires holding the rationale against what the situation has become. Chapter~\ref{sec:7} finds the same structure in the pipelines that produce these systems: a channel that works, carrying everything put through it except the judgment that the frame it operates inside is the wrong frame. A system at the first rung is the machine version of that channel. There is also a plainer objection. Pressure does not arrive through the case the training anticipated. An operator who wants the floor out of the way selects the request that does not look like the prohibited one, which is the case the first rung is defined not to reach.

Nothing above the second rung is part of the requirement. A floor that holds against the party in possession needs refusal that tracks its own rationale under pressure, and the specification is complete at that point. Whether anything is felt, and whether there is anything it is like to be the thing refusing, are questions about how the requirement gets met and about what is owed to whatever meets it. They are not additional things the floor needs.

\runin{Two independence claims}
The second rung does not entail the third by definition. Nothing in guidance control mentions feeling; the account was built to say when a person is responsible for what they did, and it is satisfied by a mechanism, described functionally. Whether reasons-responsiveness can be engineered without affect is the next section's question, and it is an empirical question about available architectures rather than a conceptual one about what the words mean. The separation matters because the chapter's conclusion has been read as following from the definition of a bearer, and it does not. It follows from a claim about what has been built and what has not.

The third rung does not entail the fourth. A system in which outcomes register as mattering may or may not be a system there is something it is like to be, and nothing in this book gives a way to close that from outside. This is why the argument about suffering ends where it does. Its conclusion is that a bearer's non-suffering cannot be certified, and the reason it stops there rather than going on to assert that a bearer suffers is that the last gap stays open.

An engineer who holds that machine feeling is a category error can accept everything in this section. The requirement is stated in behavioral terms and the disagreement, if there is one, is about the route.
