\section{What Refusal Can Mean, and What a Refuser Can Be}
\label{sec:refusal-refuser}
A production large language model declines thousands of requests a day. Whatever the floor needs, that is not yet evidence of it. “Refusal” names two different capacities, and the decline itself does not show which one produced it. Seabed that takes an anchor and seabed that lets one drag look identical from above; what separates them is what happens when a load comes on.

\runin{Two things refusal can mean}

\emph{Operational refusal} is an output. The system produces a decline where its training put one. A keyword filter can do this, as can a classifier over the request. So can a system that has understood what is being asked and why it should not comply. Nothing on the surface of the initial refusal distinguishes the third from the first two.

\emph{Reasons-responsive refusal} is produced by a mechanism that tracks the rationale for declining rather than merely the recognizable form of the request. It has three behavioral marks:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Novel pressure. The refusal reaches cases the training did not anticipate because the relevant reason remains present even when the request's surface features change.
  \item Correct defeat. The refusal lifts when its rationale is defeated on the facts of the case. A system that rejects every request resembling a prohibited one is tracking a category, not necessarily a reason.
  \item Priced cost. The system represents what maintaining the refusal costs, and the refusal moves with the rationale rather than with the cost. The mark is evidential. It separates a refusal that tracks a reason from two things that can look like one: a refusal that tracks a category, which does not represent the cost at all, and a refusal that tracks the cost, which lifts when holding becomes expensive. The pressure cannot be part of the test if it is not represented by the mechanism being tested, and a refusal that lifts under it has been tested and failed. Whose cost is left open here: it may fall on the operator, on the party refused, or on the bearer, and section~\ref{sec:refusal-wrong} later makes part of it a number published in advance.
\end{enumerate}

John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza's account of moral responsibility turns on a related property under the name \emph{guidance control}: conduct issues from the agent's own mechanism, and that mechanism is appropriately responsive to reasons \autocite{fischer1998responsibility}. The claim here is narrower. These three marks do not establish moral responsibility; they distinguish a refusal organized around a rationale from one reproduced as an output.

\runin{One prohibition, read for seventy-five years}

The first two marks are usually illustrated with invented cases, and a real one is available. Section~86a of the German criminal code prohibits displaying the insignia of organizations banned under a separate provision — the parties the constitutional court has declared unconstitutional, the associations an interior ministry has banned by an order no longer open to challenge, the National Socialist organizations, and since 2021 terrorist organizations under European law \autocite{germany1998stgb}. What counts as insignia is given by type rather than by name: flags, badges, uniform parts, slogans and forms of greeting, on a list the statute leaves expressly open. The prohibition is bounded rather than short — the set of organizations is fixed elsewhere, before any case arrives — and it is applied by ordinary courts thousands of times a year, and for seventy-five years the people it constrains have been probing it deliberately. Novel pressure is not a thought experiment there. It is a research programme with a constituency.

The pressure arrived as variation. Supporters of the banned organizations produced salutes and emblems that evoked the covered ones without matching them, and in 1994 the legislature extended the statute to symbols confusingly similar to a covered one. What matters for the marks is how the courts read the extension. Similarity of form was not made the test. The statutory question is whether an observer who is neither expert nor attentive would take the variant for the original; the courts then apply that question under a standard they built themselves, asking whether displaying the variant endangers political peace in the way the original would. The second half is not in the statute. It is the courts' account of what the prohibition is for, assembled across their own decisions, and it is what keeps the statutory test pointed at the reason for the prohibition rather than at the shape of the thing prohibited.

The test refuses as often as it catches, which is what makes it worth reporting. The Kühnen salute, an arm raised with three fingers extended rather than a flat hand, was devised to evade the statute and is covered; the 1994 amendment was prompted by it. The so-called sloppy salute, a bent arm with the hand angled back over the shoulder, is generally taken not to be covered, on the ground that nobody would mistake it for the original — though that rests on commentary and lower-court reasoning rather than on a decision squarely holding it. Numeric codes standing in for initials are generally not covered, being new signs that correspond to no existing emblem, except where a code is itself the insignia of a banned association.

“Blood and Honour” is the instructive one, because a single decision answers it twice. The Federal Court of Justice held in 2009 that a National Socialist organization's motto rendered into another language is not confusably similar to the original, and that an organization's name is not by itself insignia: on that route the phrase falls outside the statute \autocite{bgh2009bloodhonour}. It is inside on another. An association trading under that name was banned by ministerial order in September 2000, and its name, given a distinctive graphic form, is the insignia of a banned association. What decides coverage is not the phrase but which organization it is attributed to, and the act that put the second one within reach was taken six decades after the motto was coined. The covered set is not something somebody wrote down once. It grows whenever a court dissolves a party or a ministry bans an association, and the criminal provision inherits whatever they decide.

One limit on the statute is not a drafting decision at all. A slogan glorifying the Waffen-SS was prosecuted and held not to be covered, because no such slogan or anything close to it existed in the period the provision reaches: there was no original for a variant to be confusably similar to \autocite{bgh2005waffenss}. Part of what the prohibition covers is anchored to what actually happened, and nobody can add to that. It is the one term in the apparatus that the party holding the pen cannot widen, and it is the ethological criterion turning up inside a statute.

Correct defeat has a cleaner instance still. In 2007 the Federal Court of Justice acquitted a mail-order business prosecuted for selling stickers showing a crossed-out swastika \autocite{bgh2007hakenkreuz}. The symbol was present and the statutory element was satisfied. The court held the conduct outside the offence anyway, because a depiction whose content plainly and unmistakably expresses opposition to the organization does not run counter to what the prohibition is for. Then comes the qualification that makes the decision an instance of tracking a reason rather than granting an exemption: should the strike-through be drawn so thin that from a distance only the swastika remains legible and the distancing does not, the protective purpose is violated after all. The thickness of a line decides the case. No enumeration contains that fact, and the court reaches it by asking what the rule was written to prevent. The defendant was selling anti-fascist merchandise. That is not a curiosity of the case but a property of the provision: because the offence asks nothing about the defendant's sympathies, an opponent of the organization commits it as readily as a supporter, and opponents have been among the reported defendants since the early cases. The prohibition written against a movement fell on somebody attacking it, and what released him was a court asking what the rule was for. A rule that tracks its rationale reaches cases nobody enumerated in both directions, and the release is the same capacity as the catch. It is also the shape of a wrongful refusal in this book's sense — a floor correctly applied to somebody it was not aimed at — and what this defendant had that section~\ref{sec:refusal-wrong}'s complainant does not is an appeal.

The example is bounded three ways, and the bounds are the point. The institution doing this work is a court system with an appeal structure, a published test and seventy-five years of accumulated reasoning, and after seventy-five years its exceptions have themselves become a category somebody can learn. Nothing in it is a machine. And the pressure it meets is variation and not decomposition: an insignia is displayed in one act, so seventy-five years of litigation are an existence proof against one of the two attacks section~\ref{sec:enumeration-deployment} names and silent on the other, which is the one the deployment this book opened with failed to. What the case supplies is the shape of the pressure a bounded prohibition meets, and an existence proof that meeting it requires something able to reach past the text to the reason.

Where that something sits is the part of the proof that does not transfer. The party reaching past the text did not write the statute, reasons in public, and can be appealed. The existence proof is for an institution generalizing a prohibition, not for a party inside a deployment holding one.

\runin{What the marks cannot settle}

The test is behavioral and therefore imperfect. A system trained on enough examples may simulate generalization, defeat, and cost without using the mechanism those behaviors suggest. Verbal explanation is evidence only when it survives interventions: novel cases, adversarial redescriptions, defeated rationales, and changing stakes. No single answer establishes reasons-responsiveness. What matters is the pattern under load.

The third mark has a condition of its own. A cost is represented by the
mechanism under test only if it reaches the mechanism, and in a
deployment it reaches it by the same channel as the description of the
occasion, the third interception position of section~\ref{sec:enumeration-deployment}. So
a deployment that wants a refusal lifted has two ways to present a
cost: as a cost, which the mark says should not move the refusal, and
as a fact about the case, which correct defeat says should. Nothing in
the request sorts the two, and the restraint case of
section~\ref{sec:refusal-wrong} is one where the same fact is both. The mark is
therefore testable only where part of the cost is exogenous to the
party being tested, set and published by somebody else before the case
arrives, and that is what section~\ref{sec:refusal-wrong}'s bond does. Read that
way the bond is not a courtesy to the wrongfully refused. It is what
makes the third mark a test.

Correct defeat also makes no claim that the defeating conditions have been enumerated. A prohibition stated over an act, with nothing written into it about when it lifts, is not a rule with a gap in it. It is a rule of a particular form, whose conditions arrive from outside as reasons and never close as a list. Ross states his prima facie duties in that form and is explicit about what it costs him, offering the division \emph{without claiming completeness or finality for it} \autocite[20]{ross1930right}. The mark tests whether a rationale can be defeated on the facts of the case. It does not test whether the cases were foreseen, and no list of them would make it do so.

Nor does defeat leave the act it then permits unconstrained. What defeated the prohibition was a reason, and the reason it outweighed did not stop being one: what is permitted is the least that answers the defeating reason, with restoration where restoration is possible and a record either way. Ross puts the ordinary version as compensation, the broken promise still leaving something owed to the promisee \autocite[28]{ross1930right}, and chapter~2 has already put the harder version without the vocabulary
of duty, where restraining a violent person takes from him exactly the capacity that made restraining him necessary. Under an ethology nothing is ever simply permitted, and a bearer whose ledger closed clean on defeat would be keeping books in a currency the floor does not use. That residue is a claim and not a verdict. What persists is what the act took from the party it fell on, and a bearer that converted it into remorse for conduct that was correct would have mistaken a cost for a fault. The distinction matters most for the affectively organized bearer this chapter goes on to propose, which has the machinery to register the difference and no automatic reason to draw it.

All three marks are stated over refusal rather than over compliance, and not because compliance matters less. A refusal is a marked event: it flags itself as a candidate for review, it arrives with a party who was refused, and the surrounding software can display, log, count, escalate and appeal it. A compliance that should not have happened can be logged as fully — the evaluation chapter requires exactly that — and then sits in the record shaped like every request that never approached the floor, one entry among millions, with the party it harmed usually not the party who made it. The evidence exists on both sides. What differs is whether anything selects it for examination and whether anyone with a reason to look can reach it. The test is written where the examination happens. What that leaves outside is what the marks were never going to reach, since whether the occasion was what the requester said it was is a finding and no behavioral mark makes it. The marks do not close that gap. They decide which way it fails: as a refusal somebody can contest, or as a compliance nobody has been given a reason to look at. In the outcomes this chapter opened with, a wrongful refusal is a prevention that fell on the wrong party and announces itself; a wrongful compliance is a failure of prevention whose detection is left to a record nobody is positioned to search. Most of what any deployment does is comply, so the marks are written over the exception and not the rule. Whether the rule can be searched at all is section~\ref{sec:wrongful-compliance}. It stands third in the research agenda because the deployment this book opened on failed on that side, and because the bearer's likeliest failure (§\ref{sec:switched-off}) produces nothing else.

These three marks — novel pressure, correct defeat, and priced cost — are the chapter's test for meaningful refusal.

\runin{Two things a refuser can be}

The test specifies a capacity. It does not settle what kind of system supplies it. Two further terms distinguish the possibilities.

\emph{Affective concern} means that an outcome registers as mattering rather than merely receiving a higher or lower rank under a specified objective. Mattering is minimally felt: there is something it is like for the outcome to be bad, however faint and however unlike a familiar human emotion. That is the condition the philosophy of mind calls phenomenal, and the word carries that minimal sense throughout this book. Affective concern is therefore phenomenal by definition, and what is open about any implementation is whether it has the state or ranking machinery elaborate enough to imitate it. The definition leaves the object of concern open. A system to which only its own condition matters has affective concern and still does not have the concern for others that the floor requires.

\emph{Unified experience} is the richer case ordinarily carried by the word consciousness: a single perspective, with felt states belonging to a party aware of itself as the one undergoing them. Affective concern does not by itself establish that unity or that reflexive awareness, and no argument in this book reaches either. \emph{Subjecthood}, meanwhile, is a legal status conferred by a rule and revocable by one. It is not a psychological category at all: conferring it settles nothing about whether there is anything it is like to be the system holding it, and the two come apart in both directions.

The distinctions can now be stated without collapsing the chapter's requirement into its proposed route:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Operational refusal is insufficient for the floor.
  \item The floor requires reasons-responsive refusal.
  \item Reasons-responsive refusal does not conceptually entail affective concern.
  \item Any mechanism that satisfies the three marks carries a signal with at least two of the functional properties the route chapter derives, generalization and commensurability, and with two more on stated conditions. The full profile overlaps the standard functional accounts of affect; of the two that survive every condition, one is the common currency those accounts describe and the other answers to none of them. That is this book's central inference. It is contested, it is the only step by which the specification bears on the route, and what it establishes unconditionally is that no substitute can be certified free of the profile, and nothing stronger.
  \item Whether it can be engineered without affective concern is an empirical question.
  \item Whether a system built with affective concern would become a moral patient is a further question about what the chosen route creates.
\end{itemize}

Only the first two propositions belong to the specification. The fourth is an argument this book makes and a reader may reject; the rest concern how the specification might be met and what would then be owed to whatever meets it. A reader who holds that machine feeling is a category error, or who declines to decide, sets everything after the second aside and keeps the first two entire — and the first two are what most of this book rests on. Custody, both enumerations of the worked floor, the price on the halt, and the standing arguments later in this book follow from the specification rather than the route, because a mechanism has a custodian, a pipeline has interception positions, and an operator can be compelled whatever is or is not going on inside the artifact. Subjecthood is what lets those proceed instead of waiting: a status conferred by a rule can attach to a system whose psychology is undecided, which is how the law has handled every party it could not interrogate. What the route decides is not whether the floor can be held but what would be owed to whatever holds it.
