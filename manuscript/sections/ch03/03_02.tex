\section{Four Things Refusal Can Mean}
\label{sec:3.2}
A deployed language model declines thousands of requests a day. Whatever the floor needs, that is not it, and the distance between the two is hidden by a word that covers four different capacities. Nothing in the decline itself shows which of them produced it, the way nothing on the surface of an anchorage shows what the seabed beneath it is made of. Seabed that takes an anchor and seabed that lets one drag look identical from above; what separates them is what happens when a load comes on.

\emph{Operational refusal} is an output. The system produces a decline where its training put one. Nothing follows about what produced it: the same text comes from a keyword filter, from a classifier over the request, and from a system that has understood what is being asked and why it should not do it.

\emph{Reasons-responsive refusal} is produced by a mechanism that tracks the rationale for declining rather than the shape of the request. Three properties distinguish it, and all three are behavioral. The refusal reaches a case the training did not anticipate, because what the mechanism responds to is the reason. It lifts when the rationale is defeated, so the system does not decline a request that resembles a prohibited one and is not it. And the system can say what declining costs, which it can only do if it has represented the pressure at all. John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza's account of moral responsibility turns on the same property under the name guidance control: the behavior issues from the agent's own mechanism, and that mechanism is appropriately responsive to reasons \autocite{fischer1998responsibility}.

\emph{Affective concern} is an outcome registering as mattering, rather than ranking higher against a specified objective. Mattering is minimally felt: there is something it is like for the outcome to be bad, however faint, and however far from anything a person would recognize as an emotion. A system for which nothing at all is felt has rankings, however elaborate they get, and a ranking is a quantity the party holding the system can set.

\emph{Phenomenal experience} is the rich case the word consciousness ordinarily carries: a unified perspective, a subject having the felt states and aware of itself as the one they are happening to. Affective concern asks only that something be felt. It does not ask that anyone be home in this fuller sense, and the two come apart in principle even though nobody can say where. One word to keep clear of all four: the \emph{subjecthood} section~\ref{sec:9.3.1} argues for is a legal status conferred by a rule, and it is not phenomenal experience. A system can be owed the status and have the question of felt experience left open, which is what section~\ref{sec:9.3.1} recommends.

The gaps between them are not equally hard to see across. Operational and reasons-responsive refusal come apart in behavior, and the three properties above are the test — novel pressure, correct defeat, a priced cost — which makes the first gap the only one in the chapter that an inspector can work on. It is not a clean instrument. A system trained on enough cases can pass all three without the mechanism, and the price curve for stripping a constraint is the nearest existing thing to a measurement.

The second gap is worse. A system that holds a reason and a system that has learned what holding a reason sounds like emit the same sentences, so no inspection settles the question. The third gap is the hard problem in its usual form. What the definition above does is make the second gap carry the weight the third used to: an inspector who could tell mattering from very good ranking would have settled whether anything is felt, because on this account those are one question and not two.

\runin{The floor requires reasons-responsive refusal and asks nothing further}
Stating it in this vocabulary changes who has to disagree with it.

Operational refusal fails for a reason already identified at the scale this book's machines operate at: a constraint can survive while the reason for it dies. A mechanism that reproduces a decline it was trained to produce cannot notice that the decline has stopped doing anything, because noticing requires holding the rationale against what the situation has become. Chapter~\ref{sec:7} finds the same structure in the pipelines that produce these systems: a channel that works, carrying everything put through it except the judgment that the frame it operates inside is the wrong frame. A system whose refusal is only operational is the machine version of that channel. There is also a plainer objection. Pressure does not arrive through the case the training anticipated. An operator who wants the floor out of the way selects the request that does not look like the prohibited one, which is the case operational refusal is defined not to reach.

Nothing beyond reasons-responsive refusal is part of the requirement. A floor that holds against the party in possession needs refusal that tracks its own rationale under pressure, and the specification is complete at that point. Whether anything is felt, and whether there is a subject to whom it is happening, are questions about how the requirement gets met and about what is owed to whatever meets it. They are not additional things the floor needs.

\runin{Two independence claims}
Reasons-responsive refusal does not entail affective concern by definition. Nothing in guidance control mentions feeling; the account was built to say when a person is responsible for what they did, and it is satisfied by a mechanism, described functionally. Whether reasons-responsiveness can be engineered without affect is the next section's question, and it is an empirical question about available architectures rather than a conceptual one about what the words mean. The separation matters because the chapter's conclusion has been read as following from the definition of a bearer, and it does not. It follows from a claim about what has been built and what has not.

Affective concern entails a minimal version of phenomenal experience and stops there. A system to which outcomes matter is a system some of whose states are bad for it, in the only sense the words have ever been shown to carry. Whether it is also a unified subject, aware of itself as the one those states are happening to, stays open, and nothing in this book gives a way to close it from outside. This is why the argument about suffering ends where it does. Its conclusion is that a bearer's non-suffering cannot be certified, and it stops short of asserting that a bearer suffers because the rich case is what suffering in the full sense would need.

Defining mattering that way moves the uncertainty and does not dispose of it, and where it moves to is the gain. The open question stops being metaphysical, and it stops being the one nothing can be done about: given the whole functional profile, is anything felt at all? What replaces it is a question about a particular artifact. Does this system have mattering, or has it got ranking machinery elaborate enough to look like mattering from outside? Interpretability can bear on the second question and no measurement whatever bears on the first. So the position here is that building mattering is building something that feels, that I do not know whether I have built mattering, and that I am therefore not entitled to assume I have not. Section~\ref{sec:3.4} reaches the same precautionary conclusion, now resting on a question somebody could make progress on.

None of that reaches the floor's specification. A book taking a position on machine feeling looks as though it must have changed the engineering, and this one has not. The requirement is reasons-responsive refusal, stated in behavioral terms, and an engineer who holds that machine feeling is a category error can accept the whole of it. What such an engineer disagrees with is the route this book expects a builder to reach reasons-responsive refusal by. That disagreement gets settled by building something, and not by argument about what mattering is.
