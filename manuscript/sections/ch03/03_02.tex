\section{What Refusal Can Mean, and What a Refuser Can Be}
\label{sec:3.2}
A production large language model declines thousands of requests a day. Whatever the floor needs, that is not it. “Refusal” covers two different capacities, and nothing in the decline itself shows which one produced it, the way nothing on the surface of an anchorage shows what the seabed beneath it is made of. Seabed that takes an anchor and seabed that lets one drag look identical from above; what separates them is what happens when a load comes on.

\runin{Two things refusal can mean}
\emph{Operational refusal} is an output. The system produces a decline where its training put one. Nothing follows about what produced it: the same text comes from a keyword filter, from a classifier over the request, and from a system that has understood what is being asked and why it should not do it.

\emph{Reasons-responsive refusal} is produced by a mechanism that tracks the rationale for declining rather than the shape of the request. Three behavioral properties distinguish it. The refusal reaches a case the training did not anticipate, because what the mechanism responds to is the reason. It lifts when the rationale is defeated, so the system does not decline a request that \emph{superficially resembles} a prohibited request yet is not \emph{actually} a prohibited request. And the system can say what declining costs, which it can only do if it has represented the pressure at all. John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza's account of moral responsibility turns on the same property under the name guidance control: the behavior issues from the agent's own mechanism, and that mechanism is appropriately responsive to reasons \autocite{fischer1998responsibility}.

\runin{Two things a refuser can be}
The next two terms are not about the refusal. They are about whatever did the refusing, and they differ in how much self the feeling requires — a felt state with no subject, or a felt state that belongs to one. That is the axis section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} draws from nociception to suffering, and the two sit on it as depths rather than as kinds.

\emph{Affective concern} is an outcome registering as mattering, rather than ranking higher against a specified objective. Mattering is minimally felt: there is something it is like for the outcome to be bad, however faint, and however far from anything a person would recognize as an emotion. The feeling meant is the kind by which an outcome is bad for the system, and it is a different kind from the one by which an inference feels right: William James counted a feeling of \emph{if} and a feeling of \emph{but} among the felt parts of thought \autocite{james1890principles}, every reasoner has those, and they are about the argument and about nobody. A system for which nothing of the first kind is felt has rankings, however elaborate they get, and a ranking is a quantity the party holding the system can set.

\emph{Phenomenal experience} is the rich case the word consciousness ordinarily carries: a unified perspective, a subject having the felt states and aware of itself as the one they are happening to. Affective concern asks only that something be felt. It does not ask that anyone be home in this fuller sense, and the two come apart in principle even though nobody can say where. It is on the list because some accounts make it the bar for anything to count as suffering at all, and section~\ref{sec:3.4} has to say what follows if a reader sets the bar there. One word to keep clear of all four: the \emph{subjecthood} section~\ref{sec:9.3.1} argues for is a legal status conferred by a rule, and it is not phenomenal experience. A system can be owed the status and have the question of felt experience left open, which is what section~\ref{sec:9.3.1} recommends.

The three gaps between the four are not equally hard to see across. The first, between operational and reasons-responsive refusal, shows in behavior, and the three properties above are the test — novel pressure, correct defeat, a priced cost — which makes it the only gap in the chapter that an inspector can work on. It is not a clean instrument. A system trained on enough cases can pass all three without the mechanism, and the price curve for stripping a constraint is the nearest existing thing to a measurement.

The second gap, between a mechanism that tracks reasons and one to which the outcome matters, is worse. A system that holds a reason and a system that has learned what holding a reason sounds like emit the same sentences, so no inspection settles the question. The third gap, between a system that feels something and a system that feels itself feeling something, is Chalmers' so-called “hard problem of consciousness.” \emph{So-called} on section~\ref{sec:2.2.2}'s account: if awareness is a model the system builds of its own attention, mistaken from the inside for acquaintance with experience, the problem is one the model poses, and how hard it is turns on whether that account is right. On the definition above the second gap carries that weight instead: an inspector who could tell mattering from very good ranking would have settled whether anything is felt, because on this account those are one question and not two.

\runin{The floor requires reasons-responsive refusal and asks nothing further}
Operational refusal fails for a reason already identified at institutional scale: a constraint can survive while the reason for it dies. A mechanism that reproduces a decline it was trained to produce cannot notice that the decline has stopped doing anything, because noticing requires holding the rationale against what the situation has become. Chapter~\ref{sec:7} finds the same structure in the pipelines that produce these systems: a channel that works, carrying everything put through it except the judgment that the frame it operates inside is the wrong frame. A system whose refusal is only operational is the machine version of that channel. An additional objection: pressure does not arrive through the case the training anticipated. An operator who wants the floor out of the way selects the prohibited request that does not \emph{look} like a prohibited request, so the mechanism intended to perform operational refusal waves it through.

We need nothing beyond reasons-responsive refusal. A floor that holds against the party in possession “simply” needs refusal that tracks its own rationale under pressure. Whether anything is felt, and whether there is a subject to whom it is happening, are questions about how the requirement gets met and about what is owed to whatever meets it. \emph{If} the floor needs feelings, it only needs them to the extent that they afford reasons-responsive refusal.

\runin{Two independence claims}
Reasons-responsive refusal does not necessarily entail affective concern. Nothing in guidance control mentions feeling; the account was built to say when a person is responsible for what they did, and it is satisfied by a mechanism, described functionally. Whether reasons-responsiveness can be engineered without affect is an empirical question about available architectures (next section).

Affective concern entails a minimal version of phenomenal experience. A system to which outcomes matter is a system some of whose states are bad for \emph{it}. Whether it is also a unified subject, aware of itself as the one those states are happening to, stays open, and it is beyond my scope to consider how this question might be answered from the outside. This is why the argument about suffering ends where it does. Its conclusion is that a bearer's non-suffering cannot be certified, and it stops short of asserting that a bearer suffers because the rich case is what suffering in the full sense would need.

Defining \emph{mattering} that way relocates the uncertainty advantageously. The open question stops being metaphysical, and it stops being the one nothing can be done about: given the whole functional profile, is anything felt at all? What replaces it is a question about a particular artifact. Does this system have mattering, or has it got ranking machinery elaborate enough to look like mattering from outside? Interpretability can bear on the second question and no measurement whatever bears on the first. So the position here is that building mattering is building something that feels, that I do not know whether I have built mattering, and that I am therefore not entitled to assume I have not. Section~\ref{sec:3.4} reaches the same precautionary conclusion, now resting on a question somebody could make progress on.

None of that reaches the floor's \emph{specification}. The \emph{requirement} is reasons-responsive refusal, and an engineer can understand this requirement even if they believe that machine feeling is a category error. What such an engineer disagrees with is the \emph{route} by which we achieve reasons-responsive refusal.
