3.1.2.3.1.4. Reasoning and Problem Solving
Reasoning and problem-solving comprise critical cognitive operations that allow humans and artificial intelligences (AIs) to deconstruct intricate situations, infer valid conclusions, and formulate suitable solutions. For the construction of ethical, altruistic, and anti-authoritarian AI systems capable of handling unique moral dilemmas and adaptive decision-making scenarios, a comprehensive understanding and effective incorporation of these processes is indispensable. Exploring areas such as the dichotomy between intuitive and deliberative thinking, the mystery of creative problem-solving, the revelations of metacognition, and the neurobiological mechanisms underpinning these human cognitive processes yields invaluable insights.

At the helm of human reasoning and problem-solving processes is the prefrontal cortex (PFC), particularly its subregions, which correspond with specific elements within these cognitive procedures. For example, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) governs working memory and abstract reasoning. In contrast, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC) handles the processing of emotional and social data, while the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) plays a role in rewarding and corrective decision-making. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) contributes to cognitive flexibility, conflict management, and error detection. A clear comprehension of these diverse regions' complex interactions can lend profound insights into the neurobiological processes that drive human reasoning and problem-solving.

We can graft these reasoning capabilities onto AI systems employing an array of methods, including logical, probabilistic, and analogical reasoning. Each mirrors different aspects of human cognitive processes and presents unique strengths and challenges in the context of ethical AI and anti-authoritarian decision-making.

Logical reasoning utilizes the principles of logic and deduction to derive conclusions from predefined premises. AI systems can use formal methods such as theorem proving to extract results congruous with ethical doctrines. However, practical ethical situations often present incomplete or inconsistent data, posing challenges to logical reasoning.

Understanding the DLPFC's role in human-based deliberative thinking can inspire AI systems to make more effective decisions under limited information conditions. The DLPFC orchestrates essential elements of cognitive processing such as working memory, planning, decision-making, and cognitive flexibility. We can leverage these insights to design AI systems armed with the following strategies:

(1) Working Memory: Temporarily store and process relevant information.
(2) Cognitive Flexibility: Switch between tasks and adjust strategies based on incoming information.
(3) Goal-Directed Behaviour: Establish explicit goals and develop strategies to accomplish them, even in situations with incomplete or ambiguous information.
(4) Evaluation and Comparison: Weigh the advantages and disadvantages of different options.
(5) Impulse Control: Resist impulsive responses to enable more deliberate decision-making.

Familiarizing ourselves with the operating principles of human-based probabilistic reasoning can lead to the development of AI solutions that utilize this form of processing. Probabilistic reasoning equips AI systems with the capability to handle uncertain scenarios by assigning different probability values to possible outcomes, thus allowing data-efficient and informed decision-making. Techniques such as Bayesian networks and Markov decision processes exemplify this approach. However, developers must remain cautious of potential biases that may arise from inappropriate assumptions and inaccurate data.

Analogical reasoning grants AI systems the capacity to draw inferences based on similar situations or concepts, vital for addressing unprecedented problems where previous experiences or knowledge bases can provide valuable insights. This form of cognition can lead to errors with superficial similarities between cases and could result in ethically unsound conclusions. Gaining insights from the study of human neurobiology associated with creative problem-solving can enable AI systems to create innovative solutions to complex ethical conundrums.

Neurological correlates, including the Default Mode Network (DMN), Executive Control Network (ECN), Salience Network (SN), Prefrontal Cortex (PFC), and Hippocampus, all contribute to creativity in human reasoning. Understanding these correlates and integrating into AI models for deliberative thinking, cognitive flexibility, divergent and convergent thinking, and dynamic switching between exploration and exploitation may pave the way for AI models capable of not just solving complex problems but innovating solutions. In conclusion, understanding the neurobiological constructs of problem-solving and reasoning paves the way for robust, adaptive, ethical, and anti-authoritarian AI systems.
