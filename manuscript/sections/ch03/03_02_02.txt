3.2.2. Inverse Reinforcement Learning and Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning in Altruistic AI
Reinforcement learning trains an agent to make decisions by tying its actions to rewards and punishments. Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) runs this the other way: instead of taking a reward function as given, it infers the reward function that best explains a demonstrator's observed behavior.

That matters for altruistic AI because it lets a system infer human values and preferences from what people actually do, rather than from an explicit specification that is unlikely to capture them fully.

An autonomous vehicle using IRL to learn from a human driver observes speed choices, following distance, and reactions to changing road conditions, and infers the principles behind them — shifting its own driving toward human norms rather than a fixed rulebook.

Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (CIRL), formalized by Dylan Hadfield-Menell and colleagues [[cite:C0030]], extends IRL into a two-way process. Rather than assuming its own reward signal is complete, the AI treats the human's true reward as unknown and to be inferred jointly, through interaction — closer to an apprentice reading a mentor's intentions than an agent executing a fixed reward function.

A domestic robot helping with cooking or cleaning can use CIRL to treat a household's daily interactions as ongoing evidence about what its resident actually wants, adjusting its help to that person's preferences rather than to a generic standard.

Both methods depend on the quality, diversity, and representativeness of the demonstrations they learn from. Training data drawn from too narrow a set of situations and people will not generalize, and unexamined bias in that data gets learned and reproduced rather than corrected — the opposite of what altruistic AI is meant to do.
