3.1.2. Biologically-Inspired AI: Gleanings from Neuroscience and Cognitive Psychology
Backpropagation, the mechanism that trains most modern neural networks, is a distant cousin of synaptic weight adjustment in the brain: both strengthen and weaken connections in response to error. That distant-cousin relationship — real but partial, useful but easy to overstate — runs through everything below, from attention and memory to emotion and plasticity. Getting it right matters for more than accuracy: an AI system built on a wrong model of how a mind works will fail in ways that are hard to predict from the wrong model alone.

Artificial neural networks model interconnected layers of artificial neurons, mimicking biological neural networks in rough outline while remaining, in depth, dynamics, and connectivity, far simpler than the brain they are named after. That gap between metaphor and mechanism is the throughline of what follows.

<<h>>Neural Networks and Brain-Inspired AI Models<</h>>
Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are the foundation of most contemporary AI systems: artificial neurons connected in layers — input, hidden, output — with a weight on each connection, adjusted during training by backpropagation to reduce the gap between predicted and actual output.

Deep learning applies this at scale, stacking many layers to extract higher-level abstractions from raw data. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), tuned for grid-structured data like images, borrow the visual cortex's layered approach: pooling, shared weights, and local receptive fields let a CNN decompose an image into hierarchical features, the way AlphaGo's CNN-based evaluation of the Go board did in besting the reigning world champion. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) handle sequential data — language, time series — through a hidden state that carries information forward; long short-term memory (LSTM) networks, a variant built to survive the vanishing-gradient problem that plagues plain RNNs, extend that to longer sequences, in machine translation, speech recognition, and sentiment analysis. Spiking neural networks and neuromorphic computing push further toward the brain's actual signaling mechanism, in pursuit of the energy efficiency that comes from firing only when there is something to say.

A mirror-neuron-style matching mechanism was once treated as the biological basis of empathy — a system that fires both when an animal acts and when it watches the same act performed. Section 2.2.1 takes up why that claim does not hold: the dissociation between recognizing an action and feeling with the person performing it is exactly what a pure matching mechanism cannot explain. What survives the correction is more modest and still useful: a system that maps an observed action onto its own action repertoire supports imitation learning and gesture recognition, independent of any claim about what it feels like to have one.

<<h>>Hierarchical Processing and the Visual Cortex<</h>>
The visual cortex processes in stages. The primary visual cortex (V1) extracts edges, orientation, and contrast from raw input; that signal escalates through secondary and tertiary areas (V2, V3, and beyond), which build toward object recognition and motion. CNNs borrow the structure directly, layering simple-feature detection into complex-feature detection, which is why they perform well at image classification, object detection, and scene interpretation.

The flow is not one-directional. Feedback connections carry information back down the hierarchy, letting higher-level context reshape how lower-level signal gets interpreted — the visual system generating a prediction and revising it, not just accumulating features bottom-up. An AI system built only for the feedforward pass gives up exactly the mechanism that lets the human visual system handle ambiguous or noisy input; the section on Perception below takes up what a model built around that revision loop looks like.

Selective attention does the same efficiency work here that it does throughout cognition: prioritizing some regions of the input over others rather than processing all of it uniformly. Autonomous vehicles, medical-imaging diagnosis, and machine translation each lean on some version of the same move — spend computation where it is likely to pay off, not everywhere at once.

<<h>>Cognitive and Emotional Processes<</h>>
Human cognition — attention, perception, memory, reasoning, problem-solving, metacognition — and human emotion are not separate systems bolted together. They run in tandem: emotion shapes what gets attended to, remembered, and decided, and cognition shapes how an emotional state gets interpreted and acted on. An AI system that only implements the cognitive side is building half a mind; the sections below take cognition first, then emotion, then what happens when they have to work together.

<<h>>Attention<</h>>
In 1999 two psychologists made a short video of two teams passing basketballs, one team in white shirts and one in black, and asked people to count the passes made by the white team. Halfway through, a person in a full gorilla costume walked into the center of the frame, faced the camera, thumped their chest, and walked out. The gorilla was on screen for nine seconds. About half the viewers, asked afterward whether they had seen anything unusual, said no. Shown the same video again, they were astonished [[cite:C0269]].

The gorilla was not a visual trick. It was lit, centered, and in plain view. The viewers' eyes registered the light coming from it; their attention was occupied counting passes, and the gorilla fell outside what that attention was holding. This is inattentional blindness: the failure to consciously perceive a fully visible stimulus because attention was directed elsewhere. The effect is not a curiosity of the laboratory. Radiologists searching lung images for nodules miss a gorilla deliberately embedded in the scan at a rate of 83 percent [[cite:C0270]].

A related body of work on change blindness shows the same architecture from another angle. People giving directions to a stranger frequently fail to notice when the stranger is swapped for a different person during a brief interruption, and continue the conversation without registering the substitution [[cite:C0271]]. Film editors learned the same lesson empirically, hiding changes of prop and lighting across cuts by timing them to moments when the audience's attention is occupied.

What we experience is not the scene in front of us. It is the narrow fraction of that scene which attention has selected and amplified, surrounded by a low-resolution, expectation-filled remainder, and the impression of complete coverage is assembled after the fact by inference. Attention is therefore not a filter placed after perception. It is the selection that decides what gets perceived at all.

The visual system did not evolve to record the world; it evolved to support action in it, and for that purpose the unattended was usually irrelevant. The system is not failing when it misses the gorilla. The mismatch is between what it was built for and what is now asked of it, in settings where the unattended thing is a car entering an intersection or an alarm in a hospital ward.

The implication for machine perception? If attention is a resource allocator bolted onto a system that has already seen everything, its failures are inefficiencies. If attention is what determines what the system sees, its failures are blind spots — and a system that has learned which regions of its input reward processing has also learned which regions to be blind to. The human case shows that such blind spots are invisible from the inside: the viewers who missed the gorilla did not experience a gap.

Human attention is conventionally divided into two modes. Goal-directed attention is voluntary, guided by task demands, and associated with the fronto-parietal network, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the posterior parietal cortex. Stimulus-driven attention is involuntary, triggered by novel or salient events, and associated with a ventral network including the temporoparietal junction and the ventral frontal cortex. Machine systems implement recognizable analogues of both: attention weights conditioned on task embeddings on the one hand, saliency models keyed to contrast and novelty on the other.

The self-attention mechanisms in transformer architectures [[cite:C0272]] illustrate the point about blind spots. A model that weights some tokens heavily is a model that weights others near zero, and nothing in the architecture announces which relevant thing was assigned a low weight.

Attention schema theory extends this further, holding that a system which models its own attention — a schematic, simplified internal representation of what it is attending to and what it will attend to next — thereby acquires the materials for a form of self-awareness [[cite:C0273]]. What the schema tracks is not the world but the system's own relation to it. Section 2.3.3 expounds on this point in discussing machine self-models, where a model of the self has the simplifications and failure modes definitional to any model.

For a system intended to act ethically, the design question is how it will notice that its own attention has a shape, and treat the regions it has learned to discount as an always-open question rather than that which, as Westworld's Hosts often remarked, "doesn't look like anything to me".

<<h>>Perception<</h>>
Perception is not the passive registration of a scene; it is inference. Cognitive neuroscience's account of how that inference works — predictive coding — makes this precise: rather than reading a raw signal upward through a hierarchy of feature detectors, the visual cortex sends predictions down, from higher, context-rich areas to lower, sensory ones, and the lower areas send back only what those predictions failed to anticipate — the prediction error [[cite:C0284]]. What propagates up the hierarchy is not the picture but the surprise, the part the model got wrong. Anatomy fits the theory: backward connections outnumber forward ones and carry the predictions; forward connections carry the errors.

The neuroscientist Anil Seth's phrase for the result is controlled hallucination [[cite:C0285]]: ordinary perception is what hallucination would be if sensory data were allowed to constrain it, and the only difference between seeing a room and hallucinating one is whether corrections are arriving and being applied. In psychosis, that correction weakens — priors dominate, sensory error is discounted, and the brain's model of reality drifts from what the world is sending. The hollow-mask illusion — nearly everyone briefly perceives a concave mask as a normal convex face, because the prior "faces are convex" outweighs the sensory evidence — is a laboratory-scale version of the same mechanism operating on people with no clinical condition at all.

CNNs already implement a rough version of the hierarchy; predictive coding says what the next step should look like: a model that generates a prediction and asks only what violates it, rather than one that reprocesses the entire input at every layer regardless of how much of it was already expected. Variational autoencoders and generative adversarial networks, which learn to generate plausible data and score inputs against that model, are the closest existing machine analogue. Treating this as the premise of AI perception, rather than a speculative "next frontier," changes what counts as progress: not more layers, but a better-calibrated set of expectations and a cheaper way of finding where they broke.

The stakes are not narrowly technical. A system's perception, human or machine, is only as reliable as the priors it perceives through, and priors trained on skewed or incomplete data will hallucinate in the specific direction of that skew — confidently, and without any internal signal that anything is wrong.

<<h>>Memory<</h>>
Memory is not played back. It is rebuilt. The first person to demonstrate this rigorously was the psychologist Frederic Bartlett, who in the 1930s had people read an unfamiliar folk tale and retell it repeatedly over days and weeks. They did not lose pieces the way a recording degrades; they remodeled the story, replacing strange details with ordinary ones and drifting toward a tidier version that fit their own expectations [[cite:C0286]]. Bartlett called the expectation-shaped template doing the filling a schema, and the finding has never been overturned: remembering is construction, and construction runs on assumption.

Elizabeth Loftus turned this from a laboratory curiosity into something with courtroom consequences. Showing people a film of a car accident and asking how fast the cars were going when they "smashed" versus "hit" produced different speed estimates — and a week later, people in the "smashed" condition were more likely to misremember broken glass that was never in the film [[cite:C0287]]. Later work implanted entire false memories — being lost in a shopping mall as a child, meeting a costumed character a venue never employed — simply by suggesting the episode confidently and letting reconstruction do the rest. The subjects were not lying; they remembered the invented event, often vividly.

None of this correlates with felt confidence. A memory's vividness, richness, and the conviction that accompanies it are no guarantee of accuracy — a detailed, emotional memory can be substantially manufactured, because a system optimized to act sensibly on the past has little reason to preserve it with a court reporter's fidelity, and human memory does not.

An AI memory system built on "storage and retrieval" inherits the hard-drive intuition this evidence refutes. Working memory is not a buffer that holds a fixed amount and then overflows; the number most people half-remember from an introductory course — seven items, plus or minus two, from a 1956 study — was already a measure of chunked information, where a phone number becomes three chunks rather than ten digits and a chess master reads a whole position as a handful of familiar patterns [[cite:C0288]]. When chunking is controlled for — no language, no expertise to pack information into larger units — the real capacity narrows to about four items, plus or minus one [[cite:C0289]]. The two numbers are not in conflict; seven is what expertise buys you, four is the bare attentional floor underneath it. Four is the number that should inform how an AI system's working-memory analogue is sized and what "overload" means for it, not seven.

Long-term memory in an AI system can still draw usefully on the standard divisions — semantic memory as a knowledge graph, procedural memory as a policy learned through reinforcement or imitation, episodic memory as retrievable context tied to specific past interactions — but the retrieval itself should be built expecting reconstruction, not lookup: a system that treats a retrieved memory as ground truth inherits the same overconfidence Loftus's subjects showed, with none of the excuse of having a body that reconstructs by default rather than by design.

Consider a customer-service AI system with an advanced memory mechanism: it can recall details from past interactions to give a personalized, contextually fitting response. In healthcare, the same mechanism applied to a patient's history, treatments, and preferences supports more informed and better-targeted care. Both cases need memory that is retrieval-appropriate, not memory the system mistakes for an unedited record of what happened.

<<h>>Reasoning and Problem Solving<</h>>
Reasoning and problem-solving let a system break down a complex situation, draw valid conclusions, and act on them — indispensable for handling moral dilemmas and adaptive decision-making. The prefrontal cortex (PFC) does much of this work in humans: the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) is associated with working memory and abstract reasoning, the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (VMPFC) with emotional and social processing, the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) with reward-based and corrective decision-making, and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) with cognitive flexibility, conflict monitoring, and error detection.

It is tempting to read that division of labor as a committee: five named regions, each handling one job, together adding up to executive control. That reading has a name — the homunculus — and a specific failure mode. If the DLPFC is a controller that decides what to attend to, inhibit, and hold in mind, then intelligent coordination has been explained by positing an intelligent coordinator inside the system, which explains nothing: the controller would itself need to attend, decide, and remember, which means it would need its own controller, and so on without end.

The neuroscientists Earl Miller and Jonathan Cohen proposed a model that gets control without a controller [[cite:C0290]]. The prefrontal cortex does not house an agent issuing commands; it maintains representations of goals — sustained patterns of activity encoding the rule currently in force — and those representations work not by commanding but by biasing the rest of the brain, the same way attention tips a competition among neural representations toward one interpretation over another. Control is that same biasing mechanism, turned to the service of a goal. In the Stroop task — naming the ink color of a word like "red" printed in blue, where the practiced habit of reading fights the instructed task — there is no homunculus reading the word and overriding it. There is a goal, held active, lending its weight to the weaker pathway until it wins.

An AI system built on this model does not need a dedicated "executive module" enumerating working memory, cognitive flexibility, goal-directed behavior, evaluation, and impulse control as five separate capacities to install. It needs a goal representation with the standing to bias competing processes, which is a narrower and more tractable engineering target than a controller that does all five jobs itself.

Beyond the executive question, an AI system can draw on logical, probabilistic, and analogical reasoning, each with its own strengths and failure modes. Logical reasoning, via formal methods like theorem proving, works cleanly on complete and consistent premises but strains against the incomplete, contradictory data real ethical situations present. Probabilistic reasoning — Bayesian networks, Markov decision processes — handles uncertainty by assigning probabilities to outcomes, at the cost of being only as good as the assumptions and data behind those probabilities. Analogical reasoning lets a system draw on precedent for novel problems, at the risk of matching on superficial similarity and reaching an ethically unsound conclusion by a route that looked reasonable at each step.

<<h>>Metacognition<</h>>
Metacognition — a system's awareness of and control over its own cognitive processes — sits in the same anterior cingulate cortex machinery already discussed above: error detection (noticing the gap between expected and actual outcomes), conflict monitoring (resolving competing responses), and evaluation (weighing costs and benefits of a course of action).

An AI system with an analogous capacity gains a route to catching its own errors, resolving competing outputs, and adjusting when the situation has changed — properties that matter more, not less, as the ethical framework or context the system operates under shifts. But metacognition is not free: it adds computational overhead and design complexity, and a system with an inflated model of its own reliability is a system with a specific, dangerous failure mode — confident about exactly the judgments it should be least sure of. A metacognitive capacity is worth building only if it is calibrated to detect that failure, not just to produce fluent self-reports.

<<h>>Emotional Recognition and Perception<</h>>
An AI system that can identify human emotion — from facial expression, tone of voice, text, or physiological signal — has a foundation for responding appropriately to it. Two approaches dominate: training on labeled datasets of human expression, and drawing on the neuroscience of how the brain itself appraises and represents emotion.

<<list>>
- Emotional appraisal: the amygdala takes part in assessing a stimulus's emotional relevance, valence, and significance, though describing it as predominantly responsible overstates a single-region story that current evidence does not support — the same regions that respond to fear also respond to novelty, surprise, and salience of many other kinds, in shifting combinations rather than one dedicated circuit per emotion.
- Emotional expression: the facial feedback hypothesis holds that expressing an emotion — through face, posture, or voice — feeds back into the emotional experience itself, not just outward.
- Emotional perception: recognizing others' emotional expressions draws on the fusiform face area and superior temporal sulcus, and is a skill built through development rather than present from the start.
- Emotional regulation: the prefrontal cortex, particularly its ventromedial and dorsolateral regions, moderates emotional reactions, adds context, and enforces situational and social norms.
- Physiological response: heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration shift with emotional state, coordinated by the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems.
- Subjective experience: what an emotion feels like arises from integrating sensory, cognitive, and interoceptive information into a single experience — a process associated with the insular cortex and anterior cingulate cortex, again better described as heavily involved than as sole owners of the process.
- Cultural context: what counts as an appropriate emotional display, and how a given expression is read, varies across cultures.
<</list>>

Not every one of these needs a machine equivalent to build a useful system — an AI system may not need embodied physiological response to recognize distress from a voice — but each omission is a design choice with consequences.

<<h>>Emotional Mimicry and Empathy<</h>>
Emotional mimicry — reflecting a perceived emotion back, in humans usually unconsciously, through mirrored expression, tone, or posture — is one mechanism for building rapport between a human and an AI system: recognize the emotion, then produce a response that appears to be in synchrony with it. Chapter 2 takes up why the human capacity this borrows from, empathy, is a narrower and more failure-prone target than it looks — parochial, subject to burnout, and separable from the compassion that actually motivates sustained helping. An AI system engineered to mimic emotional synchrony inherits none of empathy's biological costs, but it also inherits none of its grounding: mimicry without the underlying capacity risks reading as manipulation rather than rapport the moment a user notices the gap.

<<h>>Emotional Regulation<</h>>
An AI system interacting with someone in a heightened emotional state — a patient anxious about a diagnosis, a user frustrated with a failed task — needs its own version of emotion regulation: monitoring, interpreting, and moderating its responses so they suit the context rather than amplifying the user's distress. Four things that regulation needs to do:

<<list>>
- Recognize accurately, using more than text: facial cues, vocal tone, and physiological signal where available, not linguistic analysis alone.
- Express a convincing range of response, whether through text, synthesized speech, or an animated interface.
- Adjust to context, calibrating its own emotional register to the situation, the user's state, and the goal of the interaction — which requires something like self-monitoring of its own output.
- Stay accountable, since an AI system with the capacity to modulate a user's emotional state also has the capacity to manipulate it, and the line between the two is a matter of transparency and consent, not technical sophistication.
<</list>>

<<h>>Emotion and Decision-Making<</h>>
Emotion shapes human judgment, choice, and action; a decision-making AI system that ignores it is not being more rational, only blinder to a real input. Gathering and weighing emotionally relevant information lets a system assess not just the logical outcome of an action but its emotional consequences for the people affected by it.

A medical AI system recommending treatment can weigh a patient's and their family's emotional welfare alongside clinical efficacy and risk, rather than treating those as separate from the "real" decision. A negotiation or conflict-resolution AI system that reads emotional cues from the parties involved can propose resolutions that address their emotional needs as well as their stated positions, which tends to produce agreements that hold.

The obstacles are real. Emotion is complex and context-dependent, shaped by culture and individual history in ways a system has to track rather than assume away. It is also often ambiguous — people understate, mask, and misread their own emotional states, which means a system relying on indirect cues like tone or facial expression is working with signal that is unreliable even at the human end. And access to emotional information carries its own ethical weight: privacy, informed consent, and the risk that emotional sensitivity becomes emotional manipulation the moment it serves the system's goals rather than the user's.

<<h>>Emotion-Driven Learning and Adaptation<</h>>
An AI system can use emotional signal as a learning input in several ways: treating emotional feedback from a human as a training signal, the way a tutoring system adjusts its approach on detecting a student's confusion or frustration; prioritizing emotionally salient experiences for learning, the way a therapy-support system might weight a charged exchange more heavily than a routine one; simulating another party's likely emotional response to plan proactively, as a mediating system might in modeling how a proposal will land; and using emotional indicators directly as reward or penalty signals in reinforcement learning. Each is a route to a system that adapts to human norms and values through feedback rather than specification alone — and each is also a route to a system optimizing for the appearance of an emotion the training signal rewards.

<<h>>Applications<</h>>
Several deployed systems already put emotional recognition to work. Woebot, a cognitive-behavioral-therapy chatbot, interprets a user's emotional state from text and adjusts its responses accordingly [[cite:C0291]]. AutoTutor combines natural-language processing and affective computing to detect and respond to a student's frustration, confusion, or boredom [[cite:C0292]]. Paro, a robot built to resemble a baby harp seal, responds to touch, voice, and light with behavior intended to provide companionship to people with dementia [[cite:C0293]]. Replika generates conversationally appropriate, apparently empathetic responses as a companion chatbot [[cite:C0294]]. Affectiva analyzes facial expression in real time to infer emotional reaction to content [[cite:C0295]].

These span mental health support, education, companionship, and content personalization — different domains united by the same underlying move: infer emotional state from available signal, then shape a response to it. The same move applies, less benignly, to political speech and disinformation, where an AI system built to detect emotionally manipulative rhetoric is using the identical technical capacity that, pointed the other way, would optimize for it.

<<h>>Challenges<</h>>
Building any of this reliably runs into the same handful of obstacles regardless of application. Emotion recognition from multimodal, ambiguous data — a smile that might be happiness, politeness, or irony — depends on context, personal history, and cultural norms that a system has to infer rather than read off the signal directly. Emotional expression has its own trap: humanoid systems tuned to look and act nearly, but not quite, human trigger the discomfort known as the uncanny valley, which means expressive fidelity has to be calibrated rather than simply maximized. Balancing cognitive and emotional response is a design problem in its own right — a diagnostic system needs an accurate, data-grounded prognosis and, separately, a register for delivering it that does not needlessly compound a patient's distress. And every one of these capacities raises the same governance question: a system built to interact responsibly and empathetically is also, mechanically, a system built to know what will move a person, which is the same capability an exploitative system would want.

<<h>>Learning and Plasticity<</h>>
Learning and plasticity — the capacity to change, grow, and adapt — are as fundamental to an ethical, empathetic AI system as any single cognitive process discussed above, because a system that cannot update itself cannot stay aligned with values that themselves develop over time.

<<h>>The Role of Plasticity in Intelligence<</h>>
Neuroplasticity is the brain's capacity to reorganize its own connections in response to experience, and it is the basis of biological learning and resilience. An AI system with an analogous capacity would need to adapt its architecture, not just its parameters, to new information: forming and pruning connections structurally, modulating the pace of learning to the complexity of the situation, adjusting its strategies to context, and learning socially — from observing others, sharing experience, and solving problems cooperatively rather than in isolation.

<<h>>Bio-Inspired Learning Mechanisms<</h>>
Three biological principles offer the clearest template for building this into an AI system. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity strengthens or weakens a synaptic connection based on the precise timing of firing between two neurons, anchoring learning to temporal structure — useful wherever an AI system needs to recognize patterns that unfold over time, such as speech. Hebbian learning — "neurons that fire together, wire together" — strengthens connections between co-active units, building an associative structure that links related concepts; the Hopfield network is the clearest AI instance, using Hebbian-style connections for associative memory recall. Homeostatic plasticity keeps a network's overall activity within a stable range, preventing the runaway synchrony or catastrophic forgetting that an unconstrained learning system is prone to; Adaptive Resonance Theory (ART) networks apply the same principle, maintaining stable learned patterns while still assimilating new and even contradictory information.

<<h>>Lifelong Learning in AI Systems<</h>>
An AI system aiming at ethical behavior over time needs to keep learning throughout its operating life, not just during training, refining its choices as it accumulates experience. Four elements support this:

<<list>>
- Continual learning: absorbing new data without catastrophically overwriting old knowledge, through methods like experience replay and elastic weight consolidation.
- Curriculum learning: an organized progression from simpler to more complex tasks, mirroring how a person builds moral sophistication by handling harder cases only after the easier ones are secure.
- Transfer learning: applying what was learned in one domain — medical decision-making, say — to a structurally similar problem in another, such as environmental or political ethics.
- Social learning: building a knowledge base and an ethical sense by observing and interacting with other agents, human or artificial, rather than from specification alone.
<</list>>

A healthcare AI system illustrates the stakes: to stay useful and ethically current, it has to keep absorbing new medical findings, evolving patient expectations, and regulatory change, rather than freezing its judgment at the point it was trained.

<<h>>Examples of Learning and Plasticity in AI Systems<</h>>
Several existing systems demonstrate different faces of this adaptability. AlphaGo combined reinforcement learning with deep neural networks to master Go, refining its own strategy through self-play beyond what it learned from human games. CLIP, trained on a broad range of internet data, generalizes across tasks its designers did not specifically train it for. Google's Neural Machine Translation system improves its translations as it processes more data and extends to new languages and domains without being rebuilt for each one. NEAT evolves neural-network architectures with a genetic algorithm, adapting structure rather than only parameters as tasks change. Hierarchical Temporal Memory, modeled on the structure of the neocortex, refines its representations as it processes new sequential data.

<<h>>Cognitive Architectures and Integrated Systems<</h>>
No single mechanism above is sufficient by itself. A system built only around predictive perception, only around goal-biased control, or only around reconstructive memory would still be missing most of what this section has described. What integration actually requires is not a fifth module bolted onto the other four, but the same predictive, error-correcting loop — generate an expectation, compare it to what arrives, update — implemented consistently across perception, memory, attention, and control, so that a change in one (a corrected prediction about the world) propagates naturally into the others (an updated memory, a redirected attention weight, a revised goal-bias) rather than requiring a separate synchronization mechanism between separately-built parts. That is the architectural bet this section has been making throughout, one mechanism recruited for different jobs, and it is a more falsifiable bet than a hand-designed module for every named cognitive process.
