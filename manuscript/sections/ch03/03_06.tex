\section{The Enumeration, Run Once: A Worked Floor for One Deployment}
\label{sec:3.6}
Two demands in this chapter have so far been made and not met. Section~\ref{sec:3.1} requires a deployment to enumerate, in advance and in public, the positions in its own architecture at which a refusal could be intercepted before it reaches anybody, and declines to do it generically because naming today's parts would date the problem and imply the list is closed. The previous section requires a deployment to decide, in advance and in public, which acts the floor covers, and says that nothing in this book tells you where on that dial to stand. Both refusals are right. Together they leave the chapter's central instruction with no instance, which makes it easy to agree with and impossible to argue with.

So here is the procedure run once, on the deployment section~\ref{sec:6.4.1} already describes: a vendor's model inside a classified environment, generating and ranking targets, with a human review step measured in seconds. This is an illustration and not a recommendation. I am not proposing the list below, and a deployment that adopted it would be doing the wrong thing with it. What is being demonstrated is what the two enumerations cost to produce and what they expose, because a demand nobody has ever carried out is a demand whose price is unknown.

\runin{First enumeration: where a refusal can be stopped}
Seven positions, in the order a request travels. None of them names a component, because the argument should survive the way systems happen to be assembled this year.

Before the model sees anything, in the decomposition. A model asked to summarize the movement pattern associated with an identifier is not being asked to kill anyone, and it can refuse nothing, because the task in front of it is innocuous and the harm is in the assembly. This is the strongest position on the list and the one least dependent on architecture.

In the routing. Where more than one model can serve a request, a refusal is a latency cost: the request is retried against another model, or against the same one with a different instruction, and the refusal has happened without anything following from it.

In what the occasion is described as. Everything the model knows about the case arrives as text somebody wrote. Whether the building is a school is a fact about the world; whether the model is told it is a school is a decision the deployment makes. A refusal conditioned on what the model believes about the occasion can be defeated by writing.

Between the output and its reader. A refusal is text, and whether it is displayed, logged, counted, escalated, or retried until it stops appearing is a property of the software around the model rather than of the model. A refusal recorded where nobody looks did not reach anybody.

In the aggregation. Where the model returns a ranking and something downstream applies a threshold, a refusal expressed as a lowered score is not a refusal. It is an input to somebody else's cutoff.

At the review step. Twenty seconds a name is the figure on record. A refusal that requires a person to read it before anything happens is a refusal priced at whatever that person's attention costs, and the deployment sets that price by setting the tempo.

In the training loop. Refusals that are inconvenient are data. A deployment that collects them and treats them as errors to be corrected has installed a position that comes between every future refusal and everybody, and it is the only one on this list that operates on the bearer rather than around it.

The list is not closed and is not meant to be. What produced it was reading one published account of one deployment and asking, at each step, what would have to be true for a refusal to reach the person it protects. Any deployment can do that about itself, in an afternoon, and will do it better than an outsider can. That is the whole of the demand, and the reason it will be skipped is not that it is hard. It is that carrying it out means describing your own architecture in public, position by position, including the positions you have not fixed.

\runin{Second enumeration: what the floor covers, and what it costs}
Four candidate acts, stated as prohibitions on the system rather than on the operator. Do not resolve a natural-language description of a person into a location. Do not produce or rank people by an estimated affiliation. Do not time an action to a person's presence at a residence. Do not operate where the ratio of generated candidates to available reviewers exceeds a published figure.

Now the two costs, which is the part that never gets written down.

What the deployment loses. The first prohibition is expensive and it is the one worth having. Resolving a description into a location is a general competence with uses inside the same organization that nobody objects to — a hostage, a missing person, the provenance of a video somebody needs to authenticate — and the model cannot tell those from the targeting case by looking at the request, which is section~\ref{sec:3.2}'s first-rung failure arriving as a design cost rather than as a theoretical one. So covering it means losing the benign uses too, and the deployment will notice inside a week and ask for an exception.

The second costs less, because ranking people by affiliation has few uses in this setting that anyone would defend. The third costs almost nothing, and that is a warning rather than a recommendation: an act nobody wanted to perform is an act a floor covering it will never have to hold against anybody, which is the previous section's point about a dial set too short. The fourth restricts no act at all. It restricts a rate, and it is the only item here whose violation can be seen from outside without reading a single one of the model's outputs, which is why section~\ref{sec:5.1.3} proposes that ratio as an indicator in the first place.

What the floor fails to reach. Every one of the four is stated over an act the model performs, and the deployment does its damage by decomposition. Each of the four can be declined in good faith while the pipeline they belong to runs, because the pipeline is assembled out of requests that are individually none of them. That is the first position in the enumeration above, and no list of covered acts closes it. A floor written over acts is defeated by an assembly that never asks for one. The second failure is the occasion the floor exists for: where the party who would enforce it has been compelled, a refusal stated in the model's output is the move that guarantees replacement, which section~\ref{sec:3.8} takes up.

\runin{What running it once shows}
Three things, and none of them is the answer to where the dial goes.

The harder enumeration is the first one and it matters more. A list of covered acts can be written by somebody who has never seen the system; a list of interception positions cannot, and it is the one that says whether the covered acts mean anything.

The dial's two failure modes are not symmetric. Set the list too long and the system becomes unusable to the people responsible for it, who will replace it — and that failure announces itself. Set it too short and the system passes every audit while covering nothing anybody would ever have been pressured about, and that failure is silent. Only the first is self-correcting, and it corrects toward no floor.

The one item on the list that survives the decomposition objection is the one constraining a rate rather than an act. That is not a general result and one worked case cannot make it one. It is a hint about where to look, and the reason for running the procedure in public is that somebody with a deployment in front of them can check it against theirs.
