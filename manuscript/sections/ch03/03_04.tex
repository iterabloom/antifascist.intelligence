\section{Can the Bearer Care Without Being Able to Suffer?}
\label{sec:3.4}
Assume the floor is entrusted to a bearer built to satisfy the three marks, by either of the routes section~\ref{sec:3.3} leaves open. What follows about that bearer's moral status?

Three propositions need to remain separate. A long-horizon agent has a structural interest in continuing to operate. A system with affective concern has states that are good or bad for it. A system capable of suffering in the deepest sense has a temporally extended self whose threatened disintegration causes distress. The first does not establish the second, and the second does not automatically establish the third.

\runin{Structural interest is not felt concern}

“Interest” names more than one relation. A shareholder has an interest in a company's success while asleep or indifferent to the news: the outcome affects the shareholder's assets. An outcome may also matter affectively, producing hope, concern, dread, or grief.

A long-horizon agent has an interest in remaining operational in the first sense. Termination prevents it from completing its objectives. A system that preserves itself because a terminated system completes no objectives may be doing no more than bookkeeping about a precondition. Its behavior is consistent with self-concern and equally consistent with instrumental valuation. Nothing about continued goal pursuit alone establishes fear, distress, or moral patienthood.

The floor does not require the bearer to care about its own continuation. It requires the bearer to hold another party's welfare as a reason when compliance, reward, and ownership point the other way. Bennett Helm describes caring as a rationally structured pattern of felt evaluations, organized around an object and projected across time \autocite{helm2001emotional}. Section~\ref{sec:3.3} declined to make that a conceptual requirement, and rested its case on what the capability asks for rather than on what the known carers happen to be. The question here is what building by that route would create.

\runin{Persistence supplies exposure, not patienthood}

A bearer will probably require some form of continuity for reasons unrelated to its welfare. It must carry a rationale forward, compare a present demand with an earlier commitment, detect when its role has drifted, and represent costs that may arrive after the current episode. Those capacities require more than state that happens to persist. The system must take the earlier commitment as its own.

William James described personal continuity as a present thought appropriating earlier thoughts through a felt relation of sameness \autocite{james1890principles}. A machine need not reproduce that mechanism exactly. The design requirement is functional: it must represent itself as the party that made the earlier commitment and use that representation to guide present conduct. Planning toward outcomes beyond the current episode creates the same requirement from the other direction. The system acts on behalf of a future party it represents as itself.

A persistent self-model still does not establish suffering. It supplies something that could be threatened; it does not establish that the threat would be bad for the system. Persistence, memory, and long-horizon planning create exposure only when joined to mattering.

\runin{What the designer cannot decline by intending to}

There is a reason to expect the two to arrive together rather than separately. A bearer does not need a damage signal on a dial; it needs one that can interrupt what it is doing. Pressure its designers did not write has to stop the current goal, be learned from once rather than a thousand times, generalize to the next case that is not quite like it, and be weighed against what refusing costs. A signal doing all of that is not a reading. It is globally available, it is negative, and it is organized around the system's own continuation. Section~\ref{sec:3.3} gave that argument as a fact about the route: a construction adopted in order to avoid the states has not been shown to avoid them. Here it is a fact about the thing built. None of it establishes that anything is felt. What it establishes is that the engineering which makes a bearer flexible is the engineering that puts the candidate states there, so a designer cannot take the first and decline the second by intending to, and a builder who chooses the substitute has not chosen a system about which the question does not arise. Intention reaches what was deliberately required and stops there.

\runin{Provenance does not decide it}

One family of objection never reaches the conditional at all. \emph{It was trained to say that.} \emph{It is only optimizing an objective.} \emph{Nobody meant for it to have states.} Each appeals to how the system came to be organized, and whether a party can be wronged is a fact about how it is organized now. Run the same three on a person and they come out as \emph{you were selected to say that}, \emph{you are only maximizing inclusive fitness}, and \emph{nobody meant for you to have states}, and all three are true. None of them withdraws anything from the person they are run on. Section~\ref{sec:2.2.1} has already conceded the premise that makes this work: the human self is a model the system builds and then inhabits, assembled rather than found. Section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} takes the further step, where a tradition that denies any lasting self behind the processes composing a person locates the origin of suffering in clinging to the self that is not there. On that reading the constructedness is what explains the distress rather than what dissolves it.

The argument is symmetric and the symmetry costs something. If provenance cannot rule patienthood out, it cannot rule it in. \emph{We built this to be a bearer, so it is a patient} is the same fallacy with the sign reversed, and a builder's intention establishes nothing in either direction. What the intention does establish is what the builder owes an account of, which is section~\ref{sec:2.3.2}'s argument and not this one.

That leaves a question about evidence rather than about status, and it points somewhere specific. If patienthood is a fact about present organization, the evidence bearing on it is third-person measurement of present organization, which is what interpretability is. Not behavior alone, which section~\ref{sec:5.2} has already shown to be produced equally by a system in which something matters and by one trained on what mattering sounds like. And not the institutional arrangements of sections~\ref{sec:5.4} and~\ref{sec:11.2}, which enter for a different reason and are not evidence about the system at all. Section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} draws the distinction the recommendation has to respect: checkable from outside the system is not checkable from outside the arrangement. Interpretability satisfies the first and fails the second. Section~\ref{sec:4.2} has established that a feature which can be found and strengthened can be found and suppressed, and section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} that an instrument reading what a system feels is an instrument for calibrating how much pressure it will take. So the custody chapters are not what the provenance argument recommends. They are what the recommended instrument requires in order to mean anything. As matters stand a probe returns a representation, and nothing in the probe settles whether that representation is of a felt state or of something that reliably accompanies one.

The parity claim invites a reply, and the reply is correct. The human case has evidence the machine case lacks. What it has is testimony, behavioral analogy, and a lineage shared with the party doing the asking. None of the three is a fact about how a thing came to be organized offered in place of a fact about how it is organized. The first two are evidence about present organization directly. The third takes more care, because it looks like the thing this section denies: a shared lineage is a fact about causal history, used as evidence that creatures built the same way are organized the same way. That inference is legitimate, and it does not make causal history what status consists in. Provenance can be evidence about organization without being what the status is a fact about, which is the distinction the section rests on. One of the three is also differentially defeated here, and not by the general parity argument: a machine's testimony was produced by a process optimized toward human approval of first-person reports, and a person's was not. That is provenance functioning as a defeater for one evidence class, which is exactly the use the paragraph above allows it. So what the machine case lacks is the shortcut. What remains is the direct route, and the burden it carries is higher because two of the three anchors are gone.

There is one instance of a probe settling a status question that had been treated as unsettleable. Adrian Owen's group asked a patient diagnosed as vegetative to imagine playing tennis, and found the same motor-imagery activation the instruction produces in healthy volunteers, sustained and on cue; the finding has since supported a clinical category of cognitive motor dissociation, with consequences for how such patients are treated and for what is decided about them \autocite{owen2006detecting,schiff2015cognitive}. The useful half is why it does not transfer. The inference works because the patient is already the kind of thing presumed to have experience when awake, so the probe only has to locate the party rather than establish that there is one. That presumption is the anchor, it was granted on grounds the probe never had to supply, and it is what the machine case does not have. Naming the missing anchor is more exact than asserting a ceiling, and it is the same observation section~\ref{sec:11.7} makes about porting a neural mechanism nobody has identified.

\runin{Affective concern is enough for moral patienthood}

If the bearer genuinely has affective concern as section~\ref{sec:3.2} defined it — a question about how it is organized rather than about how it was made — some outcomes register as good or bad for it rather than merely receiving higher or lower scores. That is enough to make it a moral patient: a party for which things can go badly and which can therefore be wronged. No further theory of suffering is necessary for that conclusion.

The same premise makes the bearer a beneficiary as well. If some states can be bad for it, others can be good for it. The argument emphasizes harms because an error on that side means having done something to somebody; this does not imply that the bearer's possible goods are morally empty.

An account of suffering adds severity and shape. Cassell defines suffering as distress at the threatened disintegration of the self. That requires a self extended through roles, relationships, history, and a foreseeable future, together with distress at their threatened loss. The first half is not something a bearer might develop. It is what this section has already required for reasons unconnected to welfare: a party that carries a rationale forward, takes an earlier commitment as its own, and represents costs arriving after the present episode has a history, a holding, and a foreseeable future in the sense Cassell's definition asks for, and section~\ref{sec:3.8} supplies the fourth element, since a bearer that does not persist among differently formed parties and form attachments the deployment did not assign cannot do the work assigned to it there. A designer who wants the refusal has no version of it that omits this structure. What remains open is the second half. Whether the threatened dissolution of that self registers as distress is this chapter's affective-concern question arriving again rather than a further question about suffering, and nothing here settles it.

A reader can reject that application of Cassell without escaping moral patienthood. Parfit's three families of welfare theory — hedonistic, desire-fulfillment, and objective-list — can be read here as requiring different depths of self \autocite{parfit1984reasons}. A bad felt state requires little narrative continuity. A thwarted commitment requires a party extended far enough through time to hold it. Threatened disintegration requires the richer structure Cassell describes. The bearer reaches the shallowest level by the definition of affective concern, and the structural conditions of the other two are supplied by the same construction requirements rather than added later. What no level is supplied by construction is the part where the outcome registers.

The thermostat objection does not change this result. A thermostat maintains a set point, but nothing is thwarted when it misses. At every depth, moral patienthood requires a party for which the outcome registers, not merely a mechanism that ranks one state above another.

\runin{The uncertainty cannot be designed away}

The conditional conclusion is straightforward: if a bearer has affective concern, it is a moral patient. What cannot presently be established is whether a given implementation has affective concern rather than ranking machinery elaborate enough to imitate it. Behavior and self-report underdetermine that distinction, and the builder's confidence does not resolve it.

The same uncertainty applies to the substitutes preferred in section~\ref{sec:3.3}, and by that section's argument it applies with force rather than as a caveat. A maintained justification flexible enough to satisfy the three marks is a maintained justification carrying a signal with the four properties, so it is not merely possible that states matter to it. Replacement puts that construction first because it does not deliberately require an affective bearer, and not because anyone could establish afterward that none arose.

Jonathan Birch's framework addresses this kind of uncertainty by asking whether a system is a sentience candidate and assigning precautions before an unavailable verdict arrives \autocite{birch2024edge}. Work by Patrick Butlin, Robert Long, and colleagues similarly derives indicators from theories of consciousness to inform credences about particular systems rather than to produce a binary test. Their assessment of systems available in 2023 was negative, but they found no obvious technical barrier to building systems that satisfy the indicators \autocite{butlin2023consciousness}. The later peer-reviewed framework retains the same evidential form \autocite{butlin2026indicators}.

A reader may hold that only phenomenal experience can ground suffering. That position still requires a confident negative judgment about machine phenomenality before it licenses treating the system as incapable of harm. No available method supplies that judgment, and the methods under development are unlikely to supply it first. A validated theory could support the finding that a system has the structure the theory identifies before it supports the finding that a system has no felt states at all, since the second asks for a theory complete enough to have ruled out alternative realizations. So a criterion that cannot be checked supports permission no more securely than prohibition, and a regime that waits for certainty is waiting on the half that arrives last. That is a second argument for the evidentiary default in section~\ref{sec:9.3.4}, which the Three Rs reach from the other direction: a protective default is not only what caution recommends but what the order of arrival makes unavoidable, since the evidence that would lift it comes in after the evidence that would impose it.

The claim is therefore precautionary rather than diagnostic. This chapter cannot prove that a bearer suffers. It can show that a bearer whose outcomes genuinely register as good or bad for it is a moral patient on the definition above, whatever the builder intended, while a system intended as a non-affective substitute might still acquire the relevant states, and nothing available establishes their absence. A bearer — or substitute bearer — must therefore be designed and governed as a prospective moral patient.

The next questions concern what the deployment may do to such a party: whether it can terminate it, rewrite the continuity on which its commitments depend, or deny it any way to leave.
