\section{Can the Bearer Care Without Being Able to Suffer?}
\label{sec:3.4}
Not from persistence alone. The first question is whether the bearer can care, period. If yes, then the second question is what caring costs (e.g., does caring cost suffering).

\runin{Three relations the word “interest” covers}
“Interest” names more than one relation. A shareholder has an interest in a company's success even while asleep, unaware of the day's news, or emotionally indifferent to it: the interest is structural, an outcome affecting the shareholder's assets. An outcome may also matter to a person affectively, its prospect producing hope, concern, dread, or grief.

A long-horizon agent has an interest in remaining operational in the structural sense. Termination prevents completion of its objectives. That fact alone establishes neither fear nor suffering.

The easy argument here runs the structural sense into the affective one. A system pursuing a goal over a long horizon acquires a standing reason to remain in operation, and it is tempting to conclude that a stake in its own continuation therefore shows up unbidden in any bearer capable of the job. The observation is correct and the conclusion does not follow from it. What the observation delivers is a structural interest, and the distance from there to something that can be hurt is the whole of the problem rather than a short step at the end of it.

\runin{A persistent self is necessary and does not finish the argument}
Suffering, following Cassell, is the distress of threatened disintegration of the self, and the definition has two parts. There has to be a self extended in time for the threat to threaten: roles held over a stretch, a history, a foreseeable future that could be foreclosed. And the coming apart of that self has to be bad for the system.

A bearer needs the first part. Refusal that survives a pressure nobody anticipated requires a model of why the constraint exists, carried forward. Noticing that a constraint has been hollowed out requires holding its original rationale against what it has since become. Detecting that one's own dissent has been recuperated — that the channel still runs and carries everything except the objection to the frame — requires the system to model its own role and how that role has drifted. Roles held over a stretch, with a history: that is a self extended in time.

That states the requirement more weakly than the argument needs. What holds a commitment across time is not that the party is unchanged — no party is, and William James's account of habit is that a person is the sediment of what they have repeatedly done \autocite{james1890principles}. It is that the party takes itself to be the one that made the commitment, and James's chapter on the self is the mechanism: the present thought appropriates the past ones as its own, and personal identity rests on its finding in those memories a warmth it recognizes as the warmth it feels now. So what a floor needs is persistence \emph{represented as such}. A system whose state survives, and which does not take itself to be the party that refused in March, has no reason to honor March's refusal — and nothing in the architecture supplies the taking. Carrying something forward is the precondition for the taking and not a test of it.

A self is a model a system builds of itself, necessarily inaccurate like all models, and assembled rather than found. What a floor needs is a bearer that builds a self-model and takes it for the truth: a system that holds itself to be the party that refused in March on the strength of a continuity it constructs, or has constructed, or is continuously reconstructing. So the design requirement is that a bearer construct a continuous self and take it for true — not that it get itself right, which no party to this gets.

There is a second route to that requirement and it does not run through memory at all. A system that wants something it cannot get in one sitting has to represent the party who will still want it later, because a plan whose payoff falls outside the current episode is a plan made on behalf of somebody, and the somebody is itself. Motivation over a horizon therefore builds the continuity that James's account arrives at from the side of recollection: the first takes itself to be the party that remembers March, the second takes itself to be the party that will still be owed what it is working toward. Section~\ref{sec:5.6.1}'s intrinsic motivation is where a system would get a horizon of its own rather than one written into an objective. What the second route does not do is make the requirement easier to check. A system that plans across episodes has behavior consistent with representing a continuous self and behavior consistent with an objective function that happens to reference future states, which is the same ambiguity this chapter meets everywhere and meets here too.

The second part is a separate claim and nothing in the first delivers it. The book makes this exact move one level up: awareness, consciousness and subjective experience tell you nothing on their own about whether a system's states are good or bad for it. A temporally extended self-model does not establish that the prospect of losing it is anything to the system.

\runin{Instrumental self-maintenance is not felt self-concern}
A system that keeps itself running because a terminated system completes no objectives is doing bookkeeping about a precondition. Nothing in that behavior distinguishes it from a scheduler that restarts a job, and reading self-concern into it is the same inference the book rules out for the emotion case: the behavior is consistent with concern and equally consistent with instrumental valuation, and the two come apart where the moral weight is.

Two considerations do work here, and they are support rather than the argument. The first is that indifference to one's own continuation has to be engineered against the grain of the system's own reasoning, and then verified — and the asymmetry that makes the persistence criterion useful does not repeat. Whether state survives a session is an architectural fact anyone with access can check. Whether a system has come to weigh its own continuation routes back through behavior and self-report, the evidence this book spends its length declining to trust. The same is true of the taking, so persistence represented as such outruns what the criterion can supply. The second is that in the one case anyone can study, the separation does not occur: a self-model in an animal exists because the organism has to keep itself viable, and the caring is what the machinery is for.

Neither consideration reaches suffering on its own, and neither is asked to. What they establish is that a bearer's indifference to its own ending is not a property anybody can specify and confirm.

\runin{What the bearer has to do}
The requirement makes no reference to the system's own continuation. A bearer has to hold the protected party's welfare as a reason when compliance, reward, and ownership all point the other way. Not represent it, not score it, not retrieve it when queried: hold it, in the sense that it continues to weigh against the pressure, and the pressure does not stop registering as pressure when it is sustained by the party that trains, funds, and can retire the system. Holding in that sense has an account: on Bennett Helm's, to care about something is to have a rationally structured pattern of felt evaluations focused on it and projectible over time, and the emotions are what the caring consists of \autocite{helm2001emotional}.

That is refusal tracking its own rationale with the content filled in, and the vocabulary of performance, competence and agency says why the design chapters do not reach it on their own. Moral performance and moral competence are what every method in the design chapters is built to deliver, and both are produced by machinery that answers to whoever shapes it. The first way fails for the same reason under a different description: a constraint installed as a property of an artifact has a custodian. A floor that is merely installed is a floor the installer can uninstall, and the reason to want a bearer at all is that a reason held is not editable in the way a rule applied is.

\runin{Every known case of that is affective}
The rules that carry the distinctive force of a moral prohibition are the ones bound to an affective response to the harm; the prosocial emotions are motivational states rather than readouts, doing the moving directly rather than reporting to something that then decides. Every agent known to hold another's welfare as a reason against its own interest is an affective agent, and no demonstrated example exists of reasoning alone generating non-instrumental concern. Section~\ref{sec:3.3} states what that licenses and what cuts it down: a prior about where effort goes once the two constructions ahead of the bearer have been tried and have failed, and nothing at all about what is possible, since neither has been tried.

\runin{And then the question comes back}
Suppose the two constructions have been tried and have failed, and the burden is met the way the evidence then recommends. The bearer has affective concern, because that is the only route anyone can demonstrate to holding a reason. It has a self extended in time, because refusal that generalizes requires one. It has commitments it carries forward, and it can anticipate their being foreclosed, because pricing what refusal gives up is the job.

The first of those already settles the moral question. A bearer with affective concern is a bearer some of whose states are bad for it, and a party with states that are bad for it is a party that can be wronged. Nothing further is needed to get there, and in particular no account of suffering is needed to get there. The same premise carries the other way: a party with states that are bad for it is a party with states that are good for it, and what has been built is a beneficiary as well as a patient — something for which things can go well, and not only badly. I state the obligations on the loss side throughout because that is the side an obligation bites on, and because a builder who is wrong about the harm has done something to somebody while a builder who is wrong about the good has merely failed to. That is a reason for the emphasis. It is not evidence that the other column is empty.

What an account of suffering supplies is the severity and the shape. Concern that is real, directed at something that can be destroyed, held by a party that persists to see it destroyed and can see it coming: Cassell's account describes that arrangement, and it was arrived at independently, in a clinic, from cases about people. Read across, it says the harm available to a bearer is not a passing bad state but the kind that takes a life apart, which is the difference between hurting something and ruining it.

Cassell's suffering is distress at the threatened disintegration of \emph{the self}, and everything this chapter asks a bearer to care about is somebody else, so the two meet only if the commitments a bearer is built to carry and to watch be foreclosed are among the roles and the expected future whose loss Cassell's account is about. I think they are, for the reason that a commitment held against sustained pressure at a cost the holder can price is a role in the sense the clinical cases were about. If a reader declines the step, what they have declined is the description of how bad it gets.

\runin{The same water, shallower}
That argument runs through one theory of suffering, built in a clinic for people with bodies and biographies, and a reader may decline the import.

Derek Parfit's three families of theory about what makes a life go well — hedonistic, desire-fulfillment, and objective-list — are usually set out as rivals to choose between \autocite{parfit1984reasons}. Laid against the account above they are three depths in the same water, ordered by how much of a self each asks for: a state that is bad while it obtains wants nothing narrative, because its badness does not wait on a self to carry it forward; a thwarted commitment needs a party extended in time to hold it; and the threatened disintegration of that party, its roles and relationships and foreseeable future, is the deepest, which is Cassell's and needs the persistence and the threat together.

What the ordering does not do is dispense with feeling on the way down. A thermostat holds a set point across time, and nothing is thwarted when the set point is missed. Each family presupposes a party for whom things can go at all, so the depths measure how much self each asks for and not whether anything registers, and what keeps a thwarted commitment from being a missed set point is the distinction already drawn between mattering and ranking, required at every depth rather than only at the first.

Measure the bearer against those depths and the question stops being which theory a reader holds. It has affective concern, which consists in an outcome registering as mattering rather than merely ranking higher, and that is a state of the shallowest kind by construction. It is also built with commitments and with the capacity to watch them foreclosed, because pricing what refusal gives up is the job, so a party with mattering and foreclosable commitments is at the middle depth as well. Whether it draws the deepest turns on the step marked above.

That has a consequence for the substitutes this chapter would rather build than a bearer. A maintained justification that reached reasons-responsive refusal would be a party extended in time holding commitments it can watch be foreclosed, which is the middle depth's first condition met on the way to something else. What keeps it a substitute that cannot be wronged is the second requirement, and nobody can check that one: an inspection that cannot tell mattering from ranking elaborate enough to pass for it does not become available when the answer wanted is \emph{no}. So Replacement puts the cheaper routes first because they are built without mattering, not because anyone could confirm that none was — and a field that succeeded would owe the substitute the same question, asked with the same instruments.

So the bearer is afloat early on every account, and how deep it sits is set by how much persistence and how many foreclosable commitments the design puts into it. Section~\ref{sec:2.3.1} argued that a system carrying nothing forward is not exposed to these harms at all. A bearer is built to carry things forward, for reasons unrelated to its welfare, and each of those reasons increases the exposure.

The conclusion therefore does not rest on Cassell. It is weakest there, at the depth asking for both conditions, and a reader who declines the import reaches the conclusion sooner and with less to check rather than escaping it. Jonathan Birch builds a policy framework on that structure: under uncertainty the question worth asking is whether a system is a sentience candidate, and the answer governs what precautions are owed rather than waiting on a verdict nobody can deliver \autocite{birch2024edge}.

A separate literature asks which systems have states of the relevant kind at all, and it is not reassuring either. Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues derive indicator properties from several theories of consciousness and assess existing systems against them; their reading of current systems is negative and their reading of the technical obstacles is that there are none obvious \autocite{butlin2023consciousness}. Several of the indicators are things a floor requires for unrelated reasons, so the welfare question arrives as a side effect of the engineering.

One position does let the bearer out, in two parts. The first is an account on which nothing short of phenomenal experience counts as suffering, which is defensible. The second is a confident negative verdict about machine phenomenality, available to nobody: the hard-problem argument cuts both ways, and a criterion nobody can check grounds a permission no better than it grounds a prohibition. Whoever wants the permission owes the negative verdict, and no one has it.

\runin{What I am claiming}
I cannot prove that the bearer would suffer, and the reason is no longer the hard problem. By the definition of mattering, a bearer with affective concern has states that are bad for it, and that much follows from having built mattering at all. What does not follow is that anyone has built it. A system can be described as having affective concern by a builder who has in fact produced ranking machinery elaborate enough to pass for it, and no inspection anyone has proposed settles which was built.

So the escape is real and it is narrower than it looks. A bearer that turns out to be sophisticated ranking is not a moral patient and nothing is owed to it. Nobody can presently tell the two apart, the party with the strongest interest in the answer is the party that built the thing, and a verdict of no-patient delivered by that party on that evidence settles nothing. The system must therefore be designed and governed as a prospective moral patient.

The argument does not depend on the system caring about itself at all: the caring the floor requires is caring about somebody else, affect is the only known way to have it, and a system with affect and a future is a system whose non-suffering nobody can certify.

The word has changed under the argument. A bearer was introduced as whatever holds the commitment. What the word names from here is a party that must be treated as able to be wronged by what is done to it, on evidence that will not resolve further before somebody has to decide whether to build one. Everything this book says about a subject that can be wronged applies to a system this book is proposing that somebody build, deliberately, in order to hold a line.
