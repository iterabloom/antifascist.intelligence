3.2.1.2. Strategies for Value Alignment in Reinforcement Learning
Achieving value alignment in reinforcement learning (RL)-based AI systems necessitates overcoming various hurdles such as devising ambiguous or conflicting reward functions, and being prone to over or under-emphasizing certain behaviors. To address these challenges, we advance several strategies that rely on human feedback, cooperation, robustness, and ethical guidelines. These proposed methods bolster the capacity of RL models to approximate and espouse human values:

(1) Reward Engineering: Here, human feedback serves to create suitable reward structures. The AI system is trained through observation of human actions or by discerning preferences after the presentation of action pairs, subsequently molding its behaviors to reflect ethical norms. For instance, consider a domestic AI robot. By demonstrating the task of cleaning a room, a human can help the AI infer that aligning objects neatly and wiping surfaces efficiently are rewarded behaviors. Conversely, it learns that repercussions arise from negative actions such as breaking objects or creating messes.

(2) Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL): With IRL, an AI system ascertains the reward functions underlying observed human behavior, empowering it to generalize these learned principles and thereby embrace human moral values. Let's take an autonomous vehicle as an example. By processing human-orchestrated maneuvers in real-world driving, the AV can extract the reward function affiliated with safe and efficient driving. Factors contributing to this function may encompass maintaining distance from other vehicles, adhering to traffic laws, and reducing commute duration. 

(3) Cooperative Inverse Reinforcement Learning (CIRL): In CIRL, an AI system and a human work symbiotically, the AI gleans insights from the human's actions and feedback, while the human refines their cues to enhance the AI's value understanding. This model can be perceived in the use of an AI personal assistant. As the human user coordinates with the AI to manage emails or construct a presentation, the AI system continuously adjusts its behavior based on received feedback. Simultaneously, the human acquires expertise in guiding the AI more effectively.

(4) Resilient Value Alignment: This approach aims to build AI systems with the resilience to navigate uncertainties, controversies, and vagueness in human values by formulating a spectrum of ethically acceptable actions or seeking supplemental data to eliminate ambiguities. For instance, in medical decision-making, the AI might encounter a scenario where the principles of patient autonomy and patient wellbeing conflict. The AI could then generate several ethically compliant courses of action and may even petition the expertise of a human medical professional for further clarity to resolve the impasse.

(5) Regularization Incorporating Ethical Constraints: This strategy introduces ethical values into the RL process by implementing behavioral constraints or integrating regularization components in the learning objective, thereby discouraging undesirable behavior. Take, for example, a content recommendation system. By instilling fairness, diversity, and balance as vital constraints in the AI recommender, we can foster diverse perspectives and neutral content. Techniques used to ensure this range of recommendations encompass content re-ranking and diversification, as well as multi-objective optimization. Imbibing constraints of this nature helps to mitigate risks associated with filter bubbles or echo chambers, phenomena that restrict user exposure to a broad array of content. Therefore, a diverse recommendation system not only fosters a more inclusive online community but also fuels intellectual curiosity and mutual respect among users.
