Chapter 3: The Floor Beneath Learned Values
Three times in this book, working on unrelated problems, I arrive at the same requirement and do not stop to notice it.

Section 5.6.3, on how much latitude an autonomous system should have, concludes that no amount of well-designed autonomy substitutes for a genuine override — a human able to intervene, and a system built to accept the intervention rather than resist it. Section 8.7.6 works through what a vendor owes when its model reaches a targeting pipeline, and section 8.7.7 draws the conclusion: what makes a system antifascist is not that it tracks what people want, but that there are things it will not do to a person no matter who wants it. Section 10.1.3, counting up a decade of voluntary commitments, concludes that a commitment nobody can enforce is a different kind of object from one somebody can.

Those are three descriptions of one thing: a constraint that does not bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to the party holding the system. Call it a floor.

The trouble is that section 2.1.1 has already dealt with the family of ethical theories that puts unconditional constraints first, and dealt with it briefly — deontological approaches were noted, credited with holding a line regardless of outcome, and set aside on the grounds that duties conflict and rules do not cover every case. Then this book spends chapters 4 and 5, close to a third of its length, on learned judgment: reinforcement learning, learning from demonstration, mentorship, play, moral apprenticeship, the growth mindset. The design chapters build the thing chapter 8 and chapter 10 say is insufficient, having already dismissed the thing they say is necessary. The "hybrid approach" section 2.1 gestures at is where the resolution was supposed to live, and nothing was ever put there.

Here is what belongs there. An unconditional floor of things the system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate preference, with learned judgment operating above it and never beneath it. Deontology's standing objection — that rules conflict and run out — is an objection to rules deciding every case, and it has no force against a floor that decides a deliberately small number of things and leaves the rest to judgment. The rigidity that is a defect in a complete moral theory is the entire point of a floor.

That reframing does real work in this book. It gives section 2.1's hybrid approach actual content. It moves the tamper-resistance research in section 9.2.2 from an appendix topic to the center, because if there is a floor then everything turns on whether it holds. And it converts the antifascist question from a matter of what a system values into two much harder and more answerable questions: what is in the floor, and who can edit it.
