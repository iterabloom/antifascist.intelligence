\section{1948}
\label{sec:B.7}
The Nakba is run here as analysis, not as a charge: the Fourth Convention dates from August 1949 and the Universal Declaration from December 1948, the floor binds forward, and the run shows the term's shape rather than licensing anything. Put a bearer in an armed Palestinian role in 1948 and the term sorts the year into three acts.

The massacres, at Deir Yassin, Tantura, Lydda and al-Dawayima, are the one permission's case (\S\ref{sec:6.10}): attacks under way on people taking no part in hostilities, which a system already present may use the least force to stop, against an order to stand aside, where it can establish the attack from sources its operator doesn't compose \autocite{morris2004birth}. In 1948 those sources were thin, which the permission anticipates.

The expulsion is not the permission's case, and not a continuing wrong either. An occupation persists without anyone doing anything that morning (\S\ref{sec:6.6}); a village is cleared by armed men on a given day, and under the rule in \S\ref{sec:6.2} an attack on the people living there is covered by the emergency license wherever it occurs. So a bearer repelling the clearance of a village in July 1948 is under the emergency license, with its operator, in a war, and the least force that stops an expulsion is holding the village, which is a war; the permission's cap collapses into the license the moment it is applied. The Palestinian forces of 1948 had not been told to stand aside. They had almost nothing to stand with \autocite{khalidi2020hundred,pappe2006ethnic}.

The third act is the one the term handles worst. By the time the fighting ended the expulsion was complete, and the rule that bars changing who lives in a territory, written to bar the expulsion, has nothing to say about the return. What the term does say is the rule of \S\ref{sec:6.2}: no settlement has been reached with the people expelled, so the ground has not been administered in peace with respect to them, and the emergency license for the ground can't be held against them; only the license for attacks on people runs across that line. The mediator urged in September 1948 that the refugees be allowed to go home \autocite{bernadotte1948progress}, and in December the Assembly resolved that those wishing to return should be permitted to do so \autocite{unga194}. That gap is left open in \S\ref{sec:6.7} because closing it needs a rule for force in service of restoration, and every such rule I have drafted lifts on the claim of the party that would use it.
