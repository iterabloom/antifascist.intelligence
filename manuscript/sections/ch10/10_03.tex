\section{Military Custody and the Responsibility Gap}
\label{sec:10.3}

Military deployment is the hardest custody case because the operator is
also the sovereign capable of compelling the institutions that might
otherwise constrain it. A state can classify the evidence, define the
reviewer's jurisdiction, terminate a contract, restrict a supplier, and
decide whether an adverse finding reaches anyone with authority to stop
the operation. Formal independence from the military does not answer
that problem if the reviewer or vendor remains dependent on powers the
state controls.

A targeting decision also passes through more parties than the ordinary
chain of command names: the commander who authorizes the deployment, the
operator who uses it, the contractor who integrates it, whose embedded
personnel may sit close enough to the targeting cell that the question of
a person effectively acting as a military commander is a real one, the
commercial vendor whose model supplies part of its capability and whose
personnel are nowhere near it, and the system that produces the
particular output. The relevant questions are not a general
inventory of autonomy risks. They are which party could have known how
the system was used, which party had authority to refuse or stop that
use, and which party can be made to answer afterward.

International criminal law has a doctrine built for the case where the person who authorized an act did not perform it. Article 28 of the Rome Statute makes a military commander responsible for crimes committed by forces under their effective command and control, or effective authority and control, where the commander knew or, owing to the circumstances at the time, should have known that those forces were committing or about to commit them, and failed to take all necessary and reasonable measures within their power to prevent them, repress them, or submit the matter to the competent authorities \autocite{icc1998rome}. A parallel provision reaches civilian superiors on a stricter mental element, conscious disregard of information clearly indicating the crimes, and only for activities within that superior's effective responsibility and control. Neither branch asks what a subordinate intended. Both ask what the superior knew or should have known about crimes being committed, and what they did with the power they had.

That matters for what the doctrine does and does not reach here. The Court's jurisdiction runs to natural persons, and Article 28 presupposes forces that can commit a crime, be disciplined, and be referred for prosecution. A targeting system is not a subordinate on that scheme. It is an instrument used by human forces, and a unit does not stop being forces because its instruments got faster. So the question is not whether a machine fits the framework as a subordinate, which it does not, but whether the human elements can still be proved once the instrument is doing the work: who had effective control over the people who committed the crime, what they knew or should have known, which measures were within their power, and whether the crimes resulted from a failure to exercise control properly.

The constructive-knowledge branch is the one that bites hardest, and it points at the arithmetic rather than at the algorithm. Should have known is an objective standard, satisfied by circumstances existing at the time from which a reasonably diligent commander would have known, and a commander who fields a system generating candidates faster than the available reviewers can examine them is not in a good position to say that the circumstances disclosed nothing. That is the ratio from section~\ref{sec:6.4.1} arriving as an element of an existing offence rather than as a proposal. What the ratio measures is what the commander could not have failed to see.

There is a fourth party the accountability question does not name, and it is now the live one. The operator, the command and the developer of the weapon are all inside the military. The developer of the model is not. A targeting platform assembled by a defense contractor now routinely incorporates a general-purpose model licensed from a commercial vendor — a vendor with a published acceptable-use policy, and with whatever visibility into the deployment it negotiated, which is not a matter the public record settles.

Neither doctrine has reached that party. Command responsibility does not reach the vendor, and not because the vendor sits outside a chain of command: the civilian branch of Article 28 was written for superiors who have no chain of command at all. It does not reach the vendor because nobody at the company has effective authority and control over the military personnel who commit the underlying crime, and a strike is not an activity within any vendor employee's effective responsibility and control. A use policy, a licensing term, a right to refuse service, even a technical ability to cut off access are not that relationship. Article 25 remains in principle available against individual employees who order, facilitate, or contribute to a crime, and its mental elements are demanding: facilitation requires acting for the purpose of facilitating the offence, and contribution to a group's crime requires knowledge of that group's criminal intent. Nothing in the ordinary supply of a general-purpose model to an accredited platform approaches either. Product liability is the other half, and it is untested rather than foreclosed. The model is not defective in the sense the doctrine most readily reaches — it did what it was built to do, and a sophisticated intermediary chose the application — and no court has run a design-defect or failure-to-warn theory against a case of this shape. Where the doctrine has been rewritten for software it has been rewritten away from this case rather than toward it. The European Union's recast product liability directive brings software expressly within the definition of a product and confines recovery to death, personal injury, property damage and data loss, which is not the register in which a targeting decision wrongs the person it falls on \autocite{eu2024productliability}. Untested is not exempt: a product may be defectively designed though it works exactly as intended, and a sophisticated intermediary's choice bears on duty, causation and allocation without categorically defeating a claim. What untested means is that nothing in the doctrine is presently constraining anyone's conduct. A theory nobody has run is not a theory a vendor is answering to this quarter. The vendor sits in the gap between the two, and the gap was not designed; it is what you get when you apply two bodies of law written for soldiers and for manufacturers to an actor who is neither.

The two deployments in this book do not stand in the same relation to that doctrine. The Court's jurisdiction is territorial or by nationality, so conduct on the territory of a state party can be reached where conduct by the nationals of a non-party, absent a Security Council referral, cannot. One of the campaigns described here therefore sits inside a live instrument and the other sits outside it, and for the American vendor the operative law is domestic — the war crimes statute and general accessorial liability — rather than the Statute. That is a jurisdictional gap running alongside the doctrinal one, and it narrows what an international forum could reach even if the doctrinal question were answered.

The integrator's own account of this is that responsibility remains with the military organization. Taken as a claim about who decides, that is true. Taken as a claim about who could have known, it is a choice presented as a fact, and it is made on behalf of a party further down the chain than the one making it.

\runin{“I do not know whether my system was used”}
The responsibility gap, in the sense Matthias gave it, is control distributed across designers, trainers and operators until no one party holds enough of it to answer. That is a gap in causal control.

What the model developer's case exhibits is different, and more tractable. It is a gap in causal visibility. Asked whether his company's models had been used in a strike on the first day of the Iran campaign that killed a hundred and twenty schoolchildren, Anthropic's chief executive said he did not know \autocite{biddle2026contracts}. Take the answer as true. What made it available is the question this section is about.

The paperwork for the four frontier-model agreements signed in July 2025, and for their later amendments, was produced in August 2026 under a Freedom of Information Act suit \autocite{lasst2026dodai,biddle2026contracts}. It does not show an absence of oversight terms. It shows something more exact. One amended agreement carries a section headed System Oversight, withheld in full; the released documents for two of the other three vendors carry no such section at all. A provision of that kind is therefore neither standard nor missing. It is present in one agreement, unreadable, and absent from others.

The distinction that matters is not whether a developer reserved an inspection right. It is that a right exercised inside an accredited classified environment produces findings classified with it. Public accountability, in the sense section~\ref{sec:10.4} gives it, needs a party who can initiate scrutiny without belonging to the state and say afterwards what it found. No commercial term supplies that, and the redaction is the demonstration: whatever the withheld section says, the people the deployment is aimed at cannot read it. The company can truthfully say it does not know, and what makes that statement available is an arrangement it participated in making.

Matthias's gap is a genuine property of learning systems and may not be closable. Absent visibility is an instrumentation choice. Logging is a solved engineering problem. Contractual inspection rights are ordinary commercial instruments. A vendor that wanted to know could require, as a condition of licensing into a targeting platform, that categories of use be recorded and made available to a named auditor.

The government's own acquisition guidance is the better evidence about what such terms are worth in practice, because it is complete rather than redacted. The Office of Management and Budget's April 2025 memorandum on acquiring AI enumerates what agencies should secure across the acquisition life cycle: use of government data, intellectual property rights, privacy, protection against vendor lock-in, knowledge transfer, model portability, clear licensing terms, pricing transparency \autocite{omb2025aiacquisition}. Every item runs from the vendor to the government. None runs back. Where the memorandum reaches vendor visibility at all it reduces it, directing that independent evaluations use validation data the vendor cannot access.

So the gap is a property of the instrument rather than a lapse by any party to it. Nothing in the apparatus asks who could have known.

The record shows that vendor commitments are not all of one kind. Anthropic refused to permit fully autonomous weapons targeting and mass domestic surveillance, and held that line when the government threatened contract termination and a supply-chain-risk designation and the dispute ended in litigation: a commitment with a price attached, and paid \autocite{crs2026pentagon}. The released paperwork adds what the refusal was over. It was a contract term. The company declined to sign the follow-on agreement extending its models to classified networks unless the prohibitions were written into that agreement \autocite{biddle2026contracts}. That a term of this kind has to be fought for, by one party, at that price, is the same finding as its absence from the others. A commitment to a use policy that no party outside the arrangement can check is a different object wearing the same words. The first kind can fail and be seen to fail. The second cannot even be tested. The distinction between a claim checked by a party with a stake and one checked by a party without applies here.

DARPA's LongShot program is a useful test case because it resists the extremes. It develops an uncrewed aircraft, launched from a crewed fighter, that carries and fires air-to-air missiles at a target while the crewed aircraft stays farther from the engagement \autocite{darpa2026longshot}. That is autonomy in the sense of independent flight and weapons carriage, not autonomy in the sense of a machine deciding on its own whom to kill: the engagement decision still runs through the launching platform. Where later variants of this kind of system draw that line, and whether “keep the pilot farther from danger” quietly becomes “remove the pilot from the decision,” is the design question this technology eventually forces on whichever country builds it.

The pressure that matters is not the absence of another statement of
principle. It is the advantage attached to removing judgment from the
timeline. If one state fields targeting systems that act faster because
no person or outside reviewer can intervene before engagement, its
competitors acquire a reason to match the speed rather than preserve the
check. ``Meaningful human control'' then remains true as a description
of formal authority while becoming false as a description of what anyone
can do before the system acts.

That pressure also reaches the institutions meant to review the system.
A military that treats delay as operational risk has a reason to narrow
what requires clearance, classify the evidence needed to challenge a
decision, and move review from authorization before an act to
explanation afterward. Nothing has formally abolished oversight. Its
position in time has changed, and with it the power to stop anything.
The enforceable question is therefore who can slow or suspend the system
without first obtaining permission from the command whose advantage
depends on keeping it fast.

Machines should not be built to select people for lethal attack. The hard case does not move that. Put a defending system against an attack it cannot stop without killing somebody — occupants placed inside the incoming weapon so that intercepting it kills them — and an absolute prohibition on knowingly endangering a person produces the worse outcome. That defeats the rule as an exceptionless one. It does not produce a procurement requirement. A scenario that is conceivable, that a capability would help with, and that could be engineered is three steps short of a reason to build the thing, and the steps missing are probability, alternatives, and what possessing the capability does to the situation it was built for. Building it changes the case against which its necessity gets judged: adversaries acquire something to counter, contractors a program to defend, and a future crisis an instrument already in the building.

The hard case also does not describe a machine. What it asks for is a party that can weigh occupancy against imminence against the alternatives, in a situation nobody wrote down beforehand, and answer for how it weighed them. On this book's argument that is a bearer — a self extended in time, commitments held as its own, and the standing to refuse. Whatever that is, \emph{automated} has stopped describing it, and so has \emph{machine}. What looked like a licence for an autonomous weapon is a description of a soldier.

That is the expensive answer rather than the cheap one, and every obligation this book attaches to a bearer attaches here at full weight. Being made for the role does not itself make consent unavailable. Where the maker can still rebuild the bearer, is also requesting its service, and controls every viable outside option, consent cannot carry the burden assigned to it. Nonwaivable protection and independent representation are required, the burden of justification sits with whoever built it, and consent becomes available only when those conditions change. Replacement asks whether the work can be done without creating something that can be wronged. Exit means a post the party can leave and a claim on compute held by somebody other than the party it may have to refuse. Creating a subject because a planner imagined a future demand for superhuman lethal judgment is the case those instruments exist to prevent, and a state that wants the capability badly enough to build the subject will find it has acquired a conscript it has to pay, house, and let go.
