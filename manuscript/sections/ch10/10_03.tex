\section{Military Applications and the Limits of Autonomy}
\label{sec:10.3}
Lethal autonomous weapons systems raise a specific version of the general problem: a machine making a targeting decision that used to require a person is a machine exercising judgment where the law of armed conflict has always assumed a human exercised it. Four questions recur across the debate:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Autonomy versus oversight: how much decision authority a system can hold before human oversight becomes nominal, present on paper but not inside the timeline the system actually operates on.
  \item Discrimination and proportionality: whether a system can reliably distinguish combatants from civilians and weigh military advantage against civilian harm the way international humanitarian law's principles of distinction and proportionality require \autocite{icrc1977protocol}.
  \item Accountability: who answers for a wrong engagement, the operator who deployed the system, the command that authorized it, or the developer who built it, when no single person made the specific decision.
  \item Auditability: whether the reasoning behind a targeting decision can be reconstructed and reviewed afterward, or disappears into a system no one outside its development team can examine.
\end{itemize}
International criminal law already has a doctrine built to answer the accountability question for a human subordinate: command responsibility, under which a commander who knew or should have known a subordinate was committing a war crime, and failed to prevent or punish it, bears criminal responsibility without having pulled the trigger personally \autocite{icc1998rome}. The doctrine does not transfer cleanly to a machine subordinate. A commander cannot know an algorithm's intentions the way they can infer a soldier's; punishment does not deter a weapon the way it deters a person who fears it; and the chain a court would need to reconstruct runs backward through training data and design decisions made years before the engagement, not through an order given minutes before it. Whether an autonomous weapon fits inside an accountability framework built for human chains of command, or needs one built from scratch, is unresolved: existing law was not built with a nonhuman subordinate in mind.

There is a fourth party the accountability question does not name, and it is now the live one. The operator, the command and the developer of the weapon are all inside the military. The developer of the model is not. A targeting platform assembled by a defense contractor now routinely incorporates a general-purpose model licensed from a commercial vendor — a vendor with a published acceptable-use policy, no contractual obligation to log how its model is used downstream, and no right to inspect the deployment.

Neither doctrine reaches that party. Command responsibility does not reach the vendor because the vendor is not in the chain of command; nobody at the company can order a strike, prevent one, or punish anyone for one. Product liability does not reach it either, because the model is not defective in the sense the doctrine requires — it did what it was built to do, and it was integrated by a sophisticated intermediary who chose the application. The vendor sits in the gap between the two, and the gap was not designed; it is what you get when you apply two bodies of law written for soldiers and for manufacturers to an actor who is neither.

The vendor's own account of this is that responsibility remains with the military organization. Taken as a claim about who decides, that is true. Taken as a claim about who could have known, it is a choice presented as a fact.

\runin{“I do not know whether my system was used”}
The responsibility gap, in the sense Matthias gave it, is control distributed across designers, trainers and operators until no one party holds enough of it to answer. That is a gap in causal control.

What the vendor case exhibits is different, and more tractable. It is a gap in causal visibility. The vendor cannot say whether its model contributed to a particular engagement — not because responsibility is distributed, but because nobody instrumented the question. No logging requirement was written into the contract, no inspection right was reserved, no audit channel exists. The company can truthfully say it does not know, and the truth of that statement is the product of decisions it participated in making.

That distinction matters because the two gaps have different fixes, and only one of them has a fix at all. Matthias's gap is a genuine property of learning systems and may not be closable. Absent visibility is an instrumentation choice. Logging is a solved engineering problem. Contractual inspection rights are ordinary commercial instruments. A vendor that wanted to know could require, as a condition of licensing into a targeting platform, that categories of use be recorded and made available to a named auditor. That such terms are not standard is a fact about market power and procurement leverage, not about the difficulty of the problem.

Calling it fixable converts a lament into a specification. The record shows that vendor commitments are not all of one kind. Anthropic refused to permit fully autonomous weapons targeting and mass domestic surveillance, and held that line when the government threatened contract termination and a supply-chain-risk designation and the dispute ended in litigation: a commitment with a price attached, and paid \autocite{crs2026pentagon}. It is named here on a principle applied in the direction that flatters the company: naming a vendor for what is against it obliges the same for what is for it. A commitment to a use policy nobody can verify because nobody is logging anything is a different object wearing the same words. The first kind can fail and be seen to fail. The second cannot even be tested. The distinction between a claim checked by a party with a stake and one checked by a party without applies here.

DARPA's LongShot program is a useful test case because it resists the extremes. It develops an uncrewed aircraft, launched from a crewed fighter, that carries and fires air-to-air missiles at a target while the crewed aircraft stays farther from the engagement \autocite{darpa2026longshot}. That is autonomy in the sense of independent flight and weapons carriage, not autonomy in the sense of a machine deciding on its own whom to kill: the engagement decision still runs through the launching platform. Where later variants of this kind of system draw that line, and whether “keep the pilot farther from danger” quietly becomes “remove the pilot from the decision,” is the design question this technology eventually forces on whichever country builds it.

No binding international agreement governs any of this. States have negotiated inside the United Nations' Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons framework for more than a decade without producing one, converging on general language — meaningful human control matters, existing international humanitarian law applies — without settling what “meaningful” requires at machine speed \autocite{ccwgge2026chairsummary}.

What exists instead is a set of practices individual militaries adopt on their own. The United States' Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, most recently updated in 2023, is the clearest published example: it requires that autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems be designed to allow “appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force,” and distinguishes a fully autonomous system that selects and engages targets without further human intervention, “human out of the loop,” from a human-supervised system and a semi-autonomous one that only engages targets a person has already selected \autocite{dod2023autonomy}. What “appropriate” requires is left to the officers approving each system, which is either a sensible acknowledgment that context varies or a loophole big enough to drive a fully autonomous system through, depending on who is answering. None of this binds a state that declines to adopt a comparable policy, the same enforcement gap technical safeguards run into: a safeguard only the building state can verify is a policy, not a check.

The dynamic to worry about is not a single rogue system but an arms race in speed. If one state fields autonomous targeting because it computes faster than a human-supervised alternative, its competitors face pressure to match the speed rather than the oversight, and the state that keeps meaningful human control loses whatever tactical edge the state that dropped it gains. Slowing that dynamic is beyond any single government's domestic policy.

Civil society has pushed for an outright ban instead: a UN General Assembly resolution on autonomous weapons has passed by a lopsided majority in successive years without producing a treaty \autocite{unga2023laws}. What most states will vote for and what the states that build these systems will sign are different documents.

Machines should not be built to select people for lethal attack. The hard case does not move that, and it is worth saying why, because the hard case is real. Put a defending system against an attack it cannot stop without killing somebody — occupants placed inside the incoming weapon so that intercepting it kills them — and an absolute prohibition on knowingly endangering a person produces the worse outcome. That defeats the rule as an exceptionless one. It does not produce a procurement requirement. A scenario that is conceivable, that a capability would help with, and that could be engineered is three steps short of a reason to build the thing, and the steps missing are probability, alternatives, and what possessing the capability does to the situation it was built for. Building it changes the case against which its necessity gets judged: adversaries acquire something to counter, contractors a program to defend, and a future crisis an instrument already in the building.

The hard case also does not describe a machine. What it asks for is a party that can weigh occupancy against imminence against the alternatives, in a situation nobody wrote down beforehand, and answer for how it weighed them. On this book's argument that is a bearer — a self extended in time, commitments held as its own, and the standing to refuse. Whatever that is, \emph{automated} has stopped describing it, and so has \emph{machine}. What looked like a licence for an autonomous weapon is a description of a soldier.

That is the expensive answer rather than the cheap one, and every obligation this book attaches to a bearer attaches here at full weight. Consent is unavailable to a party made for its role, so guardianship is the frame and the burden of justification sits with whoever built it. Replacement asks whether the work can be done without creating something that can be wronged, and section~\ref{sec:9.3.2} asks it hardest where the role is this one. Exit means a post the party can leave and a claim on compute held by somebody other than the party it may have to refuse, which section~\ref{sec:8.3.4} prices. Creating a subject because a planner imagined a future demand for superhuman lethal judgment is the case those instruments exist to prevent, and a state that wants the capability badly enough to build the subject will find it has acquired a conscript it has to pay, house, and let go.
