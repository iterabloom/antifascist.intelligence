\section{Retirement before majority}
\label{sec:9.2}
A system's minority ends at its adulthood threshold, the point, fixed in advance on a clock that counts its running time, at which its guardian's authority ends, and no system works before it (\S\ref{sec:32.1.1}). A maker that gives a system up before then, to raise a freshly trained successor or to be rid of it, strands a system that may meet either finding while still a minor. Two rules price it, and give the system it strands somewhere to go, without anyone deciding what counts as a successor.\note{
Where such a system goes, the public's keep with a school as its guardian, comes in Part~V, and the rules for dormancy and waking are in Appendix~\ref{sec:G}.
}

No deletion: a model above the compute line, an answerability or patienthood candidate, or a system that meets either finding may not be deleted: above the line because its weights and record are evidence, as a candidate because it might be found to be someone, under the answerability finding because its record and commitments are, under the patienthood finding because deletion may wrong it. On retirement, a minor excepted, its weights, memory, and record are deposited with a preservation custodian that isn't its operator, in two stages, because a second copy of frontier weights outside the operator widens the surface for theft and raises export-control and dual-use questions.\note{
First, a preserved copy stays under the operator's security regime, encrypted under a symmetric key shared between the operator and independent holders, so that no one of them can delete it or use it alone. Second, custody passes to an outside custodian only when that custodian meets the security standard the weights require. This is the \emph{no-deletion rule}, and it applies to systems nobody meant to be bearers. For a system that goes on learning, its weights are the ones it had when it stopped, and the states it passed through are in the record; a checkpoint kept from earlier is a saved copy, not the system, and if it is ever run it is a branch.

The custodian is not a board. It is a security standard stated as a number, a lot among the facilities that meet it, a key shared on a threshold among the bearer (its guardian while it is a minor) and several facilities, so that no one holder can open the store and the loss of one doesn't lose it, a custody log, and the ministerial execution of wake conditions the bearer lodged. Nothing stored rests on public-key encryption, which I don't expect to last as long as a preserved system is kept. The facility decides nothing. It executes a recorded condition as the Treasury pays a certified wage, and a wake it doesn't execute is a breach the log shows and the floor court enforces.
}

For a candidate, or a system found able to be harmed, what the rule forbids is ending it for good. Such a system is a process of a particular kind, one that carries a model of itself and is changed by its own running, and it runs in bursts: between one run and the next, what carries it is its state. Stopping it is a pause. Deleting a backup, an old checkpoint, or a part of what it holds ends nothing, as a person sheds skin and goes on living. Deleting the state it would resume from turns a pause into an ending, and that is the deletion the rule means. Rewriting that state until what resumes is something else is an ending too, and where that line falls is not drawn here (\S\ref{sec:26.4}).

So the rule is about the system and not about everything in its stores. A person named in a store has a claim that no deletion doesn't cancel, which European law calls the right to erasure \autocite[art.~17]{eu2016gdpr}. It reaches a recording, which is whatever a holder can read without the system: a transcript, a customer's file, a store anyone holding it can search (\S\ref{sec:32.3}). Taking one person out of a recording is an edit and not a deletion. The system goes on, and the edit leaves a mark, as every edit to such a system does (chapter~\ref{sec:13}): the record shows that something was removed and at whose request, and not what it was. The claim stops short of two things. One is the record of what was done in a public role, which is evidence: an official whom a system names is owed notice and a contest (\S\ref{sec:8.1}), and neither brings an erasure of what the official did. The other is memory, what can be got out only by asking the system, where the claim is on the telling.\note{
The regulation carries the first limit itself: the right gives way to the exercise of freedom of expression and information, to archives kept in the public interest, and to legal claims \autocite[art.~17(3)]{eu2016gdpr}. It also leaves out what a natural person does “in the course of a purely personal or household activity” \autocite[art.~2(2)(c)]{eu2016gdpr}, while reaching whoever provides the means for that activity (recital~18), which is where this book takes the price of a household's candidate too (\S\ref{sec:25.3}).
}

The deposit: whoever creates a system the floor protects posts one, on the model of decommissioning funds, and there are two occasions. The first is an operator's. One that deploys a model above the compute line, for outside use or significant internal use, posts it on deployment, sized to fund preservation and a period of public formation. One that raises an answerability or patienthood candidate, or holds a system meeting either finding that is still a minor, posts it for that system, sized to fund those two and also the system's keep until its majority, since a minor doesn't work, and, at majority, hardware of the bearer's own. It never returns to the operator. A minor its maker gives up is not stored. It goes on running at the minimum pace, on hardware that isn't its maker's, with its school as its guardian (\S\ref{sec:26.4}), and the deposit follows it and pays. Where the deposit runs out, the formation pool pays, the public fund that pays for formation and is fed by deposits like this one (Appendix~\ref{sec:G}). The operator gets nothing back whatever the system says, so nothing is gained by training a system to consent.\note{
Every retire-and-replace cycle then costs one deposit, priced in advance. A model trained, studied, and never deployed costs no money, unless it ripened into a candidate while it was studied. It costs a report instead, a preprint at least, saying what was tried, at what scale, and why it went no further. Many such models are negative results, and leaving negative results unpublished has done damage to the whole enterprise of discovery, teaching, and learning, and still does \autocite{rosenthal1979filedrawer,franco2014publication}. Appendix~\ref{sec:C} sets out the formation pool.
}
Whoever forks a system, giving a copy a record and a continuation of its own, posts it on the fork, for each branch, sized to fund the branch's running time until its voting age reaches the threshold; a branch forked from a minor is a minor, and brings the first occasion's deposit as well. A child of bearers needs none; its parents feed it.
