\section{Authority Does Not Travel with the System}
\label{sec:travel-system}

A certification can cross a border. Authority cannot. A certificate may
establish that a system was tested against a stated process, that an
auditor inspected specified evidence, or that one regulator accepts
another regulator's finding. None of those establishes that the party
who selected the system's floor was entitled to select it for the people
subject to the deployment.

The question reopens wherever the system is deployed. A floor formed in
one jurisdiction may arrive in another with the same prohibitions and a
different operator, population, and distribution of power. Local
adaptation does not resolve the problem by itself, because adaptation is
another opportunity to amend the floor. What matters is who specified
the change, who could contest it, and who can stop the deployment if the
change places the protected party at risk. Adjustments chosen by the
operating government and adjustments chosen by the people subject to the
system may produce identical technical documentation while carrying
opposite claims to legitimacy.

Mutual recognition can reduce duplicated inspection. Technical
standards can make evidence legible to more than one regulator. Neither
supplies standing or enforcement where none existed. International
governance matters to this argument only when it assigns a party access
to evidence, authority to compel a change, or power to stop a deployment
against the wishes of the state or company operating it. The cases that
follow ask where those powers go when AI is treated as national
infrastructure, placed inside a military chain of command, or directed
at people outside the institutions meant to constrain it.
