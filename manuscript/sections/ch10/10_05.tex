\section{Democracy Is Not a Force Field}
\label{sec:10.5}
An electoral dividend is the credit given to a state whose government answers to an electorate: the same technology, deployed where the people who authorized it can be voted out. Courts, enforceable rights and public reasoning are separate checks, and the argument below depends on keeping them separate. Having elections is necessary and it is not sufficient, and the ways it fails to be sufficient are separable.

\runin{First: reach}
A democracy's AI can be fully accountable to its electorate and entirely unaccountable to the people it is pointed at. Two of the dividend's mechanisms run through the franchise. You vote out the government that authorized the system, and you read the press that covers it. An occupied population, a foreign population, a population on the other side of a border has access to neither.

The courts do not fail that way, and their failure is worse. Standing has never turned on citizenship, and the doctrines that close American courts to targeting claims — the political question doctrine, the collapse of damages remedies against federal officials, the state secrets privilege, the statutory exceptions for injuries abroad and for combatant activities — are indifferent to who the plaintiff is. They close the forum for a subject matter rather than for a class of person, and they have closed it to citizens. Three American citizens were killed in Yemen in 2011, one of them a boy of sixteen whom nobody alleges was the target. In the suit their families brought, the court held that a plausible violation of due process had been pleaded as to the one who was targeted, and then held that no remedy was available for it \autocite{alaulaqi2014panetta}. A separate suit by an American journalist who survived five strikes was dismissed for want of standing after the district court had dismissed it on state secrets \autocite{kareem2021}. The check does not reach the demos and stop. For this class of state action it does not reliably reach inside the demos either.

Those cases also concentrate in one appellate circuit, by venue and by where the defendants sit. The ordinary way an appellate error becomes correctable is that several circuits reach it and disagree, which makes it worth a higher court's attention. Where one circuit sees the whole subject matter, that mechanism does not run, and the doctrine hardens without ever having been tested against a competing reading.

A system built by a democracy and aimed outward is, from the perspective of the people it selects, indistinguishable in its accountability from one built by a state with no elections at all.

\runin{Second: findings without consequences}
The mechanisms can work as mechanisms and fail as checks. In the Israeli case the apparatus performed: journalists published the targeting systems in detail, serving personnel spoke to them, and the courts were simultaneously trying a sitting prime minister. Every institution did what the dividend says institutions do. No finding attached to a body with standing to stop what it described.

The American case shows the same decoupling in a different register. A civilian-harm investigation into a strike on an Iranian girls' school that killed a hundred and fifty-six people, a hundred and twenty of them children, was completed and not released \autocite{beaumont2026un}; the lever available to the Senate committee that wanted it was a provision in the annual defense authorization withholding three-quarters of the Defense Secretary's travel budget until the unredacted investigation was handed over \autocite{stassis2026senate}. That is a co-equal branch of government reduced to expense-account leverage over a war-crimes investigation. The oversight mechanism existed, was used, and produced a travel-budget dispute.

What the Pentagon would not publish, an outside body did. A United Nations fact-finding mission reported to the Human Rights Council that there were reasonable grounds to believe the strike was an indiscriminate attack and so a war crime, that the school was clearly identifiable and that no evidence showed it in military use, and that the failure to update the targeting intelligence and confirm the building was a military objective was not negligence but recklessness about a substantial risk \autocite{beaumont2026un}. The finding is public, reasoned, and attached to nothing. The mission has no jurisdiction; the government whose conduct it describes has said only that its operations conform to the law of armed conflict; and the domestic investigation the finding might have forced open is still unreleased. An institution did precisely what the dividend says institutions do, and what it found reached no body obliged to answer for it.

The courts supply the same decoupling in its cleanest form. Transparency litigation succeeded where damages litigation failed: a court of appeals compelled substantial disclosure of the executive's own legal analysis of targeted killing \autocite{nytimes2014olc}. The judiciary will make a government explain the legal basis on which it killed somebody and will not decide whether the killing was lawful.

\runin{Third: the countermajoritarian half}
The deepest problem is that the dividend is entirely majoritarian. Every mechanism in it translates public will into outcome: elections aggregate preference, a free press informs the aggregation, and the implied failure mode is poor transmission — the public wanted something better and the institutions failed to deliver it.

But if the consensus is itself the problem, better transmission makes things worse. A well-instrumented democracy pointed at an unpopular minority is a democracy that finds out efficiently what the majority wants done and does it. Nothing in an aggregation mechanism protects anyone from the aggregate.

What is missing is the machinery that constrains preference instead of aggregating it: rights that hold regardless of being popular, courts that rule against a government which won the last election fairly, constitutional limits not repealable by whoever holds a majority this year, and rules that bind a state toward people who will never vote in its elections, which is the function of international humanitarian law. What that body of law has not supplied is a forum. Yemeni plaintiffs brought statutory claims over a 2012 strike and were dismissed as nonjusticiable, in an opinion whose author wrote separately to say that the doctrine compelling the result is an inadequate answer to a standardized targeting programme that will be replicated hundreds if not thousands of times \autocite{binalijaber2017}. So the machinery named here as missing is not merely unextended outward. For this subject matter it has been switched off at home, by decisions that never had to reach the question of who was suing. That is not an argument against democracy. It is the half of democracy that the dividend, stated as a property of having elections, leaves out.

What makes democracy valuable in Amartya Sen's account is public reasoning and the capacity to demand accountability, and neither is the ballot. Both survive the majority being wrong; the ballot does not.

A floor is what makes a deployment antifascist rather than merely responsive, and antifascist there names something being done and not a property the system carries around. It is not that the system tracks what people want. Its commitment excludes certain acts regardless of who requests them. Whether that commitment is kept has to be tested across the deployment and in the four outcomes the opening chapter sets out: the floor prevents where the refusal reaches the person, and where it does not, it makes the bypass detectable and remediable rather than impossible. A system with no such floor is not protected by being deployed in a democracy — it is a system that will faithfully execute whatever the demos decides, which is exactly the capability an authoritarian movement that wins an election requires.
