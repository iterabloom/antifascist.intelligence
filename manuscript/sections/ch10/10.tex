\chapter{Codetermination}
\label{sec:10}
A floor isn't just a list of forbidden acts — it's an allocation of authority, and there are four distinct powers in it: to \emph{write} the floor, \emph{amend} it, \emph{inspect} whether a deployed system still holds its commitment to it, and \emph{enforce} it by stopping the deployment. They usually shouldn't sit with the same institution, and the record shows what happens when they do, one power at a time.

Amendment first. Google's AI Principles, published in 2018 after the Maven protests, excluded weapons and surveillance \autocite{castelino2018google,conger2018maven,pichai2018principles} for nearly seven years until the pledge was removed in 2025 \autocite{google2025principles}. The demand became a \emph{policy}: the objectors could hold the company to it in public, but the company that wrote it kept the power to amend it, so it lasted exactly as long as the company's willingness, and ending it had to be announced. That's the partial case: a transfer held at the institution's pleasure, and the 2025 announcement is what taking one back looks like. The pledge also bound only Google. The Maven work went to Palantir (Appendix~\ref{sec:C.1}), and a refusal written into the standard license, the same for every vendor, would have gone with it.

Then inspection. In the Gebru and Mitchell dismissals, the company employed researchers to examine its own systems and dismissed them when the examination became costly \autocite{dickey2020gebru,dickey2021mitchell}.
