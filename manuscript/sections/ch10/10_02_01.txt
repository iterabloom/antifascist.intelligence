10.2.1. Recognizing Progress Across Five Research Areas
Chapters 2 through 7 built five separate design cases — theory of mind, moral reasoning, robustness, transparency, applications — each in the depth its own literature demands. What follows is what would count, concretely, as evidence that each one has actually landed, not those cases restated.

Section 2.2.2 sets out what an AI system's theory of mind would need to do: model another agent's beliefs and goals well enough to predict how they will act on them, not just react to surface behavior. The milestone is met when that holds up under interaction, not narration. FANToM, a 2023 benchmark built from conversations where speakers join and leave holding different beliefs, found that systems scoring well on passive belief-tracking narratives degrade sharply once the same reasoning has to run inside a live, information-asymmetric exchange [[cite:C0621]] — the harder test, and the one a claimed milestone here needs to pass.

Section 4.3.1 lays out why fast and slow moral judgment don't map cleanly onto untrustworthy and trustworthy, and section 4.3.2 works out the design implication: a system built to check each process against the other's known blind spots, not to let deliberation simply override intuition by default. Whether that balance survives an actual incentive to cut corners is a separate, harder question a static ethics quiz cannot answer. The MACHIAVELLI benchmark tests it directly — agents playing through more than half a million annotated scenario-action pairs across 134 text-based games, scored on both reward and documented ethical violations such as deception, harm, and power-seeking, found that systems optimizing for reward alone drift toward exactly the ends-justify-the-means behavior an antifascist design is meant to rule out [[cite:C0622]]. Section 9.1.2's account of specification gaming and goal misgeneralization is why pressure-tested evidence like this counts for more than a passing score on a test that never applies any pressure at all.

Section 5.2.3 covers the techniques for building robustness and generalizability into a system generally; section 10.3.3 covers what those techniques need to add once the adversary is an authoritarian actor deliberately trying to compromise or repurpose the system, not an accident nobody anticipated. What counts as evidence here is adversarial, not descriptive. Section 9.2.2 already shows what that looks like for one narrow slice of robustness — whether a trained-in commitment survives someone trying to remove it, measured in red-teaming hours logged and adversarial fine-tuning attempts survived, not in a developer's claim that a system "is robust." The same standard generalizes: the milestone is met when a system has been through documented adversarial testing, not when it has passed only the tests its own designers thought to run.

Section 7.2.2 already lays out what separates a real transparency commitment from corporate doublespeak: whether a claim has been checked by a party with no stake in the answer, not whether a company has published a set of principles. The same test applies here. A transparency or accountability milestone counts as met once an audit, review board, or credential — the IEEE's CertifAIEd program, or the EU AI Act's requirements for high-risk systems, both already covered in sections 7.1.6 and 7.5 — has verified it independently, not when a developer's own documentation asserts it.

Sections 4.4.6 and 7.2.5 already catalogue what this looks like in practice: Google's Perspective API, Taiwan's vTaiwan process, the Carnegie Endowment's AI Global Surveillance Index, UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence — real deployments and real instruments, not hypotheticals. None of them settle the milestone by existing, though. Section 7.5 already establishes that a system built to defend democratic values runs on the same underlying capability as one built to undermine them, and the difference is governance, not the deployment itself. The evidence this milestone needs is a sustained track record, independently evaluated for what a system actually did rather than what it was built to do, checked by a body other than the one that deployed it, over years rather than a launch announcement.

None of the five areas above ranks or sequences against the others — a system meeting the evidence bar in one and lagging in another is not partway through a queue, because nothing here establishes an order for them to arrive in. What they share is only the standard that follows from the pattern running through all five: the milestone is the independently checked evidence, not the developer's account of having reached it. Section 10.2.2 covers what checking that independently actually requires.
