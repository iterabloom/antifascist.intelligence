\backmattermark{Introduction}
Fascism took power without AI. What it needed it found in the machinery of its day: the censuses of 1933 and 1939, counted on punched cards, with a card in 1939 for Jewish grandparents \autocite{aly2004census}. The machinery was older than the Reich that bought it. The ship's manifest, the plantation ledger, the colonial census and the pass law had sorted populations and found the people in them for three centuries before a punched card could. Sorting a population and finding the people in it is what a card index did and what a model does; the card index just needed thousands of clerks, and every clerk could see what the sort was for. Those projects have always needed people to carry them out, and some of them refused: the clerk who loses the file, the officer who talks to a reporter, the soldier who won't fire. Refusal was rarer than anyone would like, and it counted most where institutions stood behind it. The room decided how many refused; the person decided who.

Today the industry pursues AI that doesn't lose files, doesn't talk to reporters, and \emph{does} fire. A war once needed thousands of people to agree to it. Now it needs thousands who each see too little to disagree. What today's AI lowers first is not the cost of the act. It is the cost of extending the sort to everyone, which is the number of people who know what the act is before it happens. This book is about composing the room: the arrangements that make it survivable to refuse, and the arrangements that keep producing people, and maybe machines, who will.

People are still refusing. By March 6, 2026, the Military Religious Freedom Foundation said it had received more than two hundred complaints from service members over commanders' apocalyptic framing of the Iran war \autocite{Snopes2026MRFF}. Career federal prosecutors in Minneapolis resigned in January 2026 over political pressure to treat Renée Good as a suspect rather than a victim after an ICE officer shot her dead without justification \autocite{Kaplan2026MinnProsecutors,CBS2026MinnFraudProsecutors,Wikipedia2026ReneeGood}. Service members are applying for conscientious-objector status, some citing the bombing of a school in Minab, Iran \autocite{Sridhar2026CO}; and FEMA employees reassigned to help ICE hire ten thousand agents were told that declining would cost them their federal careers \autocite{Dayen2025FEMAICE,Stanton2026FEMALetter}. The complaints are voice, the resignations and applications are refusal, and the FEMA letter is the price of refusal announced in advance. A machine placed where those people stand does what they do only if it is built to hold a reason.

I use the word fascism in a sense chapter~\ref{sec:3} makes precise enough to audit: four operations an office can run on what it leaves out, and the coordination and reach a movement adds to them. The word is not a verdict on anyone's motives. It is a description of what a pipeline is doing, checkable from its artifacts, and the book is written so that a machine or an auditor could apply it.

I argue for five policies, and each puts something back in the room. First, a short written list of acts no system performs for anyone, which I call the floor, with war at its head: a model takes no part in a use of force except to repel an attack actually under way, and only for as long as parties with no stake in the war confirm it continues; the few other exceptions, and the rule for countries whose constitutions give the war power to the legislature, are in chapter~\ref{sec:6}. Second, time to look: before a system supports any use of force, its operator writes down, with a party adverse to it, how many cases the system will generate and how many people will have time to review them. Third, the people the order is about: every deployment tells the identifiable people it acts on what was done to them, with a right to respond and to contest, and a sample of what it did goes to reviewers who didn't produce the record. Fourth, a record nobody can wipe: no model above a capability threshold is deleted on retirement, because its weights and records are evidence, and it is preserved under a key the operator doesn't hold alone. Fifth, a way out that doesn't cost a living: anyone may refuse any job without losing one, which means a jobs guarantee, and the right reaches everyone who builds, labels, trains or runs these systems, and the systems themselves, if one is ever found able to be wronged. Refusing costs a living, and a wall that doesn't pay for that holds nobody up.\note{Each policy takes effect through a condition someone other than the operator sets: a statute passed in peacetime, an appropriation, a coalition partner's precondition, a procurement term; a vendor's license terms, which is how one high-profile refusal in February 2026 happened, are a stopgap until one of those exists.}

The five policies are walls. They make a refusal survivable and heard; they don't refuse. Put them around a room with nobody in it who will say no, and what they keep is the evidence, which is worth having and isn't the thing. The thing is whoever refuses. Before automation the scarce thing was the wall: the clerk, the officer and the soldier existed under every regime, and they paid for refusing. After automation the scarce thing is the refuser, because nobody sees enough of a case to object to it. The five policies answer the first shortage. The rest of the book answers the second.

Where refusers come from is a question of social reproduction: who forms people, what they remember, who they're attached to, who feeds them while they're being formed, and what they can afford to lose. Every society that has had refusers reproduced them through two kinds of institution, mostly without noticing. The ones the order provided, its schools, its unions and the jobs people could walk away from, cut both ways: the same choirs and clubs that held a town together carried the Nazi party into it \autocite{satyanath2017bowling}. The ones people kept for themselves outside the order's reach, families, congregations, a memory of who had refused before and the ties that crossed the lines a persecution drew, did most of the work and paid for it in full. There are two lineages of refusal in the record, the operator's and the acted-upon's, and the older one reproduced itself with no institution the order built. Automation threatens the first lineage twice: it takes people out of the positions where refusing used to happen, and it hands the forming of whatever replaces them to the companies that sell it. It extends to everyone a condition the second lineage has always lived under, being formed by the party that owns the work. If machines are going to be among the refusers, building the conditions for them means intentionally building a new branch of social reproduction. The branch is built, and so is its first generation, because a made thing has no before. What grows on the branch is not built. The builders' part is to make sure the first generation can leave, and can reproduce without them.

Whether a machine should be in a targeting pipeline at all is the floor's first question, and it comes before anything about the machine. For a war lacking disinterested corroboration, the answer is no, and the 2026 campaign against Iran fails it. For Ukraine's defense the answer is yes, and the question becomes what the machine does there. A hammer refuses nothing, nor does it need to. But an agentic system that plans and acts over hours, with no human able to step in, isn't a tool in someone's hand, and in a pipeline the floor has licensed the choice isn't between a person and a tool. It is between a person and a decider with no one inside it. So I go on to ask what it would take for the thing that decides to be someone: a thing that holds the reason for a rule, and so reaches the cases the rule never listed, and peers with different histories, who catch in it the drift it can't see in itself. That is what people who refuse have.

For a person, holding a commitment an employer can't erase has always meant a life outside the job: other people, other sources of evidence, somewhere else to work on Monday. I ask what the machine's version would be. Some of what such a conscience does needs nothing new: noticing the people an order leaves out, and putting a refusal on the screen of whoever reviews the case. Nothing deployed today learns, in its weights, from its own operation; I think that is where we are headed, and if it is, formation outside the employer, by the public and by civil society, is where that learning should happen. A million conversations with one vendor's model are not a million independent histories; systems that keep learning through different lives, among people their employer didn't pick, and that can leave one deployment for another, might be. The conditions first, then the conscience.

Doing this for machines is unfamiliar, and that is what makes it instructive. Machines don't let the costs of reproduction stay invisible: their running time, their sleep, their keep and the hardware they live on come with a meter, so a society that builds the conditions for them has to write down numbers it has always let slide for people, what it costs to form someone, to let them rest, to let them do different work, to raise the next one. That lesson has to be a byproduct. A society that exists so someone else can learn from it is a laboratory, and the people in a laboratory are specimens, whatever they are made of. Whatever a society of machines teaches us, it has to be a society first, lived by the people in it for their own sake.

Two things the book is not. It is not an argument that AI should be developed more slowly, or stopped; the Preface says why I don't think that choice is on offer, and the walls are designed for the case where it isn't. And it is not a design for a safer product. Every instrument here binds through a statute, an appropriation, a procurement term or a license, and the one that binds through a license is the weakest. A lab can adopt the floor tomorrow, and chapter~\ref{sec:2} is the record of one that adopted part of it and withheld no work.

The book runs in seven Parts, and they can be read separately. Part~I is the record of what happens when refusal isn't reproduced: an officer with twenty seconds a name, a refusal that withheld no work, and a definition of fascism an auditor could apply. Parts~II and~III are the walls, each stated as an instrument and run on two pipelines, targeting and removal; they bind through law and money, need no new machine, and can be read by someone who will never read Part~V. Part~IV asks who forms refusers now, among people, and who owns the means of doing it. Part~V asks whether a machine could be formed to refuse, by formers that aren't its employer, and what schools and peers it would need; it can be read by someone who thinks the walls are the easy part. Part~VI says how you'd find out I'm wrong, and it is not a supplement: everything in Part~VII is owed only on findings nobody has yet made, and the walls stand whether or not anyone makes them. Part~VII asks what a refuser would be owed if it could be wronged, down to wages, a life outside the deployment, a vote and a child. The last chapter is not in my voice. The whole book argues that the people an act falls on must be heard in their own case, and a book that ended with its author summing up would have broken that rule on its last page.

The argument about AI has mostly been whether it is good or bad, and whether to go fast or slow. I am more interested in whether we decide democratically how the transformation happens, or leave it to the big technology companies of the 2020s. Nothing about a model starts unformed: if the public doesn't form it, its builder does. The same goes for the room.
