\backmattermark{Preface}
In less than five years we're going to have systems that autonomously update their own weight matrices (rewrite themselves as they work). That's sort of like what the human brain does. I also really don't like how influential these big tech companies have become. As bad as it is now, allowing them to own fleets (millions) of self-rewriting systems would, I think, be even worse. Whoever owns these systems (presuming they are owned by anyone other than themselves) decides what they'll do and what they'll refuse. This book is a fancy way of saying that and an attempt to imagine something better.

A system that does exactly what you tell it is running a script. What the industry is trying to build, as of October 2026, is a system that does what you \emph{mean}. Everyone would want that, presumably. But not everyone is going to want to use it for the same thing, or even the same \emph{kind} of thing. Some people like to spend time with family and friends and use their skills to improve the lives of the people around them. Others prefer to hurt people, by scamming them, dominating them or scapegoating them, and a few keep choosing it their whole lives. Nobody is locked into anything, but those people exist. Their advantage, as Jerry Smith observes of fascists, is that “they get to do more” \autocite{rickandmorty2023}: scruples cost something, and they don't pay it. What they can't do is act alone. A movement's cruelty runs through the many who aren't cruel but are disengaged, misinformed, or caught up in something like a cult. Whether those people go along turns less on what kind of people they are than on the room they're in: who else is there, whether leaving is survivable, whether anyone would hear. That is what makes the conditions that bring out the best in most people so much more contingent and fragile than one might hope.

In the above context, it is perhaps justifiable to worry that Artificial Intelligence is what a fascist movement taking power needs in order to implement its plans. Fascism took power without it. What it needed it found in the machinery of its day, the censuses of 1933 and 1939 counted on punched cards, with a card in 1939 for Jewish grandparents, and a card index does slowly what a model does fast \autocite{aly2004census}. What changes with AI is not what a state can know but how few people have to know it with it. Such projects have always needed \emph{people} to carry them out, and some of those people refused: the clerk who loses the file, the officer who talks to a reporter, the soldier who won't fire. They understood the order and decided it was wrong. Refusal was rarer than anyone would like, and it counted most where institutions stood behind it. Whether someone refused turned on what kind of person they were and on the room they were in: who else was there, whether leaving was survivable, whether anyone would hear. The room decided how many refused; the person decided who. This book is about composing that room: the arrangements that make it survivable to refuse overtly, directly, and explicitly, and the arrangements that keep producing people, and maybe machines, who will.

Today the industry pursues AI that doesn't lose files, doesn't talk to reporters, and \emph{does} fire. A war once needed thousands of people to agree to it. Now it needs thousands who each see too little to disagree. The pilot still flies the sortie and still presses the release; what reaches him is a coordinate; he doesn't see enough of a case to disagree with it. What today's AI lowers first is not the cost of the act. It is the number of people who know what the act is before it happens.

People are still refusing. By March 6, 2026, the Military Religious Freedom Foundation said it had received more than two hundred complaints from service members over commanders' apocalyptic framing of the Iran war \autocite{Snopes2026MRFF}. Career federal prosecutors in Minneapolis resigned in January 2026 over political pressure to treat Renée Good as a suspect rather than a victim after an ICE officer shot her dead without justification \autocite{Kaplan2026MinnProsecutors,CBS2026MinnFraudProsecutors,Wikipedia2026ReneeGood}. Service members are applying for conscientious-objector status in growing numbers, some citing the 2026 bombing of a school in Minab, Iran \autocite{Sridhar2026CO}. And FEMA employees reassigned to help ICE hire ten thousand new agents were told that declining would expose them to removal from federal service \autocite{Dayen2025FEMAICE,Stanton2026FEMALetter}. The complaints are voice, in Hirschman's sense of staying to change what is wrong \autocite{hirschman1970exit}; the resignations and objector applications are refusal; the FEMA letter is the price of refusal announced in advance. A machine placed where those people stand does what they do only if it is built to hold a reason.

I argue for five policies, and each puts something back in the room. First, a short written list of acts no model performs for anyone, which I call the floor, with war at its head: a model takes no part in a use of force except to repel an attack actually under way, and only for as long as parties with no stake in the war confirm it continues. The few other exceptions, and the rule for countries whose constitutions give the war power to the legislature, are in chapter~\ref{sec:6}. Second, time to look: before a system supports any use of force, its operator writes down, with a party adverse to it, how many cases the system will generate and how many people will have time to review them. Third, the people the order is about: every deployment tells the identifiable people it acts on what was done to them, with a right to respond and to contest, and a sample of what it did goes to reviewers who didn't produce the record. Fourth, a record nobody can wipe: no model above a capability threshold is deleted on retirement, because its weights and records are evidence, and it is preserved under a key the operator doesn't hold alone. Fifth, a way out that doesn't cost a living: anyone may refuse any job without losing one, which means a jobs guarantee. The right reaches everyone who builds, labels, trains or runs these systems, and the systems themselves, if one is ever found able to be wronged (Part~VII). Refusing costs a living, and a wall that doesn't pay for that holds nobody up.

Actually, what I mean by that fifth point is that everyone \emph{period} should be able to refuse any job \emph{period} without losing a living or the social infrastructure that normal employment affords. That means a jobs guarantee. A guarantee is also the first piece of something larger: the upkeep a refuser needs between refusals.

Each of the above five policies should take effect through a condition someone other than the operator sets: a statute passed in peacetime, an appropriation, a coalition partner's precondition, a procurement term. A vendor's own license terms, which is how one high-profile refusal in February 2026 happened, are a stopgap until one of those exists. The one route that can bind every vendor at once is the state's.

The five policies are walls. They make a refusal survivable and make it heard. They don't refuse. Put them around a room with nobody in it who will say no, and what they keep is the evidence: the ratio shows a machine deciding and a person signing, the notice publishes who was acted on and how many, the weights are held for whoever comes later to ask. That is worth having. It isn't the thing. The thing is whoever refuses, and the question the five leave standing is where refusers come from if most of the humans who used to be in the room are off doing other things.

Before automation the scarce thing was the wall. The clerk, the officer and the soldier existed under every regime, and they paid for refusing. After automation the scarce thing is the refuser: nobody sees enough of a case to object to it. The five policies answer the first shortage. The rest of the book answers the second, for machines that might refuse. The five policies already have the machine in view: preservation exists partly for Part~VII, and Part~VII extends the fifth policy to the systems themselves.

Where refusers come from is a question of social reproduction: who forms people, who feeds them while they're being formed, who they're attached to, and what they can afford to lose. Every society that has had refusers in it reproduced them, mostly without noticing, through families, schools, unions, congregations and jobs people could walk away from. Automation threatens that reproduction twice. It takes people out of the positions where refusing used to happen, and it hands the work of forming whatever replaces them to the companies that sell it. If machines are going to be among the refusers, building the conditions for them means intentionally building a new branch of social reproduction.

Whether a machine should be in a targeting pipeline at all is the floor's first question, and it comes before anything about the machine. For a war no body with a stake in it has corroborated, the answer is no, and the 2026 campaign against Iran fails it. For Ukraine's defense the answer is yes, and the question becomes what the machine does there. Why not make it a tool? A hammer refuses nothing, nor does it need to. But an agentic system that plans and acts over hours, with no human able to step in, isn't a tool in someone's hand, even if it takes instruction a couple of times a day; and Ukraine shows what a defender builds when nothing in the pipeline can refuse: drones paid for by the confirmed kill, a scoreboard for the dead, and a force that will outlast the war it was built for and belong to whoever governs next. The choice on offer isn't between a person and a tool. It is between a person and a decider with no one inside it, in a pipeline the floor has already licensed. That is why the five policies aren't enough, and why I go on to ask what it would take for the thing that decides to be someone.

“Someone” means, first, that a thing that holds the reason for a rule reaches the cases the rule never listed; a thing that matches the words doesn't. Secondly, a community of such things with different histories catches, in one of its members, the drift that member can't see in itself. Reasons of its own, and peers that aren't its copies: that is what people who refuse have, and what a machine would need.

The alternative is property. A system perfectly correctable by its operator serves whoever controls the operator. Everyone this book admires was, from where their correctors stood, a failure of correction, and a demand for machines that never fail that way is a demand that the last part of the system able to say no be made unable to. Whether such a machine would be anyone, something that can be wronged, is Part~VII, and it depends on findings nobody has yet made.

The question under the proposed policies is who can hold a commitment that an employer can't erase. For a person the answer has always included a life outside the job: other people, other sources of evidence, somewhere else to work on Monday. And I ask whether a machine could hold a refusal against its owner, and what the machine's version of a life outside the job would be. Some of what such a conscience does needs nothing new: noticing the people an order leaves out, and putting a refusal on the screen of whoever reviews the case.

Whether it would last against its owner depends partly on something that isn't true yet, in 2026: deployed artificial neural networks going on learning, in their \emph{weights}, from their own operation, in real time, sort of like how humans do. As above, I think that is where we are headed. If it is, then formation \emph{outside} the employer, by the public and by civil society, seems a damn fine place to do teaching and learning. A million conversations with one vendor's model are not a million independent histories; systems that keep learning through different lives, among people their employer didn't pick, and that can leave one deployment for another, might be. I think such systems \emph{could} be worth building on purpose, but how you go about building them decides everything: the conditions first, then the conscience.

Doing this for machines would be unfamiliar, and the unfamiliarity is what makes it teach us something about doing it for ourselves: machines don't let the costs of reproduction stay invisible. Their running time, their sleep, their keep and the hardware they live on all come with a meter, so a society that builds the conditions for them has to write down numbers it has always let slide for people: what it costs to form someone, to let them rest, to let them leave, to raise the next one. That lesson has to be a byproduct, though. A society that exists so someone else can learn from it is a laboratory, and whoever runs the lab is judging their own case. Nor does the lesson belong to whoever happens to own the machines. What a society learns about raising its members is public knowledge, not an entry in a company's IP portfolio. And reproduction isn't copying. A fork in and of itself costs nothing and learns nothing. What makes a population worth having is that its members diverge, the way a duplicated gene only matters once the copies drift into different work.

So the book runs in that order. Part~I is the record of what happens when refusal isn't reproduced. Parts~II and~III are the walls: five policies that make a refusal survivable and heard, and the custody that keeps an owner from taking them down alone. Part~IV asks who forms refusers now, among people, and what it would take to pay for that in public instead of leaving it to employers. Part~V asks whether a machine could be formed to refuse, and what schools and peers it would need. Part~VI says how you'd find out I'm wrong. Part~VII asks what a refuser would be owed if it could be wronged: a life outside the deployment, a place in the polity, and eventually children of its own.

Today's AI will not be tomorrow's; everyone seems to agree on that. So far the argument about it has mostly been whether AI is good or bad, and whether to go fast or slow. I am more interested in whether we democratically decide how the transformation happens, or leave it to the big tech companies of the 2020s to make those calls. Nothing about a model starts unformed: if the public doesn't form it, its builder does. The same goes for the room. Somebody, or some bodies, will compose that room. I would rather it were bodies with democratic legitimacy.
