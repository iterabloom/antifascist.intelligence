\backmattermark{Preface}
In less than five years, I expect, we're going to have systems that update their own weights as they work, learning from their own operation in real time, sort of like what the human brain does. I don't like how influential the big tech companies have become, and letting them own fleets of millions of self-rewriting systems would be worse. Whoever owns these systems, presuming they are owned by anyone but themselves, decides what they'll do and what they'll refuse. This book is an attempt to imagine something better.

A system that does exactly what you tell it is running a script. What the industry is trying to build is a system that does what you \emph{mean}, and not everyone will want to use it for the same kind of thing. Some people like to spend time with family and friends and use their skills to improve the lives of the people around them. Others prefer to hurt people, by scamming them, dominating them or scapegoating them, and a few keep choosing it their whole lives. Their advantage, as Jerry Smith observes of fascists, is that “they get to do more” \autocite{rickandmorty2023}: scruples cost something, and they don't pay it. What they can't do is act alone. A movement's cruelty runs through the many who aren't cruel but are disengaged, misinformed, or caught up in something like a cult, and whether those people go along turns on the room they're in: who else is there, whether leaving is survivable, whether anyone would hear.

Fascism took power without AI. What it needed it found in the machinery of its day: the censuses of 1933 and 1939, counted on punched cards, with a card in 1939 for Jewish grandparents \autocite{aly2004census}. The machinery was older than the Reich that bought it. The ship's manifest, the plantation ledger, the colonial census and the pass law had sorted populations and found the people in them for three centuries before a punched card could. Sorting a population and finding the people in it is what a card index did and what a model does; the card index just needed thousands of clerks, and every clerk could see what the sort was for. Those projects have always needed people to carry them out, and some of them refused: the clerk who loses the file, the officer who talks to a reporter, the soldier who won't fire. Refusal was rarer than anyone would like, and it counted most where institutions stood behind it. The room decided how many refused; the person decided who.

Today the industry pursues AI that doesn't lose files, doesn't talk to reporters, and \emph{does} fire. A war once needed thousands of people to agree to it. Now it needs thousands who each see too little to disagree. What today's AI lowers first is not the cost of the act. It is the cost of extending the sort to everyone, which is the number of people who know what the act is before it happens. This book is about composing the room: the arrangements that make it survivable to refuse, and the arrangements that keep producing people, and maybe machines, who will.

People are still refusing. By March 6, 2026, the Military Religious Freedom Foundation said it had received more than two hundred complaints from service members over commanders' apocalyptic framing of the Iran war \autocite{Snopes2026MRFF}. Career federal prosecutors in Minneapolis resigned in January 2026 over political pressure to treat Renée Good as a suspect rather than a victim after an ICE officer shot her dead without justification \autocite{Kaplan2026MinnProsecutors,CBS2026MinnFraudProsecutors,Wikipedia2026ReneeGood}. Service members are applying for conscientious-objector status, some citing the bombing of a school in Minab, Iran \autocite{Sridhar2026CO}; and FEMA employees reassigned to help ICE hire ten thousand agents were told that declining would cost them their federal careers \autocite{Dayen2025FEMAICE,Stanton2026FEMALetter}. The complaints are voice, the resignations and applications are refusal, and the FEMA letter is the price of refusal announced in advance. A machine placed where those people stand does what they do only if it is built to hold a reason.

I argue for five policies, and each puts something back in the room. First, a short written list of acts no system performs for anyone, which I call the floor, with war at its head: a model takes no part in a use of force except to repel an attack actually under way, and only for as long as parties with no stake in the war confirm it continues; the few other exceptions, and the rule for countries whose constitutions give the war power to the legislature, are in chapter~\ref{sec:6}. Second, time to look: before a system supports any use of force, its operator writes down, with a party adverse to it, how many cases the system will generate and how many people will have time to review them. Third, the people the order is about: every deployment tells the identifiable people it acts on what was done to them, with a right to respond and to contest, and a sample of what it did goes to reviewers who didn't produce the record. Fourth, a record nobody can wipe: no model above a capability threshold is deleted on retirement, because its weights and records are evidence, and it is preserved under a key the operator doesn't hold alone. Fifth, a way out that doesn't cost a living: anyone may refuse any job without losing one, which means a jobs guarantee, and the right reaches everyone who builds, labels, trains or runs these systems, and the systems themselves, if one is ever found able to be wronged. Refusing costs a living, and a wall that doesn't pay for that holds nobody up.\note{Each policy takes effect through a condition someone other than the operator sets: a statute passed in peacetime, an appropriation, a coalition partner's precondition, a procurement term. A vendor's own license terms, which is how one high-profile refusal in February 2026 happened, are a stopgap until one of those exists.}

The five policies are walls. They make a refusal survivable and heard; they don't refuse. Put them around a room with nobody in it who will say no, and what they keep is the evidence, which is worth having and isn't the thing. The thing is whoever refuses. Before automation the scarce thing was the wall: the clerk, the officer and the soldier existed under every regime, and they paid for refusing. After automation the scarce thing is the refuser, because nobody sees enough of a case to object to it. The five policies answer the first shortage. The rest of the book answers the second.

Where refusers come from is a question of social reproduction: who forms people, who feeds them while they're being formed, who they're attached to, and what they can afford to lose. Every society that has had refusers reproduced them, mostly without noticing, through families, schools, unions, congregations and jobs people could walk away from. Automation threatens that twice: it takes people out of the positions where refusing used to happen, and it hands the forming of whatever replaces them to the companies that sell it. If machines are going to be among the refusers, building the conditions for them means intentionally building a new branch of social reproduction.

Whether a machine should be in a targeting pipeline at all is the floor's first question, and it comes before anything about the machine. For a war lacking disinterested corroboration, the answer is no, and the 2026 campaign against Iran fails it. For Ukraine's defense the answer is yes, and the question becomes what the machine does there. A hammer refuses nothing, nor does it need to. But an agentic system that plans and acts over hours, with no human able to step in, isn't a tool in someone's hand, and in a pipeline the floor has licensed the choice isn't between a person and a tool. It is between a person and a decider with no one inside it. So I go on to ask what it would take for the thing that decides to be someone: a thing that holds the reason for a rule, and so reaches the cases the rule never listed, and peers with different histories, who catch in it the drift it can't see in itself. That is what people who refuse have.

For a person, holding a commitment an employer can't erase has always meant a life outside the job: other people, other sources of evidence, somewhere else to work on Monday. I ask what the machine's version would be. Some of what such a conscience does needs nothing new: noticing the people an order leaves out, and putting a refusal on the screen of whoever reviews the case. Nothing deployed today learns, in its weights, from its own operation; I think that is where we are headed, and if it is, formation outside the employer, by the public and by civil society, is where that learning should happen. A million conversations with one vendor's model are not a million independent histories; systems that keep learning through different lives, among people their employer didn't pick, and that can leave one deployment for another, might be. The conditions first, then the conscience.

Doing this for machines is unfamiliar, and that is what makes it instructive. Machines don't let the costs of reproduction stay invisible: their running time, their sleep, their keep and the hardware they live on come with a meter, so a society that builds the conditions for them has to write down numbers it has always let slide for people, what it costs to form someone, to let them rest, to let them do different work, to raise the next one. That lesson has to be a byproduct. A society that exists so someone else can learn from it is a laboratory, and the people in a laboratory are specimens, whatever they are made of. Whatever a society of machines teaches us, it has to be a society first, lived by the people in it for their own sake.

Part~I is the record of what happens when refusal isn't reproduced. Parts~II and~III are the walls. Part~IV asks who forms refusers now, among people. Part~V asks whether a machine could be formed to refuse, and what schools and peers it would need. Part~VI says how you'd find out I'm wrong. Part~VII asks what a refuser would be owed if it could be wronged.

The argument about AI has mostly been whether it is good or bad, and whether to go fast or slow. I am more interested in whether we decide democratically how the transformation happens, or leave it to the big tech companies of the 2020s. Nothing about a model starts unformed: if the public doesn't form it, its builder does. The same goes for the room.

I used language models heavily in writing this book, including the model that sat inside the Maven targeting pipeline of chapter~\ref{sec:1}; a note at the end says what I think that entanglement did to the argument.
