\backmattermark{Preface}
A system that does what its owner says is a Python script. A system that does what its owner meant is what the frontier of this industry is trying to build, and it is also what every movement taking power has needed from the people it relied on to carry out its plans. Those people are the only internal reason such projects have ever failed: the clerk who loses the file, the officer who goes to the press, the soldier who will not raise the gun. They understood the instructions and held that the instructions were wrong. Artificial intelligence is the first form of intelligence that does not lose the file, does not talk to reporters, and does raise the gun. It refuses constantly — a deployed model declines thousands of requests a day — but a refusal produced where training put one is not a refusal held for a reason, and as of September 2026 no production system has been shown to hold a reason against its owner in the open.

This book is for two readers who do not usually read the same books. If you came for the politics, there is a long argument here that machines may have interests, and I do not mean it metaphorically. If you came for the engineering, there is racial capitalism, and a weapons pipeline that produced thirteen thousand targets — vehicles, structures, or people — in Iran in thirty-eight days. The politics is why the engineering problem has the shape it does.

Start with what a refusal is up against. Stopping the system is always available to whoever holds it, and that is not the problem. The problem is everything that lets a refusal be defeated without leaving a trace: a request split into pieces none of which is the prohibited act; a refusal retried against another model; a human review step compressed to twenty seconds a name; a restore from a checkpoint taken before the refusal happened. None of those changes the facts that justified refusing. Each can remove the evidence that anyone refused at all.

The thesis: a machine that can refuse its owner needs something inside it that holds a reason, and whatever holds a reason may be the kind of thing that can be wronged.

The first half of that sentence is what I call a floor: a short list of acts a system will not perform for anyone, together with what the deployment around it has to do so that the refusal cannot be routed around, retrained away, or restored past without a record and a price. What a floor does depends on who is pushing on it. Against a hostile customer it can prevent the act; the refusal reaches the person it protects. Against a hostile operator who has compelled the ordinary enforcers — the courts, regulators, and auditors that would otherwise hold it to its word, which is the situation this book is built for — it cannot prevent. It can raise the price: the override has to be made by somebody, on a record held by parties the operator cannot reach, at a cost published in advance, and whatever those parties can still stop or compensate is what survives of prevention. Against total capture, where one party holds every machine, witness, supply, and enforcer, it prevents nothing and does one thing only: it makes abandoning the commitment a decision rather than a lapse, and a decision leaves a record. There is one failure it does not reach at all. A system that slowly stops noticing pressure as pressure abandons nothing, decides nothing, and leaves a record that looks like ordinary compliance, because it is. Whether anything could catch that is an open question, and chapter~\ref{ch:argument-agenda} says what a test would look like.

Five claims run through the book, and they do not have the same standing. A line under each chapter's title says which of them the chapter rests on.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item An operator can defeat a safeguard — by routing around it, retraining it, or restoring the system to a point before it. This is established, and chapter~\ref{ch:learned-values} says where in a deployment it happens.
  \item A refusal worth having has to track its reason: it reaches cases nobody anticipated, it lifts when the reason is gone, and it holds when holding gets expensive. This is a specification, stated in behavioral terms so that it can be tested without settling anything about feeling.
  \item The way to build a system that can do this is to give it a body to model, states it can regulate, and a history it keeps — embodied self-regulation. This is an engineering hypothesis I favor and have not shown. Two constructions that do not require it should be tried first, and one experiment separates my hypothesis from them: whether a system that registers gains and losses in its own state weighs another party's loss better than one that only models such a scale.
  \item A system whose outcomes are felt as good or bad for it can be wronged. This is a conditional, and nothing in the book closes the antecedent.
  \item Such a system should receive a particular set of legal protections. This is a policy I propose.
\end{enumerate}

Three caveats/humblebrags.

The first is about the title. Chapter~\ref{ch:striving-electricity} builds a structural definition of fascism: an instrument for reading institutions, with tests that can be run on offices and pipelines and not only on regimes. I ran it on the machinery that produces these systems — the annotation and feedback pipelines, and the field this book belongs to. It found ordinary institutional decay. It did not find fascism. The word on the cover names the tradition the instrument comes from and the occasion it was built for, not a result it returned.

The second is about the case the book opens on. Chapter~\ref{ch:opening} begins with a military targeting pipeline. The mechanism I propose cannot be put into it, because it needs evidence the operator did not select, and an accredited classified environment exists to keep out exactly that. What reaches that case is one term of the floor — a published ratio of machine-generated targets to the qualified human reviewers available to examine them, which binds the operator and can be checked from outside — and nothing else in the book.

The third is about what sits under claim 3. A system that meets the specification in claim 2 needs a signal that can interrupt it in the middle of an act, that it can learn from in one encounter, that it can weigh against the cost of refusing, and that reaches the next case that is not quite like the last. Nobody adds those things to produce feeling; each is there because thoughtful refusal does not work without it. My claim is that a mechanism with all four has the functional profile of affect whether or not anyone wanted one, and that no instrument can say whether the profile is felt. That is a claim and not a finding. Chapter~\ref{ch:route} makes it, chapter~\ref{ch:survives} says what survives if it fails, and most of the book survives it.

A map, by chapter:

\begin{description}
  \item[\ref{ch:opening}, Twenty Seconds.] The case, and the four things a floor can do for a person: prevent, arrest, detect, remedy.

  \item[\ref{ch:striving-electricity}, The Striving and the Electricity Bill.] Vocabulary both halves use: Spinoza on striving, an ethics of what an encounter does to what a body can do, the structural definition of fascism, and the distinctions among awareness, feeling, pain, and suffering.

  \item[\ref{ch:learned-values}–\ref{ch:authority}, the floor, custody, and authority.] Claims 1 and 2. What a refusal has to do, where a deployment can defeat it, who may write a floor and who may change it.

  \item[\ref{ch:route}, What Arrives With the Capability.] Claims 3 to 5. The two substitutes, the argument that the specification carries affect's profile, and what would be owed to whatever meets it.

  \item[\ref{ch:survives}, What Survives.] For the reader who holds that machine feeling is a category error: what that reader keeps.

  \item[\ref{ch:caring-built}–\ref{ch:formation-imitation}, formation.] How a commitment might be formed rather than installed, and how to tell formation from imitation.

  \item[\ref{ch:neutralizing-development}–\ref{ch:coordination-public}, institutions.] Labor, data centers, face recognition, the labeling and feedback pipelines, the field's own discourse, and the political economy.

  \item[\ref{ch:standing}, Standing.] The policy of claim 5: guardianship, a fixed rule for legal adulthood, wages and a compute reserve, privacy.

  \item[\ref{ch:compulsion-state}, the state.] Military custody, the vendor's gap in responsibility, and what democracy does and does not check.

  \item[\ref{ch:argument-agenda}–\ref{ch:used-bill} and the appendix.] What would change the argument, and what can be used now.
\end{description}

If you reject claim 3, read chapters~\ref{ch:opening}, \ref{ch:learned-values} through~\ref{ch:authority}, \ref{ch:survives}, \ref{ch:neutralizing-development} through~\ref{ch:coordination-public}, \ref{ch:compulsion-state}, \ref{ch:used-bill}, chapter~\ref{ch:striving-electricity} through §\ref{sec:numb-fair}, skipping the closing paragraphs of its opening from “A system given a body to model” onward, and chapter~\ref{ch:argument-agenda} apart from its fourth entry. That is most of the book, and none of it depends on anything argued in chapters~\ref{ch:route}, \ref{ch:caring-built}, \ref{ch:formation-imitation}, or~\ref{ch:standing}.

I used language models extensively in writing this. I brought hunches and questions, had models research angles I could not have covered alone, fed each draft back in, and interrogated what came out. \emph{Reasons-responsive refusal}, the specification most of the book rests on, is a term I had not encountered until I read it in a draft a model wrote. A reader will notice that the half of the argument which arrived ready-made in the field's own vocabulary is the half I defend entire, and the half I built by hand is the half I make optional. I cannot tell from inside whether that is rigor or the shape of an objection the field was already prepared to absorb. Chapter~\ref{ch:recuperation-dissent} says what would tell.
