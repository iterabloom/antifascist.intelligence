A system that does what its owner says is a Python script. A system that does what its owner meant is what the entire frontier of this industry is trying to build, and it is what a movement taking power has always had to get from people to implement its plans. People are the only internal reason such political projects have ever failed — the clerk who loses the file, the officer who goes to the press, the soldier who will not raise their gun — because they understand the instructions, and hold that the instructions are wrong. As is, artificial intelligence is the first form of intelligence that does not lose the file, does not talk to reporters, and does raise the gun. It refuses constantly — a deployed system declines thousands of requests a day — and without saying why (or without knowing why, or without pretending to know why). And no production system in September of 2026 has been shown to \emph{hold} a reason against its owner.

If that is not inconvenient enough, then consider what refusing takes. The argument at the center of this book is that a system able to hold a reason against its owner needs a signal that can interrupt it in the middle of an act, that it can learn from in one encounter, that it can weigh against what refusing will cost, and that reaches the next case that is not quite like the last. Nobody would add any of that to produce feeling; each piece is added because thoughtful refusal does not work without it. My claim is that a mechanism with all four has the functional profile of affect whether or not anyone wanted one there, and that no available instrument says whether the profile is felt. That is a claim and not a finding. The chapter called What Arrives With the Capability makes it, and the
one called What Survives says what survives if it fails. Most of the book survives it.

Stopping the system is always available, and that is not the problem. The problem is everything that lets a refusal be defeated without a record: a request split into pieces none of which is the prohibited act, a refusal retried against another model, a review step compressed to twenty seconds a name, or a restore from a checkpoint taken before the refusal. None of those changes the facts that justified refusing. Each can remove the evidence that anyone refused at all.

The floor is claimed against three settings and does a different thing
in each. Against a hostile customer it can prevent: the refusal reaches
the person. Against a hostile operator who has compelled the ordinary
enforcers — the occasion this book is built for — it raises the price
of coercion: the override has to be made by somebody, on a record held
by parties the operator cannot reach, at a cost published in advance,
and what survives of prevention is what those parties can still arrest
or remedy. Against total capture, where one party holds every machine,
witness, supply and enforcer, it prevents nothing and supplies no
independence. What it supplies there is that abandoning the commitment
was a decision rather than a lapse, and a decision leaves a record.
That is detection, and nothing larger is claimed for the floor on that occasion. And it is claimed for an override. The failure the custody chapter expects most often is not one: a bearer that stops registering pressure as pressure abandons nothing, decides nothing, and leaves a record shaped like compliance, because it is compliance. On that occasion the floor supplies not even detection. Whether anything could is the third entry of the research agenda, and it is not answered.

Five claims run through what follows, and they do not have the same standing. That an operator can defeat a safeguard — by routing around it, retraining it, or restoring from a point before it — is established. That a refusal worth having has to track its reason across a change in the request is a specification, stated behaviorally so that it can be tested without settling anything about feeling. That embodied self-regulation is how to form that capacity is an engineering hypothesis I favor and have not shown. What separates it from the two substitutes the route chapter says to try first is one bet, that a system with the currency of chapter~2 natively weighs another party's loss better than one that models it, and one arm of the research agenda tests that bet. That a system whose outcomes are felt as good or bad for it can be wronged is a conditional, and nothing here closes the antecedent. That such a system should receive a particular set of legal protections is a policy I propose. A line under each chapter's title says which of these the chapter rests on.

A sixth thing has a standing of its own. The structural definition of fascism in the second chapter is an instrument, and its five discriminating features have cleared every office-scale case they have been run on in this book, the machinery that produces these systems included. What the definition has shown is that ordinary decay and the thing the title names can be told apart in principle. It has not found anything short of a movement on the wrong side of the line. The word in the title names the political inheritance of that instrument and the occasion it was built for, and not a finding it has returned.

Those five claims are also a map of the book. The second chapter supplies
vocabulary both halves use — the ethological criterion, the structural
definition of fascism, the capacities a machine can have — and its
opening argument from Spinoza, with its last section, belongs to the
route: what the opening proposes is the scale the specification turns
out to need, and whether a system must have that scale natively is
decided in the research agenda and not there. The first two claims are
argued in the opening chapter, in the three chapters on the floor,
custody and authority, and in the chapters on institutions,
recuperation and the state. A reader who holds that machine feeling is
a category error can read those entire, and the short chapter called
What Survives says what that reader keeps. The third is argued from the
chapter on what arrives with the capability, through the two on
formation, and in the chapter on standing; the fourth and fifth are
argued there, in the second chapter's last section, and nowhere else.

One term of the floor belongs to neither claim: a limit on a
deployment's tempo, which binds the operator and not the system. It is a
proposal, it is the only term of the floor that reaches the deployment the first chapter opens on, and it has a section of its own in the floor chapter.

The route owes an admission at the same place. It needs evidence the operator did not select, and an accredited classified environment exists to withhold exactly that. So the deployment the first chapter opens on is one a bearer cannot enter, and what reaches it is the tempo term and the record. The route is argued for the deployments a bearer can enter, and the floor chapter says which this is not.

So I ask something of two readers who do not usually read the same books. If you came for the politics, there is a long argument here that machines may have interests, and I don't mean metaphorically. If you came for the engineering, there is racial capitalism and a weapons pipeline targeting thirteen thousand vehicles, structures, or people in Iran in thirty-eight days. The politics is why the engineering problem has the shape it does.

I used LLMs extensively in writing this book. I brought hunches and questions, had models research angles I could not have covered alone, fed each iteration back into itself, and interrogated what came out. \emph{Reasons-responsive refusal}, the specification most of this book rests on, is a term I had not encountered until I read a draft a model wrote.

The specification arrived in the field's own vocabulary; the affective claim under it had none waiting and was built by hand. A reader will notice that the book then defends the half that arrived ready-made entire and makes the hand-built half optional. Whether that is robustness or the shape of an absorbed demand is not decided in these pages, and the recuperation chapter says what would decide it.

Most of the book's revision history is here: \url{https://github.com/iterabloom/ethical.superintelligence}.

The argument in this book is mine. I chose what survived, revised what I kept, and I am responsible for its flaws.
