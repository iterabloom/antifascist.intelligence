5.6.3. Balancing AI Autonomy and Alignment with Humane Values
Autonomy and alignment pull in opposite directions almost by definition: the more independently a system acts, the less each individual action can be checked against human judgment before it happens. Closing that gap without collapsing autonomy into constant supervision is the operational version of the question 4.6.2 left open — and it is the same shape of trade-off section 5.3 covers at the level of a single judgment, fast and autonomous against slow and deliberate.

<<h>>Value Alignment Methods<</h>>
One method for shaping that alignment is cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (CIRL), formalized by Dylan Hadfield-Menell and colleagues [[cite:C0030]]. Rather than treating a human's reward as a fixed, already-known target to execute against, the AI treats it as unknown and to be inferred jointly, through interaction, with the human as a cooperating partner in a shared-goal game rather than a passive source read once and acted on (section 4.2.2). What matters for autonomy specifically is the structural consequence: a CIRL agent's model of what it is optimizing for stays open to revision for as long as the interaction continues, which is a different shape of autonomy than an agent executing a reward function fixed once at training time and never revisited.

Intrinsic and extrinsic mechanisms can also be paired directly inside a single system. An AI system managing a city's electric grid might carry an intrinsic drive toward supply stability — keeping the grid up is the system's own standing objective — paired with an extrinsic reward for favoring renewable sources over fossil ones. Neither objective alone produces a grid that is both reliable and low-carbon; run together, each constrains where the other is allowed to cut corners.

Autonomy also needs boundaries on what a system is allowed to try while it is still learning. Constrained policy optimization, developed by Joshua Achiam and colleagues [[cite:C0347]], lets a reinforcement-learning agent search for better policies while remaining inside predetermined constraints throughout the search, not just at the end of it — safe exploration, rather than exploration followed afterward by a safety check.

A related approach treats alignment as ongoing rather than a target hit once: a system that keeps adjusting its behavior from continued interaction with the people it works alongside stays aligned with values that themselves shift, instead of freezing its judgment at the point it was trained.

<<h>>AI Autonomy and Antifascist Values<</h>>
Autonomy that is not grounded in antifascist values is a capability without a governor. A content-moderation system that operates without continuous human review is autonomous in the straightforward sense — but the same independence that makes it efficient also lets it drift into over-censorship, or get steered toward favoring one ideology's speech over another's, with nobody in the loop to catch the pattern until it is already established.

Building autonomy that stays inside antifascist bounds means designing for fairness, transparency, and accountability from the start rather than auditing for their absence afterward, and testing the design under adversarial pressure: red-teaming a system, deliberately simulating the ways it could be misused, before deployment rather than after a misuse turns up in the wild. It also means keeping people other than a system's own developers in the design process — ethicists, users, and representatives of the communities most exposed to that system's failure modes — since the group that built a system is a poor judge, on its own, of whose freedoms it might end up infringing.

None of this substitutes for a genuine override. A human able to intervene in a high-stakes case, and a system built to accept that intervention rather than resist it, is what keeps autonomy answerable to something outside itself. That is one of three places this book arrives at the same requirement from a different direction; chapter 3 collects them and asks what it would take to build the thing they all point at.

<<h>>Safeguards Against AI Systems Enabling Authoritarian Regimes<</h>>
Keeping AI systems from being weaponized by authoritarian regimes takes safeguards built in deliberately, not bolted on after a misuse is already discovered:

<<list>>
- Transparency, accountability, and explainability by design: routine third-party audits and legally required disclosure of AI use in the public sector make abuse recognizable and punishable.
- Ethical guidelines and legislation, developed jointly by governments, industry, and researchers, that name specifically which AI uses count as discriminatory, repressive, or otherwise off-limits, rather than gesturing at "responsible AI" in the abstract.
- Due diligence on exports: a system built for crowd monitoring or population surveillance carries a different risk in a country with a documented history of human-rights abuse, and a risk assessment that ignores that history is not really a risk assessment.
- Cross-sector collaboration — the Partnership on AI [[cite:C0348]] is one existing venue for it — that pools the perspective of companies, researchers, and civil-society groups rather than leaving any one of them to set the standard alone.
- AI literacy among developers, policymakers, and the public, since a safeguard nobody understands is a safeguard nobody can invoke.
<</list>>

<<h>>Strategies for Developing AI Systems Committed to Democratic Principles<</h>>
Strategies for keeping AI development itself committed to democratic principles overlap with the safeguards above but add two things the list does not already cover.

Democratic value alignment folds fairness directly into the model: fairness-aware machine learning and debiasing techniques, applied during development and re-checked as data and deployment context change, catch discrimination in a system's judgments rather than only in its stated policy. International cooperation extends the safeguards past any single country's jurisdiction — cross-border standards and shared research on authoritarian misuse matter because an export ban or an ethics guideline that stops at one nation's border does nothing about a regime that can simply buy the same technology from a supplier with no such rule.

A handful of deployed systems illustrate what this looks like in practice:

<<list>>
- Full Fact, an AI-assisted fact-checking service that flags false claims in public circulation [[cite:C0349]].
- Apple's on-device differential-privacy implementation, which lets the company learn population-level usage patterns without exposing what any individual user did [[cite:C0350]].
- Google's Perspective API, which scores text for the likelihood it reads as toxic or harassing, used to flag content for moderation rather than to censor it outright [[cite:C0351]].
<</list>>
