5.4.1. Identifying Authoritarian Applications of AI Technology
Authoritarian misuse of AI is not one problem but several, spread across surveillance, censorship, opinion manipulation, and infrastructure control. Six forms of it are documented well enough to name specifically:
<<list>>
1. Algorithmic social scoring: using AI-driven analytics to track and rank individual behavior against government-defined standards of "trustworthiness," with rewards or restrictions attached to the result. China's system is the most-cited example, though in practice it is a patchwork of court blacklists, tax-compliance ratings, and local pilot programs rather than the single national score it is often described as [[cite:C0370]].

2. Mass biometric surveillance: deploying facial recognition at population scale to track a targeted group. Authorities in Xinjiang built a platform that pulls data from checkpoints, cameras, and phone scans into one system built to flag anyone who falls outside a computed behavioral baseline, and used it to monitor the region's Uighur population [[cite:C0371]].

3. Psychographic micro-targeting: building detailed behavioral profiles from personal data to target propaganda or manipulate opinion at the individual level. Cambridge Analytica's harvesting of Facebook data to build voter profiles for the 2016 US presidential campaign, and its work around the Brexit referendum, is the case that made the technique widely known, though how much either vote was actually affected remains disputed [[cite:C0372]].

4. Automated censorship: using AI-driven content moderation to suppress dissent at a scale no human review team could match. China's Great Firewall is the standing example: automated classifiers now sit alongside its original keyword- and IP-blocking infrastructure, scoring and throttling content at machine speed [[cite:C0373]].

5. Synthetic disinformation: generating deepfake audio or video to manufacture events that never happened. Two days before Slovakia's 2023 parliamentary election, a fabricated audio recording appeared to catch an opposition leader discussing vote-rigging with a journalist; it spread during the country's mandatory pre-election media silence, when no outlet was legally permitted to debunk it before voting began [[cite:C0374]].

6. Repurposed dual-use systems: turning AI infrastructure built for one purpose toward surveillance or population control instead — a recommendation algorithm designed to hold attention retuned to amplify a regime's preferred narrative, an image-recognition system built for one task redirected to track protesters. Section 4.4.6 develops the underlying point: the difference is rarely in the technology, since the same capability serves a legitimate use and an authoritarian one alike; it is in who controls the system and what they are permitted to do with what it finds.
<</list>>
Each of these needs a different countermeasure — a system built to resist mass biometric surveillance does not need the same safeguards as one built to catch a fabricated recording — which is why 5.4.2 and 5.4.3 treat the technical and policy responses separately rather than as one general call to do better.
