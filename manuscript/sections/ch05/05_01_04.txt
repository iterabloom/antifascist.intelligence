5.1.4. Human Dignity, and One Indicator That Can Be Audited
Respecting human dignity through technology means applying AI in ways that strengthen human capability and community ties, in a domain where the same tools can as easily substitute for them. Three applications make the case concretely: helping workers organize, strengthening the welfare state, and countering the conditions that let authoritarian movements take hold.

AI can be a real asset to labor organizing. A system that surfaces patterns across large volumes of employment data — wage gaps, unsafe conditions, retaliatory scheduling — turns an information asymmetry that currently favors employers into something workers can act on. A tool that walks workers through the unionization process, answers labor-law questions, and helps draft a collective-bargaining agreement grounded in precedent lowers the practical barrier to organizing. A system built this way, drawing on the historical record of past labor disputes and their outcomes, could ground its recommendations in what has actually worked in comparable workplaces.

The same capability applies to the welfare state. An AI system that matches people to the social services they're eligible for, speeds up benefit applications, and evaluates which programs are actually working can make a welfare system more accessible without waiting on a redesign of the underlying bureaucracy. Applied to homelessness, poverty, and food insecurity specifically, the same pattern-matching that finds eligibility can flag where need is building before it turns into crisis, giving policymakers room to act ahead of it.

The same predictive capacity, aimed differently, is often said to be able to identify the material conditions that let authoritarian movements gain ground — rising inequality, eroding trust in institutions, the specific kinds of social fracture that precede political capture — early enough that a targeted response is still possible. Treat that as an aspiration with a poor track record. Forecasting political instability from social and economic indicators is an old field with modest results, and the claim here should be read as naming a research direction rather than a capability to design around (section 9.1.6).

It is easy to write that sentence and much harder to name an indicator, and a book that keeps promising the right indicators without producing one is not being serious. So here is one, and it is deliberately narrow.

<<h>>Measure targets per hour against reviewers available<</h>>
Wherever an AI system generates candidate decisions that a human is nominally required to approve — welfare eligibility, deportation referrals, content removals, military targets — the ratio between the rate at which the system produces candidates and the number of qualified people available to examine them is a measurable, auditable property of the deployment. It requires no access to the model and no judgment about intent. It says directly whether the human check is a check or a formality, because past a certain ratio the reviewer's only available behavior is approval. Section 6.4.1 shows what that ratio looks like when it has been allowed to run: a reviewer with twenty seconds per case, describing themselves as a stamp.

The welfare case is not hypothetical and the record on it is older than the current systems. Virginia Eubanks's study of automated eligibility in Indiana documents what the ratio above produces when it is allowed to run: a system that converted any error anywhere in an application into a denial for "failure to cooperate," issued those denials at scale, tripled the state's error rate in refusing benefits to people entitled to them, and was ended by contract termination after the damage was done [[cite:C0740]]. Two things in that record matter here. The denial reason was a category that could not be contested, because it named no specific failure to answer — which is section 7.1's structure arriving in a benefits office a decade early. And nothing about the system was secret: what was missing was any party positioned to compare the rate of decisions against the capacity to review them, which is the indicator this section proposes and the reason for proposing one that requires no access to the model.

The indicator generalizes because the failure it detects is architectural and not political. It applies to a benefits agency and a targeting cell on identical terms, and an institution that refuses to publish it is telling you something. An early-warning system built around indicators of this kind would not stop a determined authoritarian movement on its own, but it changes the odds by giving democratic institutions time to respond to root causes while a response is still cheap. Section 9.1.6 is candid about how much further this has to go before the broader claim — that a system can recognize authoritarian tactics as such — is something I can specify and not merely assume.

None of this works if the AI stays a tool wielded by whoever already holds power over workers, welfare recipients, or vulnerable populations. Built to shift leverage toward the people affected instead, it becomes a design commitment. Good intentions alone leave it a side effect.
