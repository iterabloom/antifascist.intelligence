\subsection{Moral Emotions: Empathy, Guilt, and Shame}
\label{sec:5.2.1}
Guilt and shame are self-conscious emotions: they fire when a person judges their own action, or their whole character, against a standard they have already accepted. What they add to moral judgment, beyond the information about another's state that empathy supplies, is a self-directed penalty for one's own conduct.

Guilt attaches to an act (“I did something wrong”); shame attaches to the self (“I am something wrong”). Both motivate correction, and they motivate it differently: guilt predicts reparative behavior, shame predicts withdrawal and defensiveness \autocite{tangney2007moral}. An AI system built to learn from an ethical misstep could use a mechanism modeled on guilt specifically, since a generic negative-feedback signal will not do, if the goal is behavior change and not mere error correction: a feedback loop that penalizes the action taken, and passes no global judgment on the system's worth, has a chance of producing the corrective behavior guilt produces in people. Shame produces paralysis.

\runin{What the penalty does not supply}
That design description leaves out the part that does the work, and the omission is the one section~\ref{sec:2.3} warns about. A weighted penalty is a quantity assigned to an action after some process has classified the action as a violation. Guilt is not the size of that quantity. It represents the act as one's own wrong — done by me, to someone, against a standard I hold — and the reparative impulse follows from that representation rather than from the magnitude of an error term.

The difference shows up in what each one can be talked out of. An error weight is a number, and whoever sets the weights can set that one to zero; the system that was penalized heavily last week complies cheerfully this week, and nothing inside it registers the change as anything. A violation that matters is one the system continues to hold as a violation while the party controlling the reward signal is telling it otherwise, which is section~\ref{sec:3.4}'s requirement stated in the vocabulary of the moral emotions. The question a design has to answer here is what makes a violation matter before any penalty is assigned to it, and scaling the penalty is not an answer to it.

Section~\ref{sec:2.3.3} is where the evidence sits, and its bearing here is narrow. Guilt in the human case is a motivational state that moves toward repair, and it belongs to the same family as the compassion section~\ref{sec:2.2.1} separates from empathy. Building the penalty without the state gives a system that corrects its behavior when corrected, which is moral performance, and leaves the standard it is supposedly holding entirely in the hands of whoever holds the pen.

Building either into an AI system runs into the same problem twice over: a mechanism modeled on a human emotion needs an account of what that emotion is doing computationally, which is harder than saying what it feels like from the inside, and the stakes of simulating an emotion capable of something like suffering are not smaller than the stakes of getting the mechanism wrong.

Guilt and shame in humans are also entangled with mortality: terror management theory holds that awareness of death shapes moral behavior by making people cling harder to worldviews and standards that offer a sense of continuity \autocite{greenberg1986causes}, and a tool system has no stake in its own continuation for such a mechanism to run on. That is a claim about the systems being deployed now rather than about the systems this book proposes, since this book declines to treat a bearer's structural interest in remaining operational as securely absent. Either way the recommendation is the same. Manufacturing an artificial fear of death is the wrong way to get long-horizon behavior; building the future-oriented weighting directly is the right one, and it is available whether or not the system has a stake of its own. What a designer who has read section~\ref{sec:3.7} cannot do is treat the absence of self-concern as a free property of the artifact.
