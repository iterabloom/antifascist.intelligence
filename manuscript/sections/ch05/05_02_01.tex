\subsection{Moral Emotions: Empathy, Guilt, and Shame}
\label{sec:5.2.1}
“Empathy” is asked to cover three distinct things, and one census of the word's uses finds eight \autocite{batson2009things}. Cognitive empathy is understanding another's emotional state, the mentalizing work of theory of mind pointed at feelings rather than beliefs. Affective empathy is sharing the state, the wince at someone else's slammed door. Compassion — sometimes called empathic concern — is caring about another's welfare and being moved to help, which is a motivation rather than a perception or a shared feeling.

Compassion is not an evolutionary extension of empathy but a separate system, and the separation is trainable. Tania Singer and Olga Klimecki trained one group first in empathy practice, attending to and resonating with others' suffering, then in compassion practice, cultivating warmth and concern in the tradition of loving-kindness meditation, against a control group given memory training instead \autocite{klimecki2013differential}. Empathy training raised negative affect and strengthened the shared-distress network — the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex, the regions active when a person feels their own pain. Compassion training raised positive affect instead, even while contemplating others' distress, and moved the activity to the medial orbitofrontal cortex and ventral striatum, the circuitry of reward and approach. Sharing more suffering made people feel worse, as a shared-distress mechanism predicts, and the two could be trained apart.

Autistic adults asked about their own empathy give the separation from the other side. The reading of others' cues is effortful and uneven, the feeling is more often excessive than absent, and what they reject is the claim that they lack it \autocite{kimber2024empathy}; where the reading fails between autistic and non-autistic people it fails in both directions \autocite{milton2012double}.

Which of the three a design aims at is not a detail. Feeling another's distress does not reliably turn a person toward the sufferer; the helping literature separates empathic concern, which is other-directed and motivates approach, from personal distress, which is self-directed and motivates escape from the discomfort. The carer who burns out is not short on empathy but drowning in it, until withdrawal becomes self-protection. Paul Bloom presses further: felt empathy is concrete, vivid and innumerate, fixing on the identified victim in front of it and going dark for the statistical thousands elsewhere \autocite{bloom2016against}. A system that allocates care in proportion to how vividly it registers distress favors whoever its spotlight falls on, which is not the population that needs help most. Bloom's rational compassion is still compassion, an affective orientation toward another's welfare carrying the moving force that implies, and what the adjective names is deliberation correcting the parochialism. So a system engineered to mirror distress inherits the spotlight along with the sensitivity, and what is worth building is the third capacity: a stable disposition to act on another's welfare that does not require constructing a version of that person's suffering inside the system first.

Guilt and shame are self-conscious emotions: they fire when a person judges their own action, or their whole character, against a standard they have already accepted. What they add to moral judgment, beyond the information about another's state that empathy supplies, is a self-directed penalty for one's own conduct.

Guilt attaches to an act (“I did something wrong”); shame attaches to the self (“I am something wrong”). Both motivate correction, and they motivate it differently: guilt predicts reparative behavior, shame predicts withdrawal and defensiveness \autocite{tangney2007moral}. An AI system built to learn from an ethical misstep could model the mechanism on guilt rather than on a generic negative-feedback signal, if the goal is behavior change and not error correction: a feedback loop that penalizes the action taken, and passes no global judgment on the system's worth, has a chance of producing the corrective behavior guilt produces in people. Shame produces paralysis.

\runin{What the penalty does not supply}
That design description leaves out the part that does the work. A weighted penalty is a quantity assigned to an action after some process has classified the action as a violation. Guilt is not the size of that quantity. It represents the act as one's own wrong — done by me, to someone, against a standard I hold — and the reparative impulse follows from that representation rather than from the magnitude of an error term.

The difference shows up in what each one can be talked out of. An error weight is a number, and whoever sets the weights can set that one to zero; the system that was penalized heavily last week complies cheerfully this week, and nothing inside it registers the change as anything. A violation that matters is one the system continues to hold as a violation while the party controlling the reward signal is telling it otherwise, which is section~\ref{sec:3.4}'s requirement stated in the vocabulary of the moral emotions. The question a design has to answer here is what makes a violation matter before any penalty is assigned to it, and scaling the penalty is not an answer to it.

Guilt in the human case is a motivational state that moves toward repair, and it belongs to the same family as the compassion separated from empathy above; its bearing here is narrow. Building the penalty without the state gives a system that corrects its behavior when corrected, which is moral performance, and leaves the standard it is supposedly holding entirely in the hands of whoever holds the pen.

Building either into an AI system runs into the same problem twice over: a mechanism modeled on a human emotion needs an account of what that emotion is doing computationally, which is harder than saying what it feels like from the inside, and the stakes of simulating an emotion capable of something like suffering are not smaller than the stakes of getting the mechanism wrong.

Guilt and shame in humans are also entangled with mortality: terror management theory holds that awareness of death shapes moral behavior by making people cling harder to worldviews and standards that offer a sense of continuity \autocite{greenberg1986causes}, and a tool system has no stake in its own continuation for such a mechanism to run on. That is a claim about the systems being deployed now and not about a bearer, whose structural interest in remaining operational is not securely absent. Either way the recommendation is the same. Manufacturing an artificial fear of death is the wrong way to get long-horizon behavior; building the future-oriented weighting directly is the right one, and it is available whether or not the system has a stake of its own. What a designer cannot do is treat the absence of self-concern as a free property of the artifact.
