\subsection{The Role of Introspection for AI Systems}
\label{sec:5.2.3}
Introspection has a general-purpose version and a moral one, and the two come apart. The general case is a metacognitive layer that tracks whether a strategy is working and adjusts. What ethics needs on top of that is narrower — a check not of whether an output was wrong but of whether it drifted from the commitments the system is supposed to be operating under. A strategy can work perfectly on the wrong goal.

The benefits are real where they are bounded, which is where the introspective output is checkable against something outside the system: a system that inspects its own allocation pattern before releasing the next round of decisions catches a skew a retrospective audit would find months later, and a system that can report which evidence moved it gives the person relying on it something to evaluate.

The difficulties are worse than the benefits are good. A self-report is worth having only if it reflects what actually happened inside, and a fluent reconstruction after the fact is indistinguishable from the real thing at the point of use. That is no less true of a bearer, whose report about itself is the evidence anyone checking on it would have. A self-monitoring layer trained the same way as the layer it monitors reproduces its biases and adds the appearance of oversight. And a system modeling its own states in that much detail is holding information it did not need for the task, with the handling questions that follow.

Two systems are partial instances. A tutoring system that tracks a model of the student's self-regulation is introspecting on somebody else \autocite{azevedo2011hypermedia}, and post-hoc explanation methods extract an account of which inputs drove an output, which is the system being interrogated rather than observing itself \autocite{gunning2019darpas}. The word gets stretched furthest where the underlying system is most impressive, and how impressive a system is says nothing about whether it introspects.
