5.3.3. Strategies for AI Safety and Risk Mitigation
An AI system learns what it is supposed to want more reliably by inference than by specification. Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning (CIRL) treats a human's reward as unknown and to be inferred jointly through interaction, rather than fixed and handed over once [[cite:C0030]]; section 4.6.3 covers the technique in depth. The same logic applies to safety specifically — an AI personal assistant that learns an individual's preferences by observing how they handle email, and keeps revising that model as new feedback arrives, is safer than one built against a static specification of what "helpful" means.

Robustness matters alongside alignment. An AI system needs to hold up against adversarial attack, model misspecification, and a changing environment, and uncertainty quantification — Bayesian neural networks, ensemble learning — gives it a way to acknowledge what it does not know and act more cautiously where its confidence is low. An autonomous-vehicle system that can recognize when a road sign looks unlike anything in its training data is in a stronger position than one that classifies every sign with the same unearned confidence.

Learning also has to happen safely, not just correctly. Constrained policy optimization keeps a reinforcement-learning agent inside predetermined safety limits throughout its search for a better policy, rather than checking for violations only after the fact [[cite:C0347]] — the difference between a robotic arm that learns to handle delicate objects without ever damaging one during training, and one that has to break several first.

Human oversight has to stay active once a system is deployed, not only during training, especially in high-stakes domains: dashboard visualization and anomaly detection can flag when a system is behaving unexpectedly, and a human expert reviewing a medical AI system's recommendations needs the standing to override them when something looks wrong.

Explaining a system's own reasoning is safety infrastructure in its own right. A lending system that can explain why it approved or denied a loan is a system whose safety failures are visible; section 5.3.4 covers how that explanation gets produced.

None of this is a one-time build. AI development needs regular evaluation, feedback, and adjustment — internal monitoring, user feedback, third-party audits — the way a content-recommendation algorithm gets adjusted on evidence that it is feeding an echo chamber.

Collaboration extends all of this past any single organization's walls: sharing research findings, best practices, and tools among developers, ethicists, regulators, and other stakeholders. OpenAI and the Partnership on AI [[cite:C0348]] are two venues where that kind of cooperation is meant to happen, though how well the practice matches the stated aim is worth asking.
