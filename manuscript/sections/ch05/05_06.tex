\section{What the term doesn't reach}
\label{sec:5.6}
Article 2(4) covers force between states. Force a government turns on its own population, such as troops against protesters or an opposition party designated a terrorist organization, is outside it. That force is governed by the Covenant's rights to life, liberty and assembly and by the prohibition on crimes against humanity.

Occupied territory is a third case. Force by an occupying power in territory it holds is, on the occupier's description, neither force between states nor force against its own population. That description is the occupier's own claim and is discounted like an attacker's claim to disputed land. Where a disinterested body has found the territory occupied, the force term engages as it does between states, and the person-directed terms govern regardless. An occupation, a blockade or a siege is not an attack under way. It is a continuing wrong, and the remedy for it waits on the second tier. Otherwise every armed group in an occupied territory would hold a perpetual first-tier licence, and so would every state that describes its neighbor's territory as occupied.

Most contemporary uses of force fall elsewhere: against non-state armed groups on another state's territory, in anticipation of an attack not yet under way, or to rescue nationals. The term handles them one way. An armed attack by a non-state group is an armed attack, and repelling it while it is under way is first-tier force. Force on a third state's territory without that state's consent, whether against the group or to rescue nationals, and force against a threat not yet under way, wait on the second tier. This rejects the “unwilling or unable” doctrine and anticipatory self-defense, both of which several states hold. I reject them for the reason the term exists: each lets the belligerent judge its own claim.
