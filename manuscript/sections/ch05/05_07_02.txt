5.7.2. Encouraging AI Systems to Deliberate Morally Through Playful Interactions
Section 4.3 covers curiosity and exploration as ends in themselves — a system driven to seek out new situations because novelty is its own reward. This section points that same appetite for the unscripted at a narrower target: training an AI system to deliberate about right and wrong inside interactions that stay open-ended. Simulating a single dilemma and stopping does not reach it.

<<h>>Gaming Environments<</h>>
Existing games built around social interaction and dilemma rather than pure optimization, are a natural fit for this. Minecraft's open building and resource-management mechanics could put an AI agent through allocation dilemmas — how to divide scarce materials, whether to help or ignore a non-player character in distress — that have no single correct answer built into the game's win condition. Among Us, built around deception and accusation among a small group of players, could train an AI agent to distinguish benign misdirection from harmful deceit and to weigh the cost of a false accusation against the cost of staying silent. Neither game was designed for this purpose, and using them this way is a proposal and no description of existing research — but the design problem they pose, ambiguous information, competing claims on trust, consequences that play out over the rest of the game, is close to the shape of an actual ethical dilemma in a way a single-shot trolley-problem prompt is not.

<<h>>Designed Play Activities<</h>>
Play does not have to run inside an existing game. Narrative construction is one alternative: give an AI system a moral dilemma and have it build a story around it, working through the perspectives of several characters with genuinely conflicting interests, without picking a winner. Role-play scenarios are another: an AI system takes a stakeholder's part in a simulated negotiation or conflict, argues from inside that position, and sees how its choices affect the other parties before the scenario ends. Both put the system in the position of constructing an outcome. The alternative selects from a menu of pre-written responses, which is closer to what moral deliberation actually demands.

What these approaches share is a refusal to reduce moral reasoning to a labeled dataset of right answers. A system that only ever sees dilemmas with a single correct output learns to match patterns against that output, and not to deliberate — and an environment built around play is, at minimum, one way to keep the second problem in view while building the first capacity.
