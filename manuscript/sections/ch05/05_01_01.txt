5.1.1. Sources of Bias in AI Data and Algorithms
Bias in AI systems can originate from multiple sources, including data collection, data labeling, algorithm design, and implementation. These biases can lead to unfair, discriminatory, or otherwise undesirable outcomes when AI systems are deployed in real-world contexts.

(1) Data Collection: Bias can be introduced during the data collection process if the dataset is not representative of the population or situation that the AI system is intended to address. For example, if a facial recognition system is trained on a dataset of predominantly light-skinned individuals, the system may perform poorly on individuals with darker skin tones, leading to misidentification or failure to recognize faces. In 2018, the ACLU conducted a test of Amazon's facial recognition system, Rekognition, using the mugshots of US Congress members. The system falsely matched 28 of them with criminal mugshots, with a disproportionately high number of false matches for non-white members of Congress. This issue stemmed from training the system on a dataset that overrepresented lighter-skinned individuals.

(2) Data Labeling: Bias can also be introduced during the data labeling process if human annotators hold conscious or unconscious biases. For instance, annotators may be more likely to label images of women as "nurse" and images of men as "doctor," which can reinforce gender stereotypes in the AI system's predictions. As another example: in the widely used ImageNet dataset, images were labeled using crowdsourced workers from Amazon Mechanical Turk. The workers may have held implicit biases, as images featuring women were often associated with appearance-related labels, whereas those featuring men were more likely to be tagged with action-related labels.

(3) Algorithm Design: Bias can emerge from the design of the algorithms themselves, particularly if they are not carefully constructed to account for potential sources of bias. For example, an algorithm may inadvertently penalize loan applicants who live in specific neighborhoods, even if the applicants meet all other criteria for loan approval. This could result in a disparate impact on individuals from minority or low-income communities. As another example: In a ProPublica investigation, the COMPAS recidivism risk assessment tool was found to incorrectly predict higher recidivism rates for African American defendants, despite having similar risk factors and criminal histories as white defendants. The bias resulted from the algorithm weighing certain factors differently based on race.

(4) Implementation: Bias can also arise from how AI systems are implemented and deployed in real-world contexts. For instance, if a predictive policing algorithm is disproportionately applied in minority neighborhoods, it may perpetuate existing disparities in the criminal justice system. In 2016, a study of the PredPol predictive policing system found that the algorithm disproportionately targeted minority neighborhoods, even when controlling for crime rates. This issue arose because the system used historical arrest data, which reflected existing racial disparities in policing practices.

Mitigating these biases requires a thorough understanding of the sources of bias and active efforts to address them. This includes collecting diverse and representative datasets, scrutinizing data labeling processes, designing algorithms that consider fairness and equity, and carefully monitoring the implementation and deployment of AI systems to prevent unintended consequences. Additionally, transparency, accountability, and collaboration among AI researchers and developers, ethicists, policymakers, and other stakeholders can help to address and minimize biases in AI systems, ensuring more equitable and fair outcomes in their real-world applications.
