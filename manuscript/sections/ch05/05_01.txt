5.1. Bias, Fairness, and Equity in AI Systems
AI systems remain vulnerable to bias from multiple sources — the training data they learn from, the structure of their algorithms, and presets encoded in their goals, whether intentional or not. Left unaddressed, these biases produce unfair, inequitable, or discriminatory outcomes: the opposite of the altruistic, anti-authoritarian AI this book argues for.

Fair, equitable AI requires addressing bias on three fronts: representation in training data, transparency in algorithmic decision-making, and continuous performance evaluation.

Representation in training data determines whether an AI system learns patterns that generalize fairly or patterns that simply encode whoever dominates the data. Gender, racial, and socio-economic skew can cause a system to perpetuate stereotypes or fail the population segments it underrepresents — face recognition systems, for instance, disproportionately misidentify women and people of color when trained on data that underrepresents them [[cite:C0358]]. Over-representation cuts the other way, treating one group's experience as the default and generalizing it, wrongly, to everyone else.

Algorithmic transparency exposes bias that representation alone can miss: not just an algorithm's mathematical structure, but how it weighs variables and reaches a decision. A predictively powerful healthcare model that ignores the socio-economic factors driving unequal access to care will reproduce that inequality however accurate its predictions otherwise are.

Bias also enters after deployment, as a system adapts to new data. Regular audits of credit-scoring and hiring systems catch and correct bias as it emerges, rather than after it has already caused harm.

Involving the people an AI system's decisions actually affect, not only its developers, keeps the system answerable to the range of values those decisions touch, rather than to one group's unexamined perspective.
