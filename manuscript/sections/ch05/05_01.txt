5.1. Moral Development in Humans and AI
Understanding how human moral judgment develops matters for building AI systems with ethical, empathetic, and antifascist traits, because the stages a human moral sense passes through — from a grasp of basic rules to principles that generalize past them — are a workable model for how an artificial one might develop too, and more than a metaphor borrowed for color.

In humans, moral development is the shift from a simple grasp of social rules to an ethical system grounded in general principles, shaped along the way by environment, social interaction, culture, and a person's own disposition toward learning. I take those same elements — social interaction, mentorship, and the stance an agent brings to its own failures — to matter for how an AI system's moral capacities develop as well.

Humans move from learning and following simple rules — don't hurt people, share fairly — to internalizing and applying principles that those rules only approximate. AI systems trained toward ethical behavior should progress the same way: from responding to predefined rules encoded into their programming to a principled understanding that can guide decisions in situations the rules never anticipated.

That progression from rules to principles depends on the learning stance an AI system brings to it. Carol Dweck's research on the growth mindset — the disposition to treat ability as something built through effort — is a useful model to adapt: a system trained to treat failure as informative has room to keep improving its moral judgment [[cite:C0320]].

The harder problem is that an AI system has no equivalent of the diverse, embodied, socio-cultural experience through which a human being builds moral intuition in the first place. What it can have instead is active learning from human mentors — building on human intuition instead of growing an equivalent from nothing.

The developmental psychologist Felix Warneken's studies of young children found that they display helping, fairness, and reciprocity through interaction and observation well before they can put any of it into words [[cite:C0321]]. A comparable moral apprenticeship for an AI system — learning through mentorship and observation — is the model section 5.1.3 works out in detail.

Ethics taught this way is ethics an AI system can apply to situations no rule anticipated. Developmental and cognitive psychology offer a template for building that capacity — not a guarantee of it, but a better starting point than treating moral competence as something to specify once and freeze.
