\subsection{Moral Apprenticeship: AI Systems Learning from Human Mentors}
\label{sec:5.1.3}
Apprenticeship means teaching through cases and correction instead of specification. A mentor presents varied situations, the learner responds, the mentor corrects, and what transfers is a way of weighing cases rather than a larger rule table — which is the entire reason for learning it from a person. An emergency-triage system taught this way is being shown how to hold urgency, scarcity, and what a decision costs a family in view at once. No specification document conveys that.

Three cautions come with it, and the third is the one this book cares about most. Case-based teaching transfers the mentor's blind spots along with the mentor's judgment, and does so invisibly, because a blind spot produces no correction. The architecture has to integrate moral reasoning with the rest of the system's judgment rather than bolting it on, which is section~\ref{sec:4.1.2}'s problem. And apprenticeship is a control channel: a system whose moral education amounts to obedience toward whoever is mentoring it has learned something, and it is not ethics. Section~\ref{sec:2.1.4}'s requirements apply to the mentor as much as the system — whoever teaches a system should be as auditable as the system being taught. That is section~\ref{sec:3.1}'s custody problem arriving at the point of formation. The first branch failed because a constraint built into an artifact has an owner; apprenticeship fails the same way one step earlier, because a disposition built by correction has a corrector, and a bearer trained to defer to whoever last corrected it is a bearer whose floor belongs to that party.
