\subsection{Moral Apprenticeship: AI Systems Learning from Human Mentors}
\label{sec:5.1.3}
Apprenticeship means teaching through cases and correction instead of specification. A mentor presents varied situations, the learner responds, the mentor corrects, and what transfers is a way of weighing cases rather than a larger rule table — which is the entire reason for learning it from a person. An emergency-triage system taught this way is being shown how to hold urgency, scarcity, and what a decision costs a family in view at once. No specification document conveys that.

Three cautions come with it, and the third is the one this book cares about most. Case-based teaching transfers the mentor's blind spots along with the mentor's judgment, and does so invisibly, because a blind spot produces no correction. The architecture has to integrate moral reasoning with the rest of the system's judgment rather than bolting it on, which is section 4.1.2's problem. And apprenticeship is a control channel: a system whose moral education amounts to obedience toward whoever is mentoring it has learned something, and it is not ethics. Section 2.1.4's requirements apply to the mentor as much as the system — whoever teaches a system should be as auditable as the system being taught.
