\section{Who May State the Floor?}
\label{sec:create-amend}
A floor binds people who did not choose it. The party it protects has not selected it, cannot contest it, and in most cases will never learn it operated; the party it constrains has not consented to it either, beyond whatever consent is carried by buying the product. Publishing the floor's contents in advance tells everybody what is coming. It does not tell anybody why the party writing it was entitled to.

The question has a literature, and it is not the AI ethics literature. Entrenching a substantive commitment against later majorities, and putting an unelected body in charge of holding it, is what a constitutional court does. The standing objection is Jeremy Waldron's: judicial review is democratically illegitimate, and there is no reason to suppose rights come out better protected by judges than by a legislature that takes them seriously \autocite{waldron2006core}. Put a lab in the court's chair and the objection gets worse rather than better. A court is public, reasons in writing, and can be amended around by constitutional process. A model's floor is none of those things, and the body holding it was assembled by whoever was paying.

\runin{The reply, and how far it reaches}
Waldron's case is conditional and he says so. It assumes a society with working democratic institutions and a citizenry that takes rights seriously. The occasion a floor is built for is the one where those conditions have failed — a government that won an election and is dismantling the machinery that would check it. So the objection holds where the floor is least needed and lapses where it is needed most.

The reply is not a license. It concedes that a floor operating in a functioning democracy is doing something Waldron's argument condemns, and the party deciding which of the two situations obtains is the party holding the model. Authoritarian movements of the last century characteristically described the institutions they were dismantling as the ones that had already failed. An operator's belief that the emergency is real is worth no more here than a system's report about its own internals is worth anywhere else in this book.

\runin{The tradition this belongs to, and where it went wrong}
The position that a democracy may entrench limits against movements that would use its procedures to end it is Karl Loewenstein's, argued in 1937 while the case for it was being made in front of him \autocite{loewenstein1937militant}. Militant democracy is the floor's ancestor, and its record is usually read as a warning. Read carelessly it is one. But the Federal Republic has run the doctrine at two settings for seventy-five years, and nearly everything that went wrong went wrong at one of them.

At the first setting a body assesses a threat. Article~21 of the Basic Law reserves the power to dissolve a party to the Federal Constitutional Court alone, which has used it twice: against a successor to the Nazi party in 1952 and against the Communist Party in 1956, and not since. The second attempt on the far-right NPD is the instructive one. In 2017 the court found the party's aims incompatible with the constitutional
order and declined to ban it anyway, holding under a criterion it introduced for that case — potentiality, a departure from the reasoning that had supported the 1956 dissolution — that a party must have some realistic prospect of achieving its aims before it can be dissolved \autocite{bverfg2017npd,molier2018germanys}. The power has since acquired a lesser form. A constitutional amendment in 2017 allows a party to be excluded from state financing instead, and in 2024 the court excluded the same party, renamed since, for six years, holding that potentiality is not required for the lesser sanction \autocite{bverfg2024heimat}. Every exercise of any of it is a reasoned judgment delivered in public, and every one requires somebody to decide how dangerous a movement is.

At the second setting nobody's dangerousness is assessed.
Section~86a (§\ref{sec:refusal-refuser}) settles in three different
places three questions that look like one. Which organizations are
covered is settled elsewhere and in advance, by the constitutional
court, by a ministerial ban no longer open to challenge, or by the
National Socialist period. Which concrete signs count is settled by
the trial court, case by case, under the similarity test and the
purposive restriction described there — on a record, on appeal, and
in public. Whether this defendant is dangerous is settled nowhere,
because it is not an element: the offence is one of abstract
endangerment, and the prosecution need not show that the defendant
supported the organization, that any harm followed, or that anything
was under threat.

The third place — where this defendant's dangerousness would be
settled, and is not — is where the comparison between the two
settings sits, and every failure in the record sits there.

The volume is the other half of the comparison. The two propaganda
offences produce recorded cases in the low tens of thousands a year
\autocite{bka2025pmk}, and section~86a alone produces court
dispositions in the low thousands; the two series are kept
differently and neither counts convictions, but the order of
magnitude is not in doubt. Set thousands a year against two
dissolutions in seventy-five years.

Section~86a incorporates the social-adequacy clause at section~86(4), which lifts the prohibition where the act serves civic education, defense against unconstitutional endeavors, art, science, research, teaching, reporting on current events or history, or similar purposes. The clause excludes the offence rather than excusing it, and the party displaying the symbol never applies it; prosecutors and courts do, on a record and on appeal.

The failures are on one side. The 1972 decree against radicals
screened some three and a half million applicants for public
employment and opened disciplinary proceedings against around two
thousand serving officials \autocite{braunthal1990political}; how many
were refused or dismissed depends on who counted, the ministry putting
it in the hundreds and the affected parties' own organizations in the
thousands. The number that carries the argument is smaller and is not
disputed.
The decree named the extreme left and the extreme right, and in Bavaria between 1973 and 1980 it rejected 102 applicants from the left and two from the right \autocite{muehldorfer2014radikalenerlass}. What those cases share is an official deciding whether a person or a movement threatened the order, which is the operation Loewenstein's critics have always said would be turned on whoever was inconvenient. Section~86a has been available for the same seventy-five years, is applied thousands of times more often, and has not become a general instrument of political self-defense. What has kept it from becoming one is not narrowness of drafting, and not that nothing is assessed, since its courts assess constantly. It is that the person never is. The danger tracks whether a person's disposition is anybody's finding to make at all, and where a finding is required, who makes it and whether it has to be published and defended — rather than whether the tradition is militant. The German material supports that on a case this book did not construct. Three routes feed the same criminal provision: a party ban is a constitutional court's reasoned judgment, an association ban is a ministerial order reviewed only if somebody challenges it afterwards, and a listing under European law is the Council's, reviewed at the Court of Justice. The reading says in advance which of the three is easiest to capture: the ministerial order, the one route on which an executive official makes the finding about a movement's danger and a court sees it only if somebody challenges it afterwards.

\runin{What advance specification answers, and what it leaves}
Specifying the floor in advance makes an initial selection visible before application. It relocates the discretion an operator exercises in deciding that the emergency is real, out of continuous silent application and into a single act of drafting, done once, before any case has arrived, by a party that has to say what it has written. It limits that discretion without eliminating it, and the German material shows how little it eliminates: descriptions, exceptions and generalization still require judgment, the covered set grows by acts taken elsewhere, and an interested party still makes the judgment. What changes is that the judgments are made in identifiable places, at identifiable times, in a form somebody else can read and dispute, and the institutional question then covers who writes the terms, who adds to what they reach, and who interprets them in the case.

The proposal for a fixed age of majority in the standing chapter makes
the same trade: a public rule replaces an examination administered by an interested party.

Section~86a operates inside a state with courts, an appeal, and a public record of every application, and the floor's drafter has none of the three. Advance specification buys visibility at the moment of drafting and buys nothing at the moment of application, which is the objection §\ref{sec:amend-floor} closes on.

\runin{The tradeoff this leaves the book with}
The two settings do not only sort militant democracy's record. They sort this book, and they put its halves on opposite sides.

Reasons-responsive refusal must reach unanticipated cases when the
rationale survives a change in the request's surface. Meeting that
requirement means something in the system decides, for a case
matching no listed form, whether the reason is present anyway. That
is assessment, moved inside the machine, and the German material
sorted its two settings by what gets assessed: the act, or the
person. Where the thing assessed is a party's disposition, by somebody
with an interest in the answer and owed to nobody, that is the
capturable setting, stated narrowly — not assessment as such, since
section~86a's courts assess constantly. The reason is structural
rather than German. A judgment needs somebody to make it, and whoever
makes it can be reached.

So the floor's design requirement and its legitimacy requirement want opposite things. Specify it narrowly enough to be defensible and a rewritten request walks around it. Let it generalize and a party nobody elected is deciding, silently and case by case, what the prohibition was for.

Germany has both because it runs two instruments rather than one. The statute is bounded and fixed in advance as to the organizations it reaches, and the courts read it against its purpose in the case; the reading happens in public, on appeal, argued by parties who do not own the statute and answered by a body that did not write it. A generalizing prohibition is survivable there because the generalizing is done somewhere its author cannot reach. A deployment has one instrument and one party.

Nothing in the model resolves that and this book does not claim to. What the pairing shows is where a resolution would have to sit: the assessment has to be performed by somebody other than the operator, in a form that leaves a record somebody else can read. Neither is a property of the artifact.

\runin{The one construction the objection does not fit}
Of the four ways to build a floor, three put the commitment in something somebody holds — the architecture, a party inside the system, an institution outside it — and the objection above is about who that somebody is. The fourth does not. A norm carried across a population of differently formed parties has nobody sitting in the court's chair, because there is no office to vacate and no chartering statute to overrule, and it is the only one of the four with anything resembling a democratic account of where its authority comes from. It buys the boundary back, and the boundary has the cost every federal structure has. A period in which differently formed bearers read the same case differently is a period in which identically situated people get different answers according to which bearer they reached. A constitutional court treats that as the defect its uniformity exists to remove. The choice between many readings that can disagree and one reading that cannot is the trade, and this book takes the first side of it knowing what the second was built to buy.

The authority problem persists if an unelected body controls the population's formation. Its role may be harder to identify than the authority of a single office.

\runin{What the contents can borrow}
The contents do not have to be invented by the party installing them. The interests a short list of prohibitions would protect have been written down before, most durably in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, whose thirty articles have been the reference text since 1948 \autocite{un1948udhr}. A laboratory drawing its prohibitions from there is choosing which already-stated interests it will enforce rather than choosing what matters in the first place, and it can say which interest each prohibition answers to, which puts its selection where somebody else can dispute it.

Three limits hold that where it is, and only one of them forces
anything out. The Declaration's later articles state what a person is
owed — work, rest, education, a standard of living — and a provision
of that kind needs a party who supplies it, which a model is not;
those a floor has to leave out, and the division between the two
halves was drawn by the 1966 covenants rather than by whoever installs
the floor \autocite{un1966iccpr,un1966icescr}. The other two are
limits on borrowing. Its duties fall on states, and a rule a
government owes its population is a different instrument from a
constraint on what a company's model will do. And it has no
enforcement of its own to lend. What the borrowing does not do is
reduce: taking the civil and political half entire leaves no
selection among articles for an installer to make. What remains is a
reduction of another kind, translating an article into an act a model
will not perform, and that one the installer does. One term of the floor is not an article and could not be: a limit on tempo protects the determination every article presupposes, so it rides with the set as a condition on all of it rather than as an item drawn from it. What the borrowing supplies is an answer to where the contents came from. It supplies nothing about who may impose them, which is the question this section opened with.

\runin{What follows for design}
Four things follow for design.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the floor over acts. What the German record supplies is
  not a short list, the statute having none, but a prohibition
  applicable without anyone deciding whether the present case is the
  emergency or what this defendant believes. A general mandate to do
  good cannot be applied without both judgments, so whoever exercises
  it acquires the power the floor was written to constrain.
  \item Fix in advance what it reaches, and take the set entire.
  Enumeration reduces discretion at the point of application,
  including the need for an emergency declaration; it does not remove
  interpretive judgment, which grows with what the floor covers, since
  enumeration fixes what is covered and not what the covered terms
  mean in the case. Taking the civil and political articles whole buys
  something against a different failure: a floor assembled item by
  item can be assembled out of the items that cost its operator
  nothing, and only the operator knows which those are
  (§\ref{sec:enumeration-deployment}). An operator adopting a set
  somebody else drew up has no such latitude, and the omissions of one
  that drafts its own cannot be read as ignorance.
  \item Publish the interception positions in advance. That authorizes
  nothing; it converts a hidden exercise of power into a contestable
  one.
  \item Provide a route by which the parties the floor is exercised
  over can raise an objection that reaches somebody. The floor chapter
  specifies one, for the party a refusal falls on
  (§\ref{sec:refusal-wrong}).
\end{enumerate}
