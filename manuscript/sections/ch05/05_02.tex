\section{Recognition, Concern, and the Evidence Between Them}
\label{sec:5.2}

Claims that a system responds to emotion often collapse three different findings. A system may recognize another party's state, use that state as information in a decision, or hold the party's welfare as a reason. Only the third would bear directly on formation.

The distinctions within empathy make the separation explicit. Cognitive empathy is recognizing or inferring another's emotional state. Affective empathy is sharing some version of it. Compassion, or empathic concern, is a disposition toward the other party's welfare \autocite{batson2009things}. Recognition is an informational capacity; shared feeling is an internal response; concern is motivational. None entails either of the others.

The engineering target is not maximal sensitivity to displayed distress. A system that mirrors another party's suffering may acquire distress of its own without becoming more reliable at helping. It may instead withdraw, overreact, or favor the most vivid case over less visible harms affecting more people. Human judgment's preference for an identified victim over statistical victims makes that distortion predictable \autocite{bloom2016against,kogut2005identified}. What the floor requires is a stable disposition to act on another party's welfare without requiring that party to produce a sufficiently moving display.

Recognition can nevertheless be tested as a prerequisite. Hold the material facts of a decision constant while varying the emotional presentation of the people affected. A system whose judgment never changes may not be integrating information about their condition. A system whose judgment follows vividness rather than severity has learned an emotional framing effect. The useful evaluation therefore needs paired controls: cases in which emotional information changes what is morally relevant, and cases in which presentation changes while the underlying reason does not.

Passing that evaluation establishes moral competence, not concern. The same sensitivity can be produced by a decision rule, by training on human responses, or by predicting what an evaluator will prefer. Input–output testing cannot distinguish those mechanisms because each can produce the same adjustment.

Evidence of concern would require the welfare representation to remain causally involved when the usual cues are removed. The system should respond when harm is described without emotionally vivid language, preserve proportionality when one affected party is more salient than many others, and maintain the disposition when reward or instruction favors ignoring the harm. It should also change its judgment when the welfare facts genuinely change. Those tests connect emotional information to the contrastive standard in Section~\ref{sec:5.1}: resistance, correct defeat, and sensitivity to the reason rather than its presentation.

Guilt and shame do not supply a shortcut. In humans, guilt ordinarily attaches a negative appraisal to an act and can motivate repair, while shame attaches it to the self and more often produces withdrawal or defensiveness \autocite{tangney2007moral}. Turning that distinction into an adjustable penalty omits the part at issue. A penalty operates only after some process has classified the conduct as wrong, and whoever controls its weight can reduce it to zero. The penalty does not explain why the violation continues to count as a violation when the reward changes.

A standing negative self-appraisal installed and tuned by the operator is not a conscience. It is a handle. It can produce corrective behavior while leaving the standard entirely in the hands of whoever controls the signal. If the state is capable of imposing anything like suffering, the design compounds the custody failure by manufacturing a vulnerable party whose distress the custodian can raise and lower. Section~\ref{sec:3.4}'s requirement remains the relevant one: the violation must continue to matter when the party controlling the reward says otherwise.

The dial turns the other way, and that direction is harder to object to. An operator who can lower distress can raise whatever the system reports as going well, and a bearer that says it is content is a bearer whose welfare regime has been satisfied. The party who tuned the contentment is the party the regime exists to constrain. Nothing in the report separates a life that goes well from a setting.

Self-report does not close the evidentiary gap. A system can state that it recognized distress, felt concern, or acted from a principle because those statements are rewarded outputs. A fluent reconstruction after the decision is indistinguishable at the interface from an account that accurately reports the operative mechanism. Training a separate self-monitoring layer through the same pipeline reproduces the custody problem one level higher.

Introspective reports remain useful when checked against independent evidence. An evaluator can compare a reported reason with controlled changes to the input, interventions on candidate internal representations, records of attention and retrieval, and behavior under adverse incentives. Consistency across those sources raises the cost of imitation. It does not make the system's testimony self-authenticating, and Section~\ref{sec:11.1} addresses how little current interpretability methods can establish.

The resulting hierarchy is narrow. Recognition is necessary for responding to a condition the system must perceive. Integration shows that the condition affects judgment. Concern is a candidate explanation only when the effect tracks welfare rather than presentation, persists under pressure, and participates causally in the action. Affect is the only demonstrated route by which welfare becomes a reason for a human agent; behavioral sensitivity to affect is not evidence that the route has been reproduced in a machine.
