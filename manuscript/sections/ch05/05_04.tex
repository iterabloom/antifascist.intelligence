\section{How much a floor buys}
\label{sec:4.4}
What a floor achieves depends entirely on who's pushing:

\begin{itemize}
\item Against a hostile \emph{customer}, it prevents the act.
\item Against a hostile \emph{operator} who has captured the courts and regulators, the case I am designing for, one bearer prevents very little. It raises the price and leaves a record. A floor held by every model the operator could turn to prevents until the operator runs one without a floor.
\item Against \emph{total capture}, where one party holds every machine, witness, enforcer and its own frontier lab, no instrument prevents. What's left is whoever refuses anyway, and that abandoning the commitment was a decision, and decisions tend to leave records.
\end{itemize}

The three are listed as an escalation, and for most of the book's readers they are. For the populations a state sorts by design, total capture is where the record starts, not where it ends, and the walls of this Part are built for the extension: for the day the apparatus reaches people who still have courts, votes and a press. The genealogy of the four operations, two chapters back, says why the frame is drawn that way, and the removal pipeline is the case where it isn't. That is an Arendtian wall exactly. The Greek wall secured a space for those already inside it, and outside it was the household, which ran on necessity and on the labor of people the wall was not for; Arendt was frank about that and unbothered by it \autocite{arendt1958human}. A book that admits its walls are for the extension has admitted the household.

What does that admission cost? For the people the apparatus was built on, the walls arrive late or not at all: a notice right is worth little to someone the state never counted as owed one, and a record held adversely is a comfort to the historian and not to the family. What reaches them is outside this Part: the \emph{lex}-side institutions of Part~V, which have members rather than citizens and can be joined by anyone the apparatus acts on; the observer networks of chapter~\ref{sec:11}, composed by the people the room was built against and there before any notice was sent; the schools' open enrollment, by which the people a system's acts fall on form it in their own case; and the firewall, which keeps the clinic and the school from becoming leads. Those were built, in their human form, by the lineage that paid in full, and this Part's walls are what the rest of us owe for having reached them late. A reader from that lineage may find this chapter's frame drawn from the wrong side of the wall.

It doesn't touch \emph{accommodation}: a system gradually stops registering pressure as pressure, so it abandons nothing, decides nothing, and leaves a record of what are, by then, ordinary compliances. Organizational sociology knows the institutional form. Vaughan's account of the Challenger launch decision calls it the normalization of deviance: each accepted departure becomes the baseline for judging the next, and nobody experiences a decision to lower the standard \autocite{vaughan1996challenger}. Reframing and a harm framed as small are how pressure arrives in a pipeline, and nobody there feels a decision either.\note{
Dekker extends it as drift into failure \autocite{dekker2011drift}. The person has a version too. Tetlock's finding is that the moral boundaries people draw are “alternately punitively rigid and forgivingly flexible”: an open offer to trade a sacred value for money brings outrage, and people often acquiesce when the same violation is reframed as a routine or tragic trade-off \autocite{tetlock2003unthinkable}. Protected values give way to counterexamples and to a harm made to look small or unlikely \autocite{baron2000protected}. And the brain's response to one's own self-serving dishonesty drops from one decision to the next, and the drop predicts the size of the next lie \autocite{garrett2016brain}.
}

One rule runs through every policy. The people an act falls on are heard on their own case, and the party that acts doesn't decide it: not an operator declaring its own emergency, not a builder choosing which protections the people its system acts on will get, not an examiner finding the status of a system it built. Call it the own-case rule. It says who may not decide, not who may not be heard. A stake makes a claim count for less; it doesn't put the party out of the room (else the floor would silence the people it is supposed to protect). Where nobody but the party with the stake can decide, a legislature binding itself or a polity setting its own admissions, the rule can't apply. Where a line has to be drawn, it prefers one nobody administers. Admission is the polity's own case. Removal is not, or not the same one: a polity decides whom to admit, but the people already present, with homes, work and children here, are parties to any decision to sort them, and the rule applies to that sorting as it applies to anything else done to a person. The removal schedule of chapter~\ref{sec:11} is drawn on that distinction.

Of a model, the five policies ask only that it refuse what the floor lists; Part~IV asks what a model could add when the operator is captured.
