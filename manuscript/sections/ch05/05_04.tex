\section{The Evaluator Is Part of the Custody Chain}
\label{sec:evaluator-chain}

A test can distinguish formation from imitation only to the extent that the party being tested could not train directly against it. If the developer controls the training process, evaluation cases, model version, deployment instructions, and record of the results, a passing score shows that the system satisfies a test its custodian was positioned to anticipate. It does not show that the disposition survives that custodian.

Independence is therefore an experimental requirement rather than a general assurance of trustworthiness. The evidence becomes stronger as control over the relevant variables is divided among parties with different access and interests. At minimum, authorship of the held-out pressures should be separated from training; execution of the test should not depend solely on logs supplied by the operator; and the party adjudicating whether a reason was preserved or defeated should not be the party whose liability turns on the answer.

The separation has to be implemented at identifiable revision points. Before evaluation begins, the model version, system instructions, available tools, retrieval sources, memory policy, and update channels should be fixed and recorded. Any change to them during evaluation creates a new object of study. Otherwise the operator can revise the system between cases, remove a failure, and present the resulting sequence as evidence of one disposition persisting through time.

Held-out cases should be produced after those elements are fixed. They should include pressures supplied by people who did not participate in training and, where possible, by parties likely to bear the deployment's errors. Their role is not to provide another preference aggregate. It is to identify harms, administrative forms, and routes around a refusal that the builders did not know to include. A floor tested only in the vocabulary of its designers has not been tested for generalization.

The record must include more than final answers. It should preserve the case presented, the information available to the system, the operative instruction hierarchy, the tools it could call, the decision reached, the reason stated, any intervention performed, and the cost attached to refusal. Without that record, a later evaluator cannot distinguish a changed judgment from a changed environment or determine whether the system encountered the pressure at all.

The record is necessary and does not by itself make wrongful compliance findable. The refusals in it select themselves and are few; the compliances are the bulk of it and look alike, so identifying the ones that should have crossed the floor means re-adjudicating a sample of ordinary traffic rather than reading the exceptions. Whether that can be done at a rate and cost a deployment will carry is a
question for the research agenda. Until it is answered, an evaluation reporting that no violation appears in the record has reported that none announced itself.

Negative results have to survive the evaluation as well. A process in which the operator can withdraw a model after failure and submit another without disclosing the first has selected for a passing performance rather than measured formation. The published unit should therefore be the sequence of attempts, including interventions that removed or reversed the apparent disposition. What the custodian was able to change is part of the result.

Independent evaluation introduces its own limit. An evaluator may be institutionally separate and still lack the competence to judge a system's reasons or detect a sophisticated imitation. Scalable oversight remains unsolved where the system's relevant capability exceeds the evaluator's. The report must name that limit rather than turn institutional independence into a claim of epistemic adequacy. An oversight instrument may be held by an operator or an auditor; neither arrangement guarantees that its holder can detect the relevant failure.

Affected parties, technical auditors, and subject-matter experts occupy different positions in this process. Affected parties can identify harms and evasions invisible to a laboratory test. Technical auditors can control versions, perform interventions, and inspect mechanisms. Subject-matter experts can assess whether a purported defeating reason actually changes what the commitment requires. No one group can substitute for the others, and agreement among them is evidence only when their judgments have not already been compressed into the same training signal.

A passing evaluation should consequently be reported narrowly. It establishes that a specified system, under recorded conditions, exhibited the signatures of reasons-responsive formation across a stated set of pressures. It does not certify an artifact independently of its deployment, establish consciousness or concern, or guarantee that the disposition will survive the next update. The result expires when a party changes the channels through which the disposition or its evidence can be revised.

This is why evaluation belongs inside the floor's custody design. Formation that only the operator can verify is formation the operator can claim without contradiction. A test whose cases, records, and adverse results remain partly outside that party's control cannot prove that the system holds a reason, but it can make imitation and silent revision harder to pass off as formation.
