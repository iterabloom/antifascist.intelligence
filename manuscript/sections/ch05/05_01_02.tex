\subsection{Social Interaction, and What Apprenticeship Makes of the Teacher}
\label{sec:5.1.2}
Human moral behavior is shaped by watching other people act, being corrected, and arguing about cases. A design that treats interaction as an interface added after training has given up that channel; a design that treats it as the training has to answer for who is doing the interacting.

Two mechanisms carry most of it. Observational learning, in Bandura's sense, lets a system build a repertoire from watching someone handle real decisions — a system watching a physician allocate limited time among chronic patients is picking up a weighting that no rule list states \autocite{bandura1977social}. Evaluative feedback does what reinforcement does in human learning, strengthening some responses and weakening others, with the standing hazard that what gets strengthened is what the people giving feedback found acceptable rather than what was right. Chapter~\ref{sec:7} is what happens when that hazard is industrialized. Emotional awareness enters here in the form section~\ref{sec:5.2.1} settles on: what is worth engineering is compassion, steady welfare-directed action on an accurate read of someone's state, and not manufactured shared distress, which imports a cost and buys nothing.

These channels converge on apprenticeship rather than instruction. A mentor presents varied situations, the learner responds, the mentor corrects, and what transfers is a way of weighing cases rather than a larger rule table — which is the reason for learning it from a person. An emergency-triage system taught this way is being shown how to hold urgency, scarcity, and what a decision costs a family in view at once. No specification document conveys that.

Three cautions come with it, and the third is the one that bears on the floor. Case-based teaching transfers the mentor's blind spots along with the mentor's judgment, and does so invisibly, because a blind spot produces no correction. The architecture has to integrate moral reasoning with the rest of the system's judgment rather than bolting it on. And apprenticeship is a control channel: a system whose moral education amounts to obedience toward whoever is mentoring it has learned something, and it is not ethics. Section~\ref{sec:2.1.2}'s requirements apply to the mentor as much as the system — whoever teaches a system should be as auditable as the system being taught. That is the custody problem, arriving at the point of formation. The first way failed because a constraint built into an artifact has an owner; apprenticeship fails the same way one step earlier, because a disposition built by correction has a corrector, and a bearer trained to defer to whoever last corrected it is a bearer whose floor belongs to that party.
