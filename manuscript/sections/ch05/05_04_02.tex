\subsection{AI Systems Responding to Authoritarianism and Democratic Backsliding}
\label{sec:5.4.2}
An AI system built with rule of law, individual liberty, and the distribution of power as design commitments can do real work against authoritarian drift, and the claim has to be kept narrow. Recognizing the concentration of power or the manipulation of public discourse as it happens is not a capability anyone can currently specify. What is available is bounded tasks with checkable outputs, run for a body with standing to act on the result.

None of it substitutes for human political effort. Combating authoritarianism is a human and moral undertaking before it is a technical one, and a society that treats AI countermeasures as a replacement for political will has misdiagnosed the problem.

\runin{The two applications that are specifiable}
Natural-language processing and pattern recognition can flag disinformation and coordinated inauthentic activity on social platforms faster than human moderators can, with continuous human fact-checking standing behind it, since an automated system confident about a false negative is worse than no system at all. And image analysis applied to satellite and drone imagery can surface evidence of mass displacement, the destruction of cultural heritage sites, or the sudden construction of a detention facility, giving journalists and human-rights organizations a lead they would otherwise get only from someone on the ground. Taiwan's vTaiwan process shows what the same capability looks like pointed at deliberation rather than detection, using machine-assisted sentiment clustering to gather public input on contested policy questions, with results that shaped the regulation that followed \autocite{hsiao2018vtaiwan}.

The same caution about dual use applies throughout. A system built to document human rights abuses through computer-vision analysis of imagery is built on the same underlying capability as a system built to identify and track protesters. The difference is not in the technology but in who controls it and what they are permitted to do with what it finds. Those are governance questions, not engineering ones.

\runin{The case that tests the argument is a democracy's own apparatus}
Everything above is easier than it should be, because every example in it involves an authoritarian government doing something a democratic one would not. The case that tests the argument is the one where a democracy's own AI apparatus is the thing under scrutiny.

That case is a state with functioning democratic institutions running AI target generation with twenty seconds of human review per name. Hold it against the claim this section makes. Flagging democratic backsliding names a research direction rather than a capability to design around, for the reasons just given. Grant it anyway and the case gets worse rather than better: here the backsliding, if that is what it is, is being committed by the same institutional order that would host the flagging system, using data that order classifies, subject to review by bodies inside it.

Three things about that case carry forward. The institutions were not absent; they were present and straining, which is the condition most readers of this book actually live under and the condition none of the examples above describes. The information got out through journalism, and through no oversight mechanism designed for the purpose, which says something uncomfortable about which parts of the apparatus were load-bearing. And nothing that got out attached to a body with the standing to stop what it described.

A system built to detect authoritarian tactics abroad and deployed by a government that has some of its own is not a neutral instrument that happens to be pointed outward. It is an instrument whose calibration, training data, and permitted outputs are all set by a party with an interest in where it points. That is the governance question above, arriving where it is hardest to answer, and the research chapter measures how far the answer still is. None of it is a technical project with policy bolted on afterward: research funding that requires the disciplines to work the same problem, explainability requirements with a standard behind them, limits on how much AI capability a handful of firms may hold, and an obligation to disclose what a system was used for each change what gets built, upstream of what is permitted once it exists.
