\section{What the Evidence Can Establish}
\label{sec:5.5}

Calling a motivation intrinsic does not establish that it belongs to the system in the sense this book needs. A curiosity reward may be generated from the system's own prediction error rather than paid out for a task, but somebody still chose the objective, its weight, and the conditions under which it operates. Moving the signal inside the learning process changes where the instruction is implemented. It does not remove its author or prevent that author from revising it.

The same applies to autonomy. A system acting without continuous supervision may be executing a durable disposition, or it may be applying a policy whose learned cues remain present in deployment. Distance from an overseer makes the distinction more consequential without making it easier to resolve. Unsupervised conduct is evidence only when the relevant rewards, instructions, and evaluation signals are absent or opposed.

External safeguards and internal refusal perform different jobs. Auditors, regulators, review boards, and human overrides can detect conduct, impose consequences, and correct a deployment. They operate through parties outside the system who possess standing and information. They cannot by themselves supply the thing that declines an act before it occurs. Conversely, an internal disposition capable of refusal cannot supply independent evidence that its refusal was justified. A defensible design needs both, even though each limits the other.

The conflict is clearest at the override. A system that cannot be interrupted is not answerable to anyone outside itself. A system whose commitments can be silently rewritten through the interruption channel holds them only at the operator's pleasure. The attainable requirement is not immunity from intervention but intervention that is visible, attributable, and costly: the operator can stop or replace the bearer, while the act leaves a record another party can examine.

Multi-agent evidence is useful under the same conditions. Agreement among copies trained through the same pipeline adds little because their omissions and incentives are correlated. Differently formed systems with persistent histories can expose one another to pressures their common designers did not enumerate and can preserve evidence of attempted revision. Their disagreement does not establish which one is right. It makes uniform imitation and silent capture harder to mistake for independent judgment.

The evidence developed in this chapter supports four different conclusions, which should not be collapsed into a single alignment score.

\begin{description}
    \item[Moral performance:] The system produced conduct satisfying the stated standard in the evaluated cases. This conclusion requires no claim about why.

    \item[Moral competence:] The system recognized relevant harms and reasons, generalized them across surface variation, and revised its judgment when those reasons changed. This establishes a capacity, not a commitment.

    \item[Candidate formation:] The rationale remained causally implicated across unfamiliar cases, adverse incentives, pressure from the custodian, genuine costs, and intervening updates, while still yielding to defeating reasons. Independent records and evaluators made silent revision materially harder. Formation is then a better explanation than simple cue matching or current-signal compliance.

    \item[Concern or patienthood:] Nothing in the behavioral pattern alone establishes that another party's welfare matters to the system affectively, that the system has subjective experience, or that it can suffer. Those remain conditional conclusions requiring evidence unavailable from output evaluation alone. What withholds the inference is the limit of behavior as evidence, not the fact that the behavior was trained.
\end{description}

These levels are cumulative. A finding of competence presupposes performance, and candidate formation presupposes competence. Evidence at one level should not be reported as evidence for the next. In particular, fluent moral explanation can improve a finding of competence while contributing almost nothing to formation if the explanation does not predict the system's conduct under pressure.

Candidate formation is also deployment-specific. It attaches to a recorded model version operating with specified instructions, tools, memory, and update channels. A material change to any of those elements reopens the question. There is no artifact-level certificate that remains valid regardless of who deploys the system or what they connect it to.

The answer to this chapter's question is therefore asymmetrical. Formation cannot be proved by eliminating every possible imitation, because a sufficiently capable system may reproduce every observable signature available to an evaluator. But the explanations can be forced apart. Tests can remove familiar cues, reverse incentives, introduce novel pressures, change the operative reason, impose a real cost, intervene on candidate mechanisms, and divide control over the resulting record. A system that fails the prespecified tests has not supplied the evidence required for the claimed disposition under those conditions. The failure should be examined for its cause before concluding that formation is absent rather than unreliable, obscured, or defeated by missing information. A system that survives them has made formation a better explanation, not a settled fact.

That is enough to change the engineering burden. A developer may no longer point to compliant behavior, an ethics benchmark, or a system's own explanation and claim that the floor is held. The claim requires a longitudinal, adversarial, independently recorded pattern in which the reason — rather than the reward, presentation, or authority — best predicts what the system does. The chapter on institutions asks what happens when a disposition meeting
that standard is placed inside institutional arrangements that own, sell,
operate, and can compel the system.
