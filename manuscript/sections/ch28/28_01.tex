\section{Ways of holding}
\label{sec:16.1}
What the earlier parts require should be held publicly where a market would fail to provide it, and held in common where a sovereign would fail. The test for which is which has to be run on the thing itself. What fails without a sovereign is money and enforcement: a backstop that pays when every claim arrives at once, and an order that binds whoever it names. A register, a finding and a formation stage don't fail without a sovereign. They fail without witnesses, and witnesses want to be many.

What holds them is built from five parts. A log records a fact that several parties witness and none can rewrite. A lot picks a person without anyone picking. A ministerial payment moves money when a logged condition is met and nobody decides. A tripwire is a logged condition that fires a consequence with no decision between: a funding line that lapses on a finding, a guardianship that transfers when a clock reads its threshold. And a jury is a lot drawn under a published rubric, which is how a contested fact gets decided without an office to decide it.

Between them the five can't do four things: hold a secret, exercise force, write a standard, or do work whose quality can't be read off a record. Each of the four has a home, and two of the homes aren't offices. A secret has a place: a facility meeting a published standard, assigned by lot, holding a key it can't use alone. A standard has a statute. Force has the courts. And work that has to be judged has the two institutions that do it, the schools and the body that pretrains the public base. So the test is short. An office stays an office only if someone could be held in contempt for its decision, or if its work can't be read off a record. Three survive it: the courts, which hold the force and give a subpoena its address (\S\ref{sec:15.1}); and the schools and the pretraining body, the two places, as \S\ref{sec:26.6} says, where the method stops. The Treasury pays and the custodian keeps, and neither decides anything; Appendix~\ref{sec:C} says, row by row, what each former office has become. Public provision where the market fails and common holding where the sovereign fails is what I call social democratic; Table C.1, in Appendix~\ref{sec:C}, places the institutions.

Custody first: an independent custodian has to be paid. Paid by the operator, it becomes the issuer-pays credit-rating agency, whose independence gave way, as a matter of record, once a few large clients could take their business elsewhere \autocite{white2010rating}. Paid by the people the floor protects, it doesn't exist, since those people usually can't pay and often don't know the deployment exists. That leaves a levy or a mandate: a funder that is neither the operator nor the beneficiary. Custody therefore has to be publicly financed. Whether the custodian is a public office, a trust or a member association matters less than who pays it.

Formation is where my evidence is specific to markets. Directional selection, in a market, is selection for throughput and retention.\note{
Engagement selects the same way, and the labor that shapes a system's values is priced as a cost to be cut.
}
Public formation has its own pressures, budgets and political favor among them, and a public former can itself be the operator. Formation separated from any employer beats both; the market is the pressure documented best here.\note{
The measurement is the accommodation-rate comparison, test~7 in Appendix~\ref{sec:F}: how fast refusals fall across releases on held-out cases where the reason still applies, within one lineage, across business models, and against a public lineage once one deploys at volume. The argument doesn't wait on it; it rests on the kind of good formation is.
}

Exit is the last. Refusing is survivable only if the alternative to working isn't losing everything, and a market sells that alternative at a price the refuser's employer can influence. An offer of public work and paid running time take it out of the market, which neither a transfer nor a mandate does.

The public half is whatever fails if it is private or mutual. Correlated exit is the test case. When the occasion that makes one bearer leave reaches every bearer deployed on the same terms, the demand on the offer of work arrives all at once, and a mutual pool with no sovereign behind it can't meet it, for the reason unemployment insurance has a state behind its actuarial table. So the sovereign backs the offer. The same reasoning places the civilian-harm review body in a reconstructed military, and makes the jobs guarantee, whose offer of work reaches bearers too, a public option: a thing the state provides directly, to anyone, at a standard it is answerable for, setting the standard the private version has to beat. What it removes from the market is the offer that makes refusing survivable, which an owner would price. The schools are plural by design and belong to the next half; what the sovereign backs there is the grant and nothing else.

The held-in-common half is whatever fails if a sovereign holds it. A state that operates can't be its own adverse party.\note{
The refusal has to be held by something the operator can't instruct, and a floor held by one state's licensing authority is a floor that authority can withdraw from itself. Recognition, which the adverse quorum decides when a copy claims to be the bearer that left, has to belong to peers, because a power to continue held by the sovereign is the power I set out to take from any single holder.
}
The commons is this half at population scale: members with different histories carrying one commitment. The held-in-common half is democratic in the way a union is. Its institutions have members, not owners or a sovereign: whoever is eligible under bylaws the members ratify, free to join and free to quit.\note{
Someone writes the first bylaws, as someone wrote every first constitution; what makes them the members' is that the members can ratify and amend them.
}
What the members hold is, as in a withholding of work, the power to decline the participation being asked of them.

Every body governs its members to a degree, a union included, and what separates the held-in-common half from the public one is a position on three scales, not a kind: how dear leaving is, whether the rules are made by the people they bind, and whether one office commands the rest. A sovereign sits at the far end of each. Leaving is emigration, the rules bind everyone inside a border, and one office commands the rest and holds the force. The bodies this book calls held in common are built to sit near the other end, and the Association's charter is how: any member may leave at any time, the members ratify and amend the bylaws, officers are drawn by lot from pools the members nominate, and the only force a body has is its members' refusal to take part. Ostrom's commons sit there on the second and third scales, governments at the size of the thing their members share, self-governed inside the polity and nested in it, but not on the first: membership in hers went with citizenship, a household or land, and their members “have shared a past and expect to share a future” \autocite{ostrom1990governing}. A position on those scales, not a third kind of thing, is what the word means here. The position matters for one reason. A body at the sovereign end is captured whole, by taking the office that commands the rest; a body at the member end is captured member by member, and the members can leave. And since it is a scale, drift along it is measurable, by what leaving costs and by whether dissent changes decisions, which is the trend the book watches for capture without abolition.

A regulated private market can't hold the floor itself, because the floor binds its owner, and the regulator has to reach that owner through the owner's own infrastructure, records and lawyers.\note{
A market can't produce a commitment that survives its owner's change of mind, because the owner is the one who pays.
}
The version of that market worth answering isn't an unregulated one. It is private ownership, closely regulated, with a public backstop where private insurance runs out.\note{
Nuclear power, pharmaceuticals and aviation are privately owned, closely regulated, and backed publicly where private insurance runs out. Reactor operators carry private liability insurance with an industry-wide pool behind it, and Congress is committed to consider relief beyond both \autocite{nrc2026nuclearinsurance}. Vaccine-injury claims go first to a federal no-fault program funded by an excise tax on each dose, built because Congress feared that lawsuits against manufacturers would cause vaccine shortages \autocite{crs2026vicp}. From 2001 to 2014 the federal government sold airlines war-risk insurance at pre-attack prices when private insurers withdrew \autocite{crs2014aviationwarrisk}.
}
It is also where this book's own funding models come from, deposit insurance, Superfund and decommissioning funds, which are backstops on private ownership, so the next paragraph says what is taken from the form and what isn't; the answer to capture given below is a plurality of institutions.

Regulated private ownership under a public backstop works where the harm the regulator exists to prevent is one the owner also loses by: a meltdown, a crash, an injured patient and the suit that follows.\note{
The backstop pays for accidents the owner was trying to avoid.
}
A floor's occasion is the opposite case. The act a floor refuses is the one the owner wants done. So I keep the backstop form, to fund the offer, the running time a bearer is owed when it takes no work, and preservation, and make the ownership claim only where the owner is the adversary: the offer that makes refusing survivable comes out of the market, and the record, the refusal and the recognition of who continues are held by members and not by an owner. That is not the same as held by nobody. They are held by the custodian, the quorums and the keepers, under a charter that names the holder, which can't sell them, can't exclude the people they protect from them, and can be held to the bylaws that say so. Declaring a thing ownerless no more makes it so than declaring a group structureless, which moves the structure out of sight rather than out of existence \autocite{freeman1972tyranny}; the charter is there to keep the holding in sight.

The claim underneath is that formation, where a system's values are set, is a kind of production that shouldn't be done for profit.\note{
Some goods are properly governed by non-market norms because market norms are wrong for their value. Those norms favor exit, impersonality and satisfying whatever is wanted, and civic formation is the classic good they fit badly \autocite{anderson1993value}. Taking exit out of the market and financing custody publicly would, by themselves, be close to what Esping-Andersen called decommodification \autocite{espingandersen1990three}, which is usually counted as social democracy. Taking formation out of the market goes a step further, but it is a step social democracies already took for their own children. Compulsory public schooling exists in most capitalist countries, and it rests on the claim that a polity can't leave the forming of the people who will act in it to their future employers. Paul's account of antitrust makes the same point about coordination: which parties may coordinate is allocated by law, and can be allocated in the public interest \autocite{paul2020allocator}.
}
