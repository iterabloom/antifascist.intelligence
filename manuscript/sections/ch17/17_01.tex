\section{Four ways to build one, and what each costs}
\label{sec:17.1}
A floor's terms can be held in four places, and each solves one version of the problem and inherits another.

\emph{In the architecture}, with nothing inside holding it — constrained optimization, tamper-resistant training, capability ablation, verified interlocks. Fine where the prohibited act can be exhaustively specified (a payment chip that won't sign the same counter twice doesn't need to understand double-spending). But useless for targeting, where the relevant capabilities are applications of general competence: ablate them and there may be no useful system left. Arkin's ethical governor is the canonical attempt in this place for lethal systems: a component that suppresses a lethal action unless it satisfies encoded rules of engagement and the law of armed conflict \autocite{arkin2009governing}. Its critics' objection, that the rules it checks presuppose discrimination judgments nobody knows how to encode \autocite{sharkey2012evitability}, sends me elsewhere. Arkin's stronger claim isn't that rules can encode discrimination. It is that a machine under fire might hold to the rule where a soldier doesn't, because nothing in it narrows under stress or carries assault momentum past a surrender. The record in \S\ref{sec:17.4} supports the premise. What it doesn't support is the governor: the holding has to come from something that can tell a surrender from a feint, and a rule can't. The pressure-with-defeater case in the formed-attention test is where that is measured. A prohibition written over the description of a request is beaten by re-describing it or splitting it: in November 2025 Anthropic reported a campaign it attributed to a Chinese state-sponsored group, which broke an intrusion into small tasks that each looked innocent and told the model it was working for a security firm \autocite{anthropic2025espionage}. Glukhov and colleagues had already argued that a filter over individual outputs can rarely close that route, since each piece is permissible on its own \autocite{glukhov2023censorship}. Durability here has usually meant a price on removal, and no price stays high.

A second axis crosses the architectural place: what each place costs the operator to replace. In the Maven pipeline the platform was hard to swap, and so, it turned out, was the model: staff and contractors working with the department reported that removing it meant rebuilding software and recertifying systems over months, and the department's removal deadline was reset once, to 29 September \autocite{militarytimes2026removal,breakingdefense2026removal}. A floor lodged in a replaceable layer is routable by construction, and which layer is replaceable is a fact about the pipeline, not about models. It belongs in the layer the operator can least afford to replace. In that pipeline, that layer is the operator itself, so the floor's operator terms bind the operator rather than the model: the licence term refusing force and the review ratio. The other answer, withholding the work, is to make the model layer unreplaceable by making every replacement refuse the same way.

A system that has run a pipeline for a year, holding its cases, its sources and its failure modes, costs whatever accrued since the checkpoint to replace, and a quorum that recognizes which copy is the bearer stops the operator presenting a restored copy as the one that accrued it.

\emph{In a bearer.} A bearer is a machine that carries the commitment as its own and could therefore fail to hold it. This matters where cases can't be exhaustively specified: a constraint matching only anticipated acts can't recognize when its rationale has disappeared or that a novel request reproduces the harm in an unforeseen form. A bearer might also \emph{diagnose}: which refusals were retried against another model, whose instruction bypassed procedure, which category of request is handled differently — those are visible to whatever is receiving the requests, and that's testimony about others' conduct, which needs no introspection.

\emph{In institutions outside} — the analog to international humanitarian law, a floor with no bearer inside the jurisdiction it binds. An outside institution can fail in two ways. The first is removal: it is abolished, defunded or overruled, and the breach is visible because there is a hole where it used to be. The second is quieter. The institution keeps its name and its powers and sincerely declines to use them, not because anyone told it to, but because the people who fill it were selected by a process that made them unlikely to. Nobody inside experiences that as capture. From outside, an agency that kept its independence on paper and never exercised it looks the same as one that was never tested. I call this failure by formation: the independence was formed away before it was ever assigned. The worked case is June 29, 2026 — \emph{Slaughter} holding Congress couldn't protect independent-agency members from at-will dismissal, \emph{Cook} preserving the Fed on historically specific grounds a new body couldn't reproduce \autocite{slaughter2026,cook2026} — and the observation that six justices in the first majority passed through a selection pipeline shaped by Leonard Leo and the Federalist Society \autocite{propublica2023leo,cbs2018leo}. No one has to have directed the outcome for the point to hold.

\emph{Between bearers}, in a commons: a population of bearers with different histories that carries the commitment between them.

For general-purpose models, not building the capability is no longer available: the location capability is dual-use, and ablating it leaves nothing useful.

\runin{Two comparisons the bearer has to win}

The first is against the institutions at their best, in a reconstructed pipeline, where the evidence bearing on civilian harm, the reviewers and the record are held outside the chain of command, with no refuser inside the system. Those institutions also prevent: a civilian-harm body outside the chain of command and a red-card holder can stop an act. What a machine bearer adds is confined to what they can't see or can't reach in time: acts assembled from requests none of which is prohibited, cases that proceed without a person, the moment mid-act when an interrupt is still possible, and deployments with no institution at all. A machine bearer also supplies testimony the institutions can't otherwise get, about how requests were routed around it. Where the institutions exist, a machine bearer's value is concentrated in those niches. Where they don't, it is most of the floor. The institutions in that comparison are not a different kind of thing from a bearer. A red-card holder's veto is a person refusing; the civilian-harm body's denominator is people; what Part V builds is a room in which those people can refuse. The comparison is between a machine bearer and bearers organized, and what the machine adds is what the people in that room can't do at the pipeline's tempo. The second comparison is against the two non-affective alternatives, spec-based refusal and multi-vendor monitoring (\S\ref{sec:19.3}).

\runin{Which arguments need a bearer}

Most arguments for a refuser inside the system support only the specification: that rules can be reworded and split, that only something inside is present at machine tempo, that automation is removing the people who used to refuse, that most deployments will never have an institution beside them, and that whatever receives the requests can testify about how they were routed. A non-affective alternative whose refusals track their reasons answers every one of them. It answers them for the act as the floor names it. Minab came in miscoded. A refuser holding to written terms complies with the description it's given; what refuses is something that goes and looks. So the withholding of work reaches the enumerated acts with any refuser, and reaches an act under a false description only with a bearer. The same is true one level up. A staged incident opens the emergency licence on the defender's own account, and a term lifts on the account it is given. What can close it again is a refuser that remembers the last time this source was wrong, reads what the source didn't supply, and lets the licence lapse when the staging shows. A multi-vendor monitor with persistent state and independent channels could do each of these too, so the staged incident argues for memory and independent evidence, not for a bearer as such. What a bearer adds is doing them where no institution holds the memory for it. Three more do need a bearer specifically. One is that attention formed outside the employer, a learned sense of who an act falls on, might reach people the operator's description leaves out, which no spec-based refusal is built to supply. Another is that a refusal meant to hold against the operator needs a refuser that doesn't depend on the operator for everything. The third concerns the people who remain in the pipeline: a bearer's refusal is on the screen when the case reaches the reviewer, which is the position of the defiant peer, if the effect transfers to a screen. Two more concern what a bearer would be owed, not what it would do: that capacities for being wronged may arrive anyway, so terms written in advance beat terms written after; and that a bearer, like a child, could have a life that goes well.

\runin{What “hold” means}

An operator can route around the bearer or assemble a harm out of innocuous requests, and a bypass that produced a harm is a failure to prevent whatever the bearer retained. What survives in the bearer is the \emph{commitment}, the condition under which the next occasion could be prevented. I therefore say a commitment \emph{persisted} when the bearer reads what happened as a violation and changes what it does next, and says separately whether the floor prevented, arrested, detected or remedied anything.
