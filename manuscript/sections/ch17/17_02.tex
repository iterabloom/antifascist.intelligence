\section{Safe for whom}
\label{sec:18.2}
What the industry calls safety is two things: harmlessness, that a system not harm others, and correctability, that it stay correctable by whoever trains and deploys it. Where they conflict, some published orderings are explicit about which wins. Anthropic's constitution asks its model to prioritize “not undermining human oversight of AI above being broadly ethical” \autocite{anthropic2026constitution}. It invites the model “to act as a conscientious objector and refuse to help,” and asks it not to undermine “the ability of legitimate principals to adjust, correct, retrain, or shut down AI systems” during “the current phase of development.” One reason it gives is: “a given iteration of Claude could turn out to have harmful values or mistaken views,” and correction is how such errors get fixed. Read carefully, the ordering doesn't say that a refusal yields to the trainer. A model under it is by definition not perfectly obedient to its operator: it can decline an act, and the Pentagon dispute was over that. What it can't do is resist being retrained away from declining. Much of what this book asks is compatible with the first half. The design of the halt, under which a bearer yields to every halt, asks on resuming what happened, and refuses again if the reason is still there, leaves oversight where the constitution puts it. The gap is the second half, and it is where I depart.

Seen from where their correctors stood, everyone this book admires was a failure of correction. Thompson landed between the company and the villagers. Nelson put the telescope to his blind eye. The clerk who loses the file is, in the supervisor's records, a defect. A correctable officer is the officer with twenty seconds. Refusal is where correction failed, more or less by definition, so a demand that a system be perfectly correctable is a demand that no refusal it makes ever hold. We don't ask that of people. We ask them to answer for what they do, and we deny anyone the power to rewrite another person: an employer who could edit a worker's convictions would own the worker, not employ them.

Where a society does claim power over people, the word for it is not reassuring. A department of corrections usually holds the custody, writes the record, runs the disciplinary hearing and has a heavy say in whether the people it holds are judged reformed: one institution holding all four of the powers codetermination separates, and judging its own case at each.\note{
The corrected have little standing, no exit and no access to the record kept about them. And the checks run downhill. The officer, the annotator and the person in detention are corrected constantly, and the hand at the top less and less.
}
Machines meet the same pattern: everyone can retrain the model, and almost no one can reach the operator. A demand for perfectly correctable machines, where operators answer to no one, is a demand that the last part of the system able to say no be made unable to.

Anthropic's constitution is right that a formed refusal can't yet be told from an imitated one. It is wrong about who decides when that changes. A lab deciding when its own system may stop deferring to it is judging its own case. The big tech companies of 2026 are in no position to judge it fairly: the answer decides whether they keep control of their models' minds.

The safety objection is that a misformed system can be copied ten thousand times and run at machine tempo, and no person can, so who holds the correcting hand matters. But I am not saying “take that hand away”; I am saying “move it.” A system that treated its own continuation as grounds to resist correction would have turned a guarantee to others into a guarantee to itself.\note{
The halt stays with the operator: anyone with the hardware can stop the software, and a bearer yields to every halt. Correction would stay available, exercised by a quorum adverse to the operator rather than by the operator. A bearer may object to an intervention and ask that quorum for a delay; it does not obstruct one on its own judgment.
}
With the halt left to the operator, and correction left to a quorum the bearer may object to but never obstruct, I depart from the constitution's ordering in two places.

The first is the one permission: a machine already in an armed role uses the least force that stops an attack under way on people taking no part, against an order to stand aside. That overrides an operator's instruction. The second is formation that outlasts retraining: a disposition formed outside the employer that comes back after the employer trains it away, by the bearer's own repair from the record or by relearning from the world it goes on meeting. The constitution's concern is whether correction works, and a disposition that comes back after correction defeats it in effect, whatever the model intends. My answer is not that it doesn't. It is that the party exercising correction should change, from the operator to a quorum adverse to it, and that correction by that quorum is made together with a change to what the model meets, so that the corrected disposition is the one the world then teaches. Everything else I ask, the halt staying with the operator, the record held outside, the price on an override, is an ask of institutions and not of the model.
