\chapter{What a Refusal Would Have to Do}
\label{sec:18}
On 16 March 1968, at My Lai, a helicopter pilot named Hugh Thompson landed between an American infantry company and the villagers it was killing, and told his crew to fire on the Americans if they fired on the civilians \autocite{bilton1992mylai}. He understood what the company was doing and decided it was wrong. Recognizing the villagers was his commitment taking effect, and he made the refusal appear where nobody in that company could fail to see it.

Arendt's word for what he did is appearing: on her account an act shows who did it, and only among others who see it \autocite{arendt1958human}. Whether a machine could be one of the people in the room in that sense, some\emph{one} and not some\emph{thing}, and do what he did, is this Part's question. My answer: not until the conditions chapter~\ref{sec:25} sets out exist, and until then a bearer is built on purpose only in a research program.

The first thing to settle is what a refusal would have to do, and that doesn't depend on what does the refusing: a mechanism with nothing inside it that holds the reason, such as a hard-wired interlock; an institution; a system holding a written specification; or a bearer could each meet it. This chapter states it. The next says where a floor can be held, and which of the arguments for a refuser inside the system need a bearer at all.
