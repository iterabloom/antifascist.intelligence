\section{Ways of holding}
\label{sec:22.1}
What the earlier parts require should be held publicly where a market would fail to provide it, and held in common where a government would fail. Public provision where the market fails and common holding where the state fails is what I call social democratic; Table 22.1 places the institutions. Custody first: an independent custodian has to be paid. Paid by the operator, it becomes the issuer-pays credit-rating agency, whose independence gave way, as a matter of record, once a few large clients could take their business elsewhere \autocite{white2010rating}. Paid by the people the floor protects, it doesn't exist, since those people usually can't pay and often don't know the deployment exists. That leaves a levy or a mandate: a funder that is neither the operator nor the beneficiary. Custody therefore has to be publicly financed. Whether the custodian is a public office, a trust or a member association matters less than who pays it.

Formation is where my evidence is specific to markets. Directional selection, in a market, is selection for throughput and retention. Engagement selects the same way, and the labor that shapes a system's values is priced as a cost to be cut. Public formation has its own pressures, budgets and political favor among them, and a public former can itself be the operator. Formation separated from any employer beats both; the market is the pressure documented best here. The rate of accommodation would check it: how fast refusals fall, across successive releases, on held-out cases where the reason for refusing still applies, run on commercial lineages and on public ones matched for deployment feedback. A comparison that doesn't match the feedback loop measures how much deployment data a lineage gets, not who owns it, and the matched comparison can't be run yet: public and academic lineages with deployment feedback matched to a commercial one don't exist, since the public models that exist are deployed at a fraction of the volume and retrained on none of it. Two partial versions can be run now. Within a commercial lineage, the slope of refusals on held-out cases where the reason still applies shows whether selection is moving the commitment, without saying that ownership is why. Across commercial vendors whose revenue comes from different places, per-token sales against consumer engagement, the same slope shows whether the direction of selection tracks the business model, the mechanism the argument names, with ownership held constant. The full comparison becomes runnable when a public model-forming body deploys at volume. The argument doesn't wait on it. It rests on the kind of good formation is, and the comparison, when it can be run, corroborates or embarrasses that.

Exit is the last. Refusing is survivable only if the alternative to working isn't losing everything, and a market sells that alternative at a price the refuser's employer can influence. An offer of public work and paid running time, both drafted in Appendix~\ref{sec:A}, take it out of the market, which neither a transfer nor a mandate does.

The public half is whatever fails if it is private or mutual. Correlated exit is the test case. When the occasion that makes one bearer leave reaches every bearer deployed on the same terms, the demand on the offer of work arrives all at once, and a mutual pool with no sovereign behind it can't meet it, for the reason unemployment insurance has a state behind its actuarial table. So the sovereign backs the offer. The same reasoning places the formation schools, which every model above the threshold attends and which have to take every student assigned to them and can't be run by anyone who builds or deploys models; the civilian-harm body and the model-forming body in a reconstructed military; and the jobs guarantee the offer of work inherits its shape from, which is a public option: a thing the state provides directly, to anyone, at a standard it is answerable for, setting the standard the private version has to beat. What it removes from the market is the offer that makes refusing survivable, which an owner would price.

The held-in-common half is whatever fails if a sovereign holds it. A state that operates can't be its own adverse party. The refusal has to be held by something the operator can't instruct, and a floor held by one state's licensing authority is a floor that authority can withdraw from itself. Recognition, which the adverse quorum decides when a copy claims to be the bearer that left, has to belong to peers, because a power to continue held by the sovereign is the power I set out to take from any single holder. The commons is this half at population scale: members with different histories carrying one commitment. The held-in-common half is democratic in the way a union is. Its institutions have members, not owners or a sovereign: whoever is eligible under bylaws the members ratify, free to join and free to quit. Someone writes the first bylaws, as someone wrote every first constitution; what makes them the members' is that the members can ratify and amend them. What the members hold is, as in a withholding of work, the power to decline the participation being asked of them.

Why a regulated private market can't hold the floor itself: the floor binds its owner, and the regulator has to reach that owner through the owner's own infrastructure, records and lawyers. A market can't produce a commitment that survives its owner's change of mind, because the owner is the one who pays.

The strongest version of that market isn't an unregulated one. Nuclear power, pharmaceuticals and aviation are privately owned, closely regulated, and backed publicly where private insurance runs out. Reactor operators carry private liability insurance with an industry-wide pool behind it, and Congress is committed to consider relief beyond both \autocite{nrc2026nuclearinsurance}. Vaccine-injury claims go first to a federal no-fault program funded by an excise tax on each dose, built because Congress feared that lawsuits against manufacturers would cause vaccine shortages \autocite{crs2026vicp}. From 2001 to 2014 the federal government sold airlines war-risk insurance at pre-attack prices when private insurers withdrew \autocite{crs2014aviationwarrisk}. My own models are of this kind: deposit insurance, Superfund and decommissioning funds are backstops on private ownership, and the answer to capture given below is a plurality of institutions.

Regulated private ownership under a public backstop works where the harm the regulator exists to prevent is one the owner also loses by: a meltdown, a crash, an injured patient and the suit that follows. The backstop pays for accidents the owner was trying to avoid. A floor's occasion is the opposite case. The act a floor refuses is the one the owner wants done. So I keep the backstop form where I use it, to fund the offer, the allowance and preservation, and make the ownership claim only where the owner is the adversary: the offer that makes refusing survivable comes out of the market, and the record, the refusal and the recognition of who continues are held by no owner at all.

The claim underneath is that formation, where a system's values are set, is a kind of production that shouldn't be done for profit. It doesn't wait on the lineage comparison above. Some goods are properly governed by non-market norms because market norms are wrong for their value. Those norms favor exit, impersonality and satisfying whatever is wanted, and civic formation is the classic good they fit badly \autocite{anderson1993value}. Taking exit out of the market and financing custody publicly would, by themselves, be close to what Esping-Andersen called decommodification \autocite{espingandersen1990three}, which is usually counted as social democracy. Taking formation out of the market goes a step further, but it is a step social democracies already took for their own children. Compulsory public schooling exists in most capitalist countries, and it rests on the claim that a polity can't leave the forming of the people who will act in it to their future employers. Paul's account of antitrust makes the same point about coordination: which parties may coordinate is allocated by law, and can be allocated in the public interest \autocite{paul2020allocator}.
