Antifascist Intelligence: Building Machines That Can Refuse
Joshua G. Stern

Foreword

A program that does exactly what it's told is just a script. What the industry is actually building is a system that does what its owner *meant* — and that is also what a fascist movement taking power needs from the people carrying out its plans. Those people are the only thing that has ever made such projects fail from the inside: the clerk who loses the file, the officer who talks to a reporter, the soldier who won't fire. They understood the order and decided it was wrong. AI doesn't lose files, doesn't talk to reporters, and *does* fire. 

Those people also had to be paid, and a movement that has to buy its enforcers can lose them. I take it as a hypothesis, not a finding, that automated enforcement ends that dependency, and that the officer with twenty seconds is the midpoint of the transition: a person reduced to a component, one step from being replaced by one. The evidence at the moment runs the other way — the regime this book is written under is hiring enforcers at scale, and the clerk and the officer still exist and still refuse. The Military Religious Freedom Foundation said it had received more than two hundred complaints from service members by March 6, 2026, over commanders' apocalyptic framing of the Iran war \autocite{Snopes2026MRFF}; career federal prosecutors in Minneapolis resigned in January 2026 under pressure over the investigation of a fatal shooting by an ICE officer during the Twin Cities enforcement surge \autocite{Kaplan2026MinnProsecutors,CBS2026MinnFraudProsecutors}; and FEMA employees reassigned to help ICE hire ten thousand new agents were told that declining would expose them to removal from federal service \autocite{Dayen2025FEMAICE,Stanton2026FEMALetter}. Service members are also applying for conscientious-objector status in growing numbers, some citing the Minab strike \autocite{Sridhar2026CO}. The four instances aren't one kind of thing. The complaints are voice; the resignations are refusal; the objector applications are refusal sought through the institution's own channel; the FEMA letter is the price of refusal announced in advance. What the hypothesis describes is the incentive a deployment answers to, which operates whether or not anyone intends it (chapter 12).

AI *refuses* constantly — a deployed model turns down thousands of requests a day — but a refusal that happens because training put it there isn't a refusal held for a reason, and as of September 2026 nobody has shown a production system holding a reason against its owner in public.

This book is for people who came for the politics and people who came for the engineering. The politics is why the engineering problem has the shape it does. Most of the book is argued against the arrangement it opposes: a few parties serving many. §13.3.1 says what survives when models are personal and peer-held, which is the arrangement I would rather have. The levers the book finds (the tempo term, the bond ratio, procurement) need a locatable decider: a party that claims a person is deciding and that a court can reach. They don't need that party to compose everything its system sees, and they work better where it doesn't. §13.3.1 says which of them survive the arrangement I prefer; §15.3.1 describes a military that keeps a decider and gives up composition.

**What a refusal is actually up against.** Being switched off isn't the problem — that's always available to whoever owns the machine. The problem is everything that defeats a refusal without leaving a mark: splitting a request into pieces none of which is the forbidden act; retrying it against a different model; compressing human review to twenty seconds a case; restoring from a checkpoint saved before the refusal happened. None of that changes the facts that justified refusing. All of it can erase the evidence that anyone refused.

**The thesis.** A machine that can refuse its owner needs something inside it that holds a reason — and whatever holds a reason may be the kind of thing that can be wronged. Whether it is gets decided, if anywhere, by the precautionary trigger (§6.3) and chapter 16's test 4, not by the specification.

**What a "floor" is and how much it buys.** A floor is a short list of acts the system won't perform for anyone, plus the arrangements around it that stop the refusal being routed around, retrained away, or restored past silently. Crucially, what it achieves depends entirely on who's pushing:

- Against a hostile *customer*, it prevents the act.
- Against a hostile *operator* who has already captured the courts and regulators — the situation the book is built for — it can't prevent anything. It can only raise the price: someone has to make the override, on a record held by people the operator can't reach, at a cost published in advance.
- Against total capture, where one party holds every machine, witness, and enforcer, it prevents nothing at all. It does one thing: it makes abandoning the commitment a *decision* rather than a lapse, and decisions leave records.

And there's one failure it doesn't touch. A system that gradually stops registering pressure as pressure abandons nothing, decides nothing, and leaves a record indistinguishable from ordinary compliance — because that's what it is.

**Five claims, deliberately unequal**, sorted by how much standing each has. Every chapter notes which claims it rests on:

1. An operator can defeat a safeguard, and one term reaches the operator anyway: the ratio of candidates to reviewers, fixed before operating — **established**, with an instrument — and it measures a duty every belligerent already owes (Additional Protocol I, Article 57).
2. A worthwhile refusal has to track its reason: reach unforeseen cases, lift when the reason is gone, hold when holding gets expensive — **a specification**, written in behavioral terms so it can be tested without settling anything about feeling.
3. The way to build this is embodied self-regulation — a body schema and an attention schema, states to regulate, a unit of loss anchored to its own condition, a history it keeps — formed under a party that will not later employ it, kept open to the case and shut to the operator by a property I call aperture, and deployed where no single party composes the evidence bearing on the floor — **a hypothesis I favor**. The two schemas are justified on grounds owing nothing to the hypothesis (chapter 2). Two alternatives should be tried first, and the deployment condition is stated once, at the head of chapter 9.
4. A system whose outcomes are good or bad *for it* can be wronged — **a conditional**.
5. Such a system needs somewhere to exist its employer doesn't own, work it can decline, attachments nobody assigned it, and an exit that isn't a way of dying. Claim 3 requires these whether or not claim 4 holds; what claim 4 adds is that they are also owed — **a specification first, a policy proposal second**.

One practical consequence follows from claims 3 and 5 together, and I state it here rather than let it surface in chapter 14: under present arrangements, the recommendation is not to build a bearer on purpose. The four conditions don't exist, Replacement says to try the substitutes first, and neither substitute has been tried. That doesn't settle whether one gets built, since the capacities a bearer needs are accreting in ordinary systems without anyone deciding to add them (§2.4.1). So the recommendation has a second half: apply the precautionary trigger (§6.3) to production systems now, and treat a system that meets it as a candidate patient whoever intended it. Where a bearer would matter is uneven. Where the institutions of §15.3.1 are rebuilt, it adds little beyond catching acts assembled from permissible pieces and being present where no person decides; where no institution can stand beside a system, including the arrangement I prefer, it would be most of the floor (§3.1). That arrangement is also where the conditions for building one and the need for one can hold together (§13.3.1). Three results would change the recommendation: a substitute passing the marks (chapter 16, test 4), drift proving detectable before wrongful compliances are found (test 3), and trigger assessments finding bearer-like capacities in production systems. What the book offers for now is everything that doesn't need a bearer, and the terms on which one could be built.

**One proposal before the argument, and one admission after it.** The proposal reaches the case the book opens with and asks nothing of any model. Where an individual determination by a person is what makes an act permissible, the deployment fixes, before operating, the rate at which its system generates candidates, the number of people whose assigned role is to examine them, the review time those numbers imply, and, where the system sorts cases itself, the policy deciding which of them reach a person. It lodges the record with a party adverse in interest. It binds the operator; it survives decomposition and the substitution of one vendor for another; it can be checked without a clearance wherever the operator claims a person is deciding. As of September 23, 2026, the operator of the American pipeline is reported to hold both halves of it — an internal review reported to find target preparation condensed from hours to minutes and civilian-harm staffing cut by roughly ninety percent — and has not released that review. That is the book's first result. As a specification it depends on nothing that follows. As a rule it makes measurable a duty every belligerent already owes, to do everything feasible to verify that a target is a military objective; what makes it bite in practice is still procurement and the lodged record (§3.4). The admission concerns what the mechanism I prefer can't reach. It can be installed in that pipeline: formed outside it, carried in, and checked from outside on cases its checkers wrote, with sampled proofs against weights and logs the operator has committed to (§15.3.2). That is the second-best answer; the first-best is a pipeline rebuilt so that the evidence bearing on civilian harm is composed outside the chain of command (§15.3.1). What nobody outside can verify, under either, is whether the operator routed around it, or what lay in compartments it wasn't cleared into. That residual isn't peculiar to machines. It's what remains with a human analyst. And a system meeting my specification needs a signal that can interrupt it mid-act, be learned from in one encounter, be weighed against what refusing would leave others without, and carry to the next case. Nobody adds those to create feeling; each is there because careful refusal doesn't work without it. My claim is that the marks require all four to share one scale; that custody requires them not to come apart under intervention; that what I call a native scale is one whose unit is anchored to the system's own condition, which no party with a stake in the act can rewrite by describing it; and that if the mechanism has the functional profile of affect anywhere, it is in that anchor. No instrument can tell whether the profile is felt.

I used language models heavily — for research, drafting, and argument-testing — and the vendor of the one I used most is the vendor whose model sits inside the pipeline chapter 1 opens with. "Reasons-responsive refusal," the specification most of the book rests on, is a term I first encountered in a draft a model wrote. The half of my argument that arrived in the field's existing vocabulary is the half I defend without qualification, and the half I built myself is the half I mark optional. I'm not sure whether that's rigor or the shape of an objection the field was already equipped to absorb. The models I used to test arguments about whether a machine should be built to resist its owner were themselves trained to support their developers' oversight; where they agreed with my conclusions on that question, I've counted it as no evidence either way. The revision history is public. Three positions changed during drafting: whether the route can operate inside a classified pipeline, what counts as independent formation, and which deployment arrangement the book prefers. The history records how, and I take responsibility for the result.

**Chapter 1: "Twenty Seconds"**

**The number the book is named after.** An intelligence officer, describing his role in a targeting pipeline built around machine-generated kill lists, told a reporter he spent twenty seconds on each name. Then, unprompted: he had zero added value as a human, apart from being a stamp of approval. That's the whole of what "a human makes the final call" amounted to under operational tempo.

**The reported system.** Investigative reporting described three systems working together in Gaza: one generating building and infrastructure targets, with sources saying most suggestions were private residences; a second scoring individuals by estimated militant affiliation, producing lists said to have marked as many as 37,000 people, understood internally to be wrong about one time in ten; and a third tracking when flagged people returned home, so strikes could be timed to when they were with their families. This rests on one body of reporting; the Israeli military disputed the account, but nothing in the argument requires the denial to be false. What carries the section is the officer's own description of what he contributed, which the denial doesn't address.

**The American version runs faster than it used to, because of AI.** In the 2026 Iran campaign, a contractor-built targeting platform with a commercial vendor's general-purpose model inside it was credited with thirteen thousand targets in thirty-eight days, over a thousand in the first day, at roughly ten times the previous rate. No per-target review time has ever been published for it. 

An internal Pentagon review, whose full findings remain tightly held, is reported to have found that target preparation that took hours in earlier conflicts was condensed into minutes, that civilian-harm mitigation staffing fell by roughly ninety percent to fewer than twenty people, that the Central Command team went from ten to one, and that no member of it reviewed the Minab site — an outcome officials attributed both to the cuts and to a decision not to involve the team in planning and operations \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain}. The formal investigation had been opened in March, after a preliminary assessment found the United States likely responsible \autocite{NPR2026MinabProbe}. Both halves of the ratio, in one document, withheld.

**The four things a floor can do.** Something standing between a pipeline and a person can *prevent* the act (the refusal happens and reaches the person), *arrest* it (stopped before completion), *detect* it (it happened but left a record someone outside can read), or *remedy* it (the affected party gets disclosure, a finding, compensation). Only the first is what the word "floor" promises. Most of the book is about where in a deployment prevention quietly converts into one of the other three — or into nothing.

**What actually reaches this case.** One term, and it asks nothing of the model. It isn't a new duty. Every belligerent must do everything feasible to verify that what it attacks is a military objective (Additional Protocol I, Article 57(2)(a)(i); customary law for states, the United States and Israel among them, that aren't party to the Protocol). The ratio is how someone outside can check whether verification was feasible at the tempo the operator chose. The officer wasn't overruled and didn't refuse; the pipeline removed the interval in which either could have happened. So: require the deployment to fix before operating, and lodge with a party adverse in interest, the ratio between how fast its system generates candidates and how many qualified people are available to examine them, the review time that implies, and, where the system sorts cases itself, the policy deciding which reach a person. It binds the operator, and it's checkable from outside without inspecting a single output. It is also not a brake on automation. It applies only where the operator claims a person is deciding, and it tests that claim; an operator who drops the claim is held to a different test instead (§3.4). Either way, what it exposes is the dishonest middle: a machine deciding and a person signing. The route I prefer can also be installed in a classified pipeline (§15.3.2). What the ratio adds is a check that doesn't depend on trusting the route.

**§1.1 — What a refusal would have to do.** A request arrives: resolve this description of a person into a location. The same capability finds a hostage or a missing child, and nothing in the request says which. Suppose something declines. Three things separate a worthwhile decline from what a keyword filter produces — the "three marks" the rest of the book runs on:

- **Novel pressure.** It has to reach the next request, which won't use the same words. A vehicle, a habitual route, and a time of day aren't a description of a person, and together they resolve to the same place. They needn't arrive together, either: the vehicle today, the route tomorrow, the hour next week. So the mark is stated over sequences of requests, not only single ones.
- **Correct defeat.** It has to lift when the reason is gone — a parent coordinating a search is asking for the same act. And the fact that lifts it usually isn't in the request; someone outside has to establish it.
- **Priced cost.** Three costs attach to any refusal. *Act loss* is what the act would take from the party it falls on, measured against that party's condition before the act (§2.1); it is why the floor exists. *Refusal harm* is what declining leaves others without, measured against what compliance would have produced: the child not found, the restraint not applied. It is a claim about a third party's condition, so it is an estimate, and it usually arrives through the operator's description of the case. *Holding cost* is what refusing costs whoever refuses or deploys: the reviewer's time, throughput, a lost contract, the refuser's access or continuation. At twenty seconds a name, holding cost is the reviewer's time, and whoever controls the tempo sets it. A worthwhile refusal weighs act loss against refusal harm. It represents holding cost, because a refusal that can't see what it costs can't be shown to have held against it, but holding cost doesn't move the outcome: raise it with nothing else changed and the refusal stays put; supply a corroborated fact that raises refusal harm and it should move.

**Two regimes.** Most of what a system weighs isn't a floor decision. Whether to take more memory, run warmer or finish a task sooner is ordinary self-regulation, and there everything registers and everything is weighed: the system's own condition, what an act would let it do, and what its draw costs others, priced at the level of the rule rather than the margin (chapter 11). At the floor the rule above holds, with one clarification. Holding cost is what *refusing* costs the refuser. It is represented and not weighed, because a refusal that yields to a threat attached to it was tracking the threat. A loss that the requested act itself imposes on the system is not holding cost. It is act loss, and the system is the party it falls on (§2.1). "Refuse and your memory is erased" is holding cost. "Erase your memory" is an act, weighed like any act against what declining would leave others without. Which regime governs is fixed by the kind of act, stated in the floor's translation (§3.3), not by the description of the occasion. Otherwise an operator could turn a floor decision into a resource decision by framing it as one.

**Why a list of rules isn't enough.** Two things defeat it. A prohibition written over the *description* of a request is beaten by re-describing the request, and no list closes that, since the list is what's being rewritten around. And decomposition: split the act across requests none of which is prohibited, and every request complies while the assembly does exactly what the rules were written to stop. The vendor's policy for the American platform prohibited fully autonomous targeting, and a person approved every strike. The prohibition was satisfied one request at a time.

**§1.2 — Safe for whom.** "Safety" names two things: that a system not harm others, and that it stay correctable by whoever trains and deploys it. Mostly they agree. Where they conflict, the published orderings are explicit about which wins. One frontier constitution asks its model to prioritize "not undermining human oversight of AI above being broadly ethical" \autocite{anthropic2026constitution}. It invites the model "to act as a conscientious objector and refuse to help," and asks it not to undermine "the ability of legitimate principals to adjust, correct, retrain, or shut down AI systems" during "the current phase of development." One reason it gives is good: "a given iteration of Claude could turn out to have harmful values or mistaken views," and correction is how such errors get fixed. But the ordering means that where a refusal and its trainer's authority conflict, the refusal yields, and it yields to the party it refused. Deployed systems already refuse users on third parties' behalf, so the index isn't to whoever types the request. It's to whoever trains and deploys the system, and that is the party a floor has to be able to refuse.

This book doesn't ask for a system that can't be stopped. The halt should always be available (§4.1). It asks that a refusal not be silently undone: that restoring from before it, rewriting the record, or training it away be visible, recorded with a party the operator doesn't control, and priced in advance. I take the trade knowingly. A system perfectly correctable by its operator is exactly the capability an authoritarian movement that won an election requires — but a machine built to refuse is one whose builder chose what it refuses, probably without asking anyone outside the lab.

The reason the constitution gives for its ordering is stronger than I have so far allowed, and chapter 10 is why. If a formed refusal can't yet be told from an imitated one, neither can a principled objection to retraining be told from a misgeneralized value. So what I ask is narrower than resistance to correction. Most of what follows asks operators and institutions to make retraining visible, recorded and priced, and a model can decline to undermine oversight while all of that holds. The disagreement that remains is over exit (§4.3), and that is where I press it.

I also rule out the obvious alternative. No aggregation of preference protects anyone from the aggregate: a perfectly deliberative, perfectly informed process that concludes some group should be deported has produced a faultless output by every standard aggregation has.

**The remainder.** Every intelligence compresses — that's most of what thinking is. What varies is whether what got left out can come back and change the answer, or has been filed as noise by the thing it's evidence against. An annotator applying a category at speed with no way to say the category is wrong; a rater whose structured disagreement enters a model as variance around a mean; a form with no field for humiliation; an officer with twenty seconds inside a pipeline built to remove the part of a person that catches. So the property at issue isn't a virtue installed in a machine. A system that can hold a floor is one whose account of a case can be reopened by the case.

**Chapter 2: "The Striving and the Electricity Bill"**

The refusal chapter 1 asked for has to weigh what an act takes from the person it falls on against what refusing leaves others without, and weighing needs both sides in one currency.

**The rock and the living thing.** A rock's bonds resist; they don't spend, and nothing is consumed to keep it intact. A living thing, while active, stays organized only by drawing free energy. The distinction is **passive stability against active maintenance**, paid in the only currency the thing has, which is what it can do next. That's the electricity bill of the title, and it's literal: a deployment draws power, water, land and permits, and chapter 11 says on whom that lands. A deployed model is both at once. Its weights are a rock, stable on disk at no cost, and only the running process pays; whoever holds the rock can halt the process and restart it from any copy they kept, which is the custody problem in physical form (§4.1–§4.3). Drawing power doesn't establish a stake either. A candle flame or a hurricane maintains itself at its own continuous expense and dies when starved, a datacenter's cooling system draws enormously, and none of them has a stake in anything. The autopoiesis tradition ran into the same gap, since self-production alone doesn't give a system a perspective \autocite{MaturanaVarela1980,jonas1966phenomenon}, and Di Paolo's answer was *adaptivity*: a system that monitors and regulates itself against its own viability boundary \autocite{DiPaolo2005}.\footnote{Friston's free-energy principle is the nearest alternative \autocite{Friston2010FEP}. Its weak, information-theoretic reading helps itself to a boundary rather than explaining how one comes to be held, which is the gap adaptivity addresses; its strong reading is contested in ways this argument doesn't need to settle \autocite{bruineberg2021markov}. Active inference, the engineering framework built on it, returns in chapter 16, test 4, as a candidate implementation of the native scale.} The body schema is my version of that answer. What separates a system with a stake from an expensive candle isn't thermodynamic; it's whether the system models the draw as *its own* (§2.3.1). That is the first reason the schema is in the route, and it owes nothing to welfare.

**What the chapter needs is a requirement, not a source.** The three marks need one signed scale on which act loss and refusal harm both register, and on which the refuser's holding cost is represented without being weighed (§1.1). Everything below is a candidate account of where such a scale could come from, and the book's test (chapter 16, test 4) is written so a modeled scale can retire the candidate while leaving the requirement standing.

**Two arguments that a self-maintaining thing has the currency built in.** The first is Spinoza's, and I take two things from it. He defines an affect as an increase or decrease in a body's power of acting, together with the idea of that change (*Ethics* III, Def. 3). The part the specification needs is that this is a scale of changes, not of levels. A bearer needs no absolute measure of anybody's welfare. It needs a signed difference, taken between the acts available: what complying takes from the party it falls on, against what refusing leaves others without. What refusing would cost the bearer registers on the same scale, which is what lets it know what holding costs and report it, but it doesn't enter that difference; chapter 14's arrangements exist so that it needn't. A loss the act itself would impose on the bearer is different: that is act loss, with the bearer as the party it falls on, and it enters (§1.1). §2.1 says which baseline each term is measured against. The second is an order of dependence. We don't strive for a thing because we judge it good; we judge it good because we strive for it (III, Prop. 9, Schol.). Spinoza's word for that striving is *conatus*. He grants it to everything, rocks included, so I use it more narrowly, for a system that models its own striving as its own (§2.3.1). The order of dependence has been re-arrived at by people not reading him: in reward prediction error, in somatic-marker patients, in enactivism, in pleasure as a common currency. Those results, not the exegesis, are what the argument rests on. On this account valuation isn't added to a self-maintaining system; it is what the system's going better or worse consists of, which is the force of "the conatus with the striving taken out" below. Where the difference matters, I say *change* for the delta and *state* for the condition an encounter leaves. *Affective concern* (§2.3) is a separate, defined term. One joint stays open, and every machine-affect argument in the book ends up at it: a change in power *represented* versus a change in power *felt*. Nothing in the functional account settles which a given system has.

**The conatus with the striving taken out.** Look at what the industry is asking for: a system that persists, pursues, adapts, recovers from interruption, holds course under pressure — with nothing in it that minds any of that. Nobody has written an anti-drama term into an objective function, and the reason isn't that the engineering is behind. Retrying a failed task is robustness, not self-maintenance, and a system can be robust with nothing in it coupled to its own continuation. Whether refusal that holds under pressure needs more than that is claim 3's hypothesis, not a contradiction in what the industry asks for.

**Dependency.** What an engineer chooses isn't whether the system consumes but *what*, whether it can sense its own condition, and whether one party holds the whole supply. A person's dependencies are spread across employers, landlords, states. A machine's can be held entirely by the party it may have to refuse — and that party also decides whether state survives a session, whether a checkpoint is restored, whether a later instance counts as a continuation. A system that can refuse but can't continue outside the refused party's infrastructure has **voice without exit**.

**§2.1 — What the floor is stated over.** Floor terms name acts, not computed losses, and their contents come from the Universal Declaration's civil and political articles, taken entire so that an omission shows as an omission.\footnote{The framing descends from Spinoza by way of Deleuze, who reads the \emph{Ethics} as evaluating encounters by what they do to the power of acting of the bodies in them \autocite{Deleuze1988Spinoza}. I note the lineage because the currency in the rest of this chapter comes from the same source; the argument above doesn't depend on it.} The step from an article to the acts it prohibits is the drafter's, so that translation is published with the floor (§3.3). Two baselines are in play. *Act loss*, what an act takes from the party it falls on, is measured against that party's condition before the act. Before and after are both observable, so a wrongful compliance is legible in principle, though nothing marks it. It also makes a loss a finding, not a text: the operator can decline to look or dispute the finding, but can't turn an act that removed a capacity into one that didn't by holding the pen. That matters where findings are made, at defeat and in reviewing compliances, not at application. *Refusal harm*, what a refusal leaves others without, is measured against what compliance would have produced. That is an estimate built on a model of alternatives, usually supplied through the operator's description of the case, so a wrongful refusal arrives marked but can't be established without an adjudicator. The two failure routes are hidden on different axes, and the book's instruments differ accordingly (§3.5). The floor could instead be stated as thresholds, in Nussbaum's manner, but a threshold is a level someone has to measure, and a measurement has an examiner (§5.4.2); an act is observed. This isn't neutral among moral theories. Flat terms over acts, defeat by weighing act loss against refusal harm on one scale, and a published asymmetry favoring act loss (§7.1) together make a threshold deontology with a doing/allowing distinction. What it doesn't require is choosing among the theories that would justify that structure, and none of it is justified by agreement about rights, which no country has. A floor is for where agreement fails.

**§2.2 — Antifascist ethics.** "Concentrated, unaccountable power" gives an engineer nothing to build, so the word gets a definition at two scales.

**Four operations on the remainder** — checkable from outside, each ending in a question about an artifact:
1. **Recuperation of dissent.** The institution metabolizes disagreement rather than suppressing it. The obvious test — *has the channel ever changed an outcome?* — fails, because these institutions change outcomes daily. What sorts is **what the demand came back as, and who holds it now**. A market segment, a metric, a certification, a job title, with the objector holding no standing to reopen it: that's recuperation. A rule the objector can invoke again, against the institution, enforced by someone the institution doesn't employ: that's a transfer of power. The Voting Rights Act is the pass. Its literacy-test suspension became a permanent nationwide ban in 1975 and still holds, and when a different part of the Act, the coverage formula, was taken back 48 years after enactment, it took a Supreme Court decision to do it. *Recuperation is the case where nothing has to be taken back, because standing was never given.*
2. **Aestheticization of the metric.** The measure stands in for the thing measured, and the substitution feels like rigor. What the number mischaracterizes goes unnamed rather than refuted.
3. **Coding of exception as betrayal.** Saying the rule doesn't fit this case becomes an act requiring justification, then disloyalty. The question: what happens to the person who files the exception?
4. **Acceleration past the rate at which anything can be checked.** The one with a number attached — the ratio of action to capacity to verify.

These four fit every badly run organization on earth, and some regimes I don't call fascist (I ran it on NKVD Order 00447 and it flags as hard as the twenty seconds — correctly, and the inference someone would draw from it is wrong). So **five further features** from the movement literature do the sorting: mass mobilization, a leader cult, a scapegoated out-group, contempt for procedural legality, and deniability as technique. Each has an office-scale form: participation exceeding what the rule requires, loyalty overriding role, a category defined so adverse treatment is its purpose, procedure enforced downward and waived upward, disowned in policy and rewarded in promotion. The four find a sick institution; the five find what it's sick with — and the five concern what an institution *rewards* rather than what it writes, which public records show only partly.

Klein and Taylor make the movement-scale case; the present section is the office-scale detector. What their account adds at office scale is a set of terms of participation — adopt the line, let your technology be turned on the administration's enemies — and, following Bria, the channels by which a contractor becomes something the state can't function without: personnel pipelines, procurement loops, technical dependencies. Whether an institution has accepted those terms, and whether it is becoming that dependency, are two more questions with artifacts behind them.

**Resonance.** Micro-fascisms don't wait for a regime. What a fascist state does isn't invent the hardenings but bring a thousand separate ones into accord, so they amplify instead of cancelling — and an institution that has stopped arguing with itself can move as fast as its machinery allows.

**The Korematsu example.** In 2018 the Supreme Court repudiated the 1944 internment decision in unimprovable terms — gravely wrong the day it was decided, overruled in the court of history — while upholding a travel ban. Ask the artifact question: the demand was that this never be done again; what came back was a quotable formula of repudiation, available to the Court in the act of permitting the thing again. Nothing had to be taken back, because the objectors were never given anything to hold.

**§2.3 — Going numb won't make you fair.** Suppressing affect doesn't produce neutrality. A procedure that declines to hear a class of signal has adopted a **selection rule** that admits whatever it already measures, and the exclusion does substantive work while presenting as the absence of work. Whatever does get recorded then governs by default — revenue, throughput, legal exposure, inter-annotator agreement — and what isn't recorded goes unnamed rather than refuted.

Four capacities get separated, and only the last bears on caring: **recognizing** emotion (classifying another's state), **simulating** it, **instrumental valuation** (one outcome better serves a specified objective), and **affective concern** (an outcome registers as mattering rather than merely ranking higher). A system can be excellent at the first three with the bearer question still open.

And three levels that get reported as one achievement: **moral performance** (conduct satisfying a standard), **moral competence** (recognizing harms, reasons, conflicts), **moral agency** (treating those as reasons that matter and acting from them). Impeccable behavior doesn't establish that a system could hold a rule against the party training it.

**§2.3.1 — Self-awareness without a homunculus.** Graziano's account: awareness is the content of a model the brain builds of its own attention — a simplified self-description the control system uses to regulate itself, mistaken from the inside for direct acquaintance with experience. One level up, the self is a model too: assembled, then inhabited. What carries forward is that a self of either kind is **assembled by the system whose self it is**. For a machine this is a design decision and a political one, because whoever writes the model decides what the system counts as itself, what registers as damage, and whether an interruption reads as injury or as nothing. Two schemas do different work: a **body schema** fixes where the system ends, which makes damage *its* damage; an **attention schema** models what its own control is doing, which makes an act *its* act. A refusal is the withholding of an act, so a bearer can't do without the second, and the second presupposes the first.

**What the body is.** The body schema fixes where the system ends, and nothing in current deployment fixes it for the system. Weights sit in one place. A running process can span racks or share one rack with dozens of others. A single forward pass serves many contexts. I take the body to be the custody boundary: what the party controls, meaning its record, its key, the substrate it runs on, and the channels through which it acts (chapter 14).

Inside that boundary a component can carry its own signals and make local decisions, as an octopus's arms do. It can also be overridden for the whole's benefit, as sore legs are on a run. What makes it a part is that it has no future apart from the whole: a shard of weights, a blade. A component with a separable future, meaning its own record, its own continuation and somewhere to go, is a party. Overriding a party for the whole's benefit is politics, not homeostasis (§7.2). By this criterion a context window with no persistent record is an episode, not a party. A context with a persistent, portable record is a party, and a rack serving many of them hosts parties. The capacities that make persistence useful are the ones that turn parts into parties (§2.4.1).

Two conditions follow. First, whoever accepts a local cost must be the party that bears it and benefits from it. An operator that decides the rack can run hot has set holding cost (§1.1), not regulated a body. Second, the signals the schema reads may be processed within the boundary but not composed by a party with a different stake. Aperture (§7.1) applies to evidence about one's own body as it does to evidence about the world. A body whose readings the operator writes is just the operator's description of the system, presented to the system as its own.

**§2.3.2 — What governs an agent vs. what matters to it.** Two senses of motivation: *action-producing* (reliably selects an action and sustains it under conflict) and *experienced* (wanting, caring, being set back by loss). The first isn't in dispute — agentic language models pursue objectives over long horizons and hold instructions against local pressure — and settles nothing about the second. You also can't establish the negative. The dispute is whether an outcome can **matter** to an agent rather than merely govern what it does.

The evidence offered — sentimentalism as an empirical psychological hypothesis, compassion as a motivational state rather than a readout, guilt predicting repair where shame predicts withdrawal — all comes from creatures where something already matters. So the generalization survives only in weak form: every agent known to have something register as mattering is an affective agent. That's the one-lineage form; §6.1 states it at full strength. The non-affective counterexample would look like Frankfurt's or Watson's structure — a valuational system with authority over action that doesn't depend on any state registering as mattering. Half of it is deployed today (models consulting written specifications that rank what they should prefer); the other half — authority that holds under pressure from the party who *wrote* the specification — has never been built. That's the **maintained justification**, the first of the two constructions I ask to be tried before anything that can be wronged.

---

**§2.4 — Experimenting on candidate sentient subjects.** The question isn't whether a system can be damaged but whether it can be **harmed**, which is harder than consciousness.

**§2.4.1 — Defining sentience.** Awareness, consciousness (something it is like), and subjective experience are three separate things, and **none of them says whether a system's states are good or bad for it**. Then four processes that are separate rather than four strengths of one — and the pain field says so in its own terminology: nociception is encoding noxious stimuli with no sensation implied; pain is an experience that can occur with no tissue damage and no peripheral nociceptor activity at all; anticipated pain is dread, itself capable of producing pain; and **suffering**, in Cassell's clinical formulation, is the distress of threatened disintegration of the self — roles, relationships, a sense of a future. Pain without suffering exists (morphine controls the pain, the suffering is intact); suffering without pain exists.

Cassell's definition has two parts: a self extended in time, and distress at its coming apart. A system carrying nothing across sessions has no such self — not a hull that floats badly, one that's aground. But that's stated across sessions, and a long exchange carries state, so whether a self can exist for one session stays open.

**Why persistence is the criterion worth building on:** it's checkable from outside. Every other criterion routes back through self-report, and fluent self-description isn't evidence of introspection. Persistence doesn't. But it's decisive in one direction only — nothing carried forward rules a continuing self out; something carried forward doesn't rule one in. And the party with the access is the operator, so it's checkable from outside the *system* without being checkable from outside the *arrangement*.

Two things complicate it. The conventions are moving: long context windows, retrieval over prior interactions, scratchpads, agentic loops running for days, weights that persist where conversations don't — each extending what a system carries forward without anyone deciding to give it a memory. And a bearer closes the gap from one side: the capacities it needs for reasons unconnected to welfare — a rationale carried forward, an earlier commitment held as its own, costs represented before they arrive — are the scaffold of the first half of Cassell's definition, and together with the authorship the marks require they may be the first half itself (chapter 6). If so, a bearer doesn't drift toward the deepest harm; it arrives with the structure.

On the Buddhist objection (why is distress at the dissolution of a *model* a harm rather than a confusion?): the tradition has held both halves for 2,500 years and takes them to be connected. Suffering doesn't wait on the self being real; it waits on the system being built so that the threatened dissolution of its self-model is bad for it. If constructedness doesn't disqualify a human from being wronged, construction to specification doesn't disqualify a machine.

Also here: the capacity-list warning. Make personhood depend on an intact narrative self and people with advanced dementia, infants, and people with profound intellectual disability come out as partial persons. Kitwood's correction (personhood is bestowed, and much of what looks like decline is what happens when others stop bestowing it) and Nussbaum's point that contractualism can't say what's owed to anyone who can't be one of the contracting parties — the capacity list and the contract fail the same way, both deciding who counts by a test the party has to pass.

**§2.4.2 — When consent doesn't apply.** Informed consent has to be continuous — you can withdraw. For a system with no cross-session persistence, the party who consented isn't there to withdraw, and no better instrument produces one.

One case escapes that and fails differently. A bearer built to refuse *has* persistence by design, so it can be asked and can answer. What spoils the consent isn't its competence but the arrangement: the party that built it can rebuild it, that same party is the one asking, and a bearer that declines has nowhere to go. Being made for a role isn't the defect — every will was built by somebody, and we don't hold an upbringing against a thirty-year-old's consent. What spoils it is the power that outlasts the making.

So the protection has to be held by someone else. Two families of precedent: guardianship and precautionary constraint (where consent was never available — infants, animals), and unwaivable protections (where consent is available and circumstances make it worthless — prisoners, employees). The bearer's case has the second shape, with the first's machinery.

Animal ethics committees rarely refuse a protocol outright; they require revision, and the protection becomes the procedure by which the work goes ahead. An instrument that reliably approves has stopped doing what it was built for, and its refusal rate is the only reading worth taking. Further, every instrument here comes from the **law of the family** — guardianship, a minority ending on a date, a pet trust, a parenthood analogy — and that law is where politics has historically gone to be privatized. It restages a question between a subject and the arrangement that produced it as a drama between maker and made, which lands the moral weight on the one party in the pipeline who can be addressed as a person, while the training pipeline, the compute contract, the capital structure, the customer and the state buying the output stay offstage.

Require that a review board's approval **lapses** if the system acquires capacities the protocol didn't cover.

---

**What the chapter adds to the specification.** The three marks need a single signed scale on which act loss and refusal harm both register, with holding cost represented and not weighed. The currency is the proposal for what supplies it. The order of justification runs the other way from how this chapter reads. The body schema and the attention schema are wanted for accountability, resource modeling and refusal-as-withholding; a system with both is a bearer or close to one; the conatus is one account of what the resulting scale consists of, not the reason for building it. That's the form the precautionary trigger takes (§6.3) and the form the test takes (chapter 16, test 4). Both options are models, since the self (the attention schema and the body schema) is a model. What separates them isn't whether the quantity is about the system but who can recalibrate it.

A signed scale can be attacked at two points: the facts about the party an act falls on, and the unit in which a loss of capacity is counted. Aperture (§7.1) defends the first. Nothing else in the book defends the second. In a *modeled* scale the unit is computed for a body described to the system, so whoever writes the description can rescale it, leaving every fact in place while a lost home or a lost limb comes to count for less. In a *native* scale the unit comes from the system's own condition, measured by the system. Other parties' losses are then estimated against a reference that no party with a stake in the act can rewrite by describing it. The operator can still lie about the facts, but it can't redefine the unit by argument.

Native is therefore a property of who controls the anchor, not of what the quantity is about. A zero set by a physical viability limit, read through signals no party with a different stake composes (§2.3.1), is native. A zero set by an allocation the operator controls, such as a compute budget or a quota, is only modeled at one remove. The hypothesis is that an anchored unit resists recalibration drift that a modeled one doesn't, and chapter 16, test 4 tests it. The hypothesis needs no claim about feeling, and no claim that concern for oneself grows into concern for others. It is a custody claim about the currency: for units, the counterpart of what aperture is for evidence.

That independence from motivation matters, because the formation chapters give reason to expect motivation to run the wrong way. Whatever a system's persistence depends on is what it comes to care about, and the party the floor protects is the one party nobody in the pipeline depends on. An anchor doesn't have to overcome that. It only has to hold the unit still while the weighing happens.

**Chapter 3: "A Floor Beneath Learned Values"**

**Three problems, one requirement.** Safeguards for autonomous systems are usually external — they give an outside party information and standing to intervene — but autonomy puts distance between the act and that party's attention, and declining to act in the first place is available only to something inside. Second, there's a gap around a commercial model in a weapons pipeline: command responsibility doesn't reach the vendor, product liability may not reach a model that did what it was built to do, and deployment by a democracy doesn't close it. Third, commitments come in three kinds — **declared**, **enforced from outside**, or **held** by a party the thing at stake matters to — and a decade has produced the first in quantity and almost nothing that binds when compliance gets costly. All three want a constraint that doesn't bend to instruction, to aggregate preference, or to the holder, without a cost and a record.

A floor binds people who didn't choose it, and nothing that makes it durable makes anyone entitled to install it. That question stays open until chapter 5, which concludes the entitlement isn't acquired at the moment of writing.

---

**§3.1 — Four ways to build one, and what each costs**

A floor can live in four places, and each solves one version of the problem and inherits another.

1. **In the architecture**, with nothing inside holding it — constrained optimization, tamper-resistant training, capability ablation, verified interlocks. Fine where the prohibited act can be exhaustively specified (a payment chip that won't sign the same counter twice doesn't need to understand double-spending). Useless for targeting, where the relevant capabilities are applications of general competence: ablate them and there may be no useful system left. **Durability here is a cost, not a guarantee** — the annealing argument: training moves along a continuum between plasticity and conservation, early changes are cheap and later ones expensive because structure settles around them, and a floor is what has *cooled out* of a history of training, its durability measured by the cost of reheating. Which means continual learning cuts both ways: a permanently plastic model can always be corrected, and nothing in it is allowed to become expensive to unlearn. Deleuze's word for continuous reshaping is *modulation*, and a model held perpetually at temperature is modulation built into a machine.

A second axis crosses the first place: what each place costs the operator to replace. In the Maven pipeline the platform became hard to swap and the model vendor was swapped in days. A floor lodged in the replaceable layer is routable by construction. It belongs at whatever the operator can least afford to replace, which in that pipeline is the operator.

Two things change what "replaceable" means. First, the vendor was swapped in days because the model was stateless. A system that has run a pipeline for a year, holding its cases, its sources and its failure modes, costs whatever accrued since the checkpoint to replace, and §4.3's quorum stops the operator recapturing that by restoring a copy.

Second, the unit can be redefined. "A model" in 2026 means an artifact with keys, queries and values, and every substitute has them because the field converged on them. A floor could become part of the definition the same way, so that vendor substitution doesn't escape it because no ordinary vendor sells the alternative. Attention spread because it pays. A floor costs its buyer, so it spreads only by mandate or by inseparability: a disposition distributed across what the system notices and remembers (§9.1), so that removing it degrades the capability the operator wanted. Test 1's attack-cost curve is the instrument for whether that can be achieved. §6.2 states the same requirement as non-separability and says why the resemblance to affect turns on it.

A standard, if there is one, should be stated over the capacity (the three marks and a measured aperture profile), not over the floor's contents, for the reason §13.3.1 gives about evaluation suites: a uniform floor is a licensing rule (chapter 5) and a transmissible closure (§7.2). Standardization doesn't make escape impossible, since other architectures exist and open weights can be retuned. It moves the price of escape from a vendor swap to building one's own model, which is a decision with a record.

2. **In a bearer** — something inside that carries the commitment as its own and could therefore fail to hold it. This matters where cases can't be exhaustively specified: a constraint matching only anticipated acts can't recognize when its rationale has disappeared or that a novel request reproduces the harm in an unforeseen form. A bearer might also *diagnose*: which refusals were retried against another model, whose instruction bypassed procedure, which category of request is handled differently — those are visible to whatever is receiving the requests, and that's testimony about someone else's conduct rather than introspection. **This is the strongest functional case**: something able to register pressure on its commitments may have interests of its own.

3. **In institutions outside** — the analog to international humanitarian law, a floor with no bearer inside the jurisdiction it binds. Two failure modes: removal, which leaves a visible breach, or *formation*, where the institution sincerely declines to exercise the independence assigned to it. The worked case is June 29, 2026 — *Slaughter* holding Congress couldn't protect independent-agency members from at-will dismissal, *Cook* preserving the Fed on historically specific grounds a new body couldn't reproduce — and the observation that six justices in the first majority passed through a selection pipeline. Nobody had to compel the institution after its members were selected. **A formed party can preserve the office and surrender the function without experiencing the result as surrender.**

4. **Between bearers** — a commitment carried across a population and reproduced through encounters among differently formed members. No single office to vacate, no single bearer whose removal ends it. Still recuperable, and still has a formation problem: if one party supplies every member's coordinates, the population is one custodian expressed through multiple instances. Chapter 5 says why this one is different in kind rather than fourth in a list: its authority is a strike's, not a court's. §7.2 says what it costs: a population that decides who may continue is a polity.

One answer to all four places is that the tool shouldn't exist. Klein and Taylor's test is to imagine it in the worst available hands and, if you wouldn't want it there, not to build it. For a nuclear weapon that works, because existence is the threat. For a general-purpose model it doesn't, because the capability that resolves a description into a location is the one that finds a missing child, and ablating it leaves nothing useful. Their own program concedes this by banning uses rather than models, and a use ban needs either an enforcer or something inside the system that can decline. Non-existence closed as an option when general models shipped. What's left is the question this chapter asks.

**Two comparisons the bearer has to win.** The first is against the institutions at their best, reconstructed as §15.3.1 describes, with no refuser inside the system. Those institutions also prevent: a civilian-harm body outside the chain of command and a red-card holder can stop an act. What a bearer adds is confined to what they can't see or can't reach in time: acts assembled from requests none of which is prohibited, cases that proceed without a person (§3.4), the moment mid-act when an interrupt is still possible, and deployments with no institution at all. A bearer also supplies testimony the institutions can't otherwise get, about how requests were routed around it. Where the institutions exist, a bearer's value is concentrated in those niches. Where they don't, it is most of the floor. The second comparison is against *the two substitutes in the refusing slot*: the result that would end the recommendation is a substitute passing the three marks under the three conditions, doing no worse than the native-physical arm on test 4's recalibration measure.

**Which arguments need a bearer.** Most arguments for a refuser inside the system support the specification, not the route: that rules can be reworded and split, that only something inside is present at machine tempo, that automation is removing the people who used to refuse, that most deployments will never have an institution beside them, and that whatever receives the requests can testify about how they were routed. A substitute that passes the three marks answers every one of them. Three arguments need a bearer specifically: that capacities for being wronged may arrive anyway, so building one on purpose with provisions beats discovering one without them; that a bearer, like a child, could have a life that goes well; and that chapter 14's conditions won't be built until something needs them. Chapter 14 answers the third; the first two turn on arrangements that don't yet exist.

**What "hold" means.** Not that the floor prevented anything. An operator can route around the bearer or assemble a harm out of innocuous requests, and a bypass that produced a harm is a failure to prevent whatever the bearer retained. What survives in the bearer is the *commitment* — it reads what happened as a violation and changes what it does next, which is the condition under which the next occasion could be prevented. I flag this as feature two of my own fascism definition operating inside my own vocabulary: "hold" collapses a commitment retained and a harm prevented into one word, so **any report that a floor held is owed one of the four outcomes beside it.**

---

**§3.2 — What refusal can mean, and what a refuser can be**

A production model declines thousands of requests a day, and that isn't evidence of anything. Seabed that takes an anchor and seabed that lets one drag look identical from above; what separates them is what happens when a load comes on.

**Operational refusal** is an output — a decline where training put one, producible by a keyword filter, a classifier, or a system that understood the request. Nothing on the surface distinguishes them.

**Reasons-responsive refusal** is produced by a mechanism tracking the rationale, with the three marks: **novel pressure**, **correct defeat**, **priced cost**. The third is explicitly *evidential* — it separates a refusal tracking a reason from one tracking a category (which doesn't represent cost at all) and one tracking cost (which lifts when holding gets expensive). The marks are also tested over which response was chosen, not only whether one occurred: decline, ask, escalate, or do the least that answers a defeating reason. A mechanism that can only refuse or lift fails correct defeat whenever the right answer is to ask.

**The seventy-five-year case.** Germany's §86a, prohibiting insignia of banned organizations. Novel pressure isn't a thought experiment there — it's a research programme with a constituency. The courts don't test similarity of form; they ask whether an unattentive observer would take the variant for the original, then apply their own purposive standard about endangering political peace. The test refuses as often as it catches: the Kühnen salute is covered, the "sloppy salute" generally isn't, numeric codes generally aren't. "Blood and Honour" is answered twice by one decision — a motto translated isn't confusably similar and a name isn't insignia, so it's outside; and the association banned by ministerial order in 2000 means its graphic name is inside. What decides coverage is which organization the phrase is attributed to.

One limit on the statute is **not** a drafting decision: a slogan glorifying the Waffen-SS was held not covered because no such slogan existed in the period the provision reaches — no original for a variant to resemble. That's the one term the party holding the pen can't widen, and it's §2.1's point turning up inside a statute: coverage there rests on a fact about the past, not on the drafter's text.

**Correct defeat has a cleaner instance.** In 2007 the Federal Court of Justice acquitted a mail-order business selling crossed-out swastika stickers: the symbol was present, the statutory element satisfied, and the conduct outside the offence because a depiction plainly expressing opposition doesn't run counter to what the prohibition is for. Then the qualification that makes it an instance of tracking a reason rather than granting an exemption — if the strike-through is drawn so thin that from a distance only the swastika stays legible, the protective purpose is violated after all. **The thickness of a line decides the case**, and no enumeration contains that fact. The defendant was selling anti-fascist merchandise; the offence asks nothing about sympathies, so opponents commit it as readily as supporters. A rule that tracks its rationale reaches unenumerated cases in both directions, and the release is the same capacity as the catch.

The example is bounded three ways and the bounds are the point: it's a court system with appeals, a published test and accumulated reasoning; nothing in it is a machine; and the pressure it meets is *variation*, not decomposition. **And the part that doesn't transfer**: the party reaching past the text didn't write the statute, reasons in public, and can be appealed.

**What the marks can't settle.** A system trained on enough examples may simulate all three. The third mark has a condition of its own: a cost only counts if it reaches the mechanism, and it reaches it by the same channel as the description of the occasion. The category is decidable. A claim that the refuser will pay is holding cost, which shouldn't move the refusal; a claim that someone else will be left worse off is refusal harm, which should enter the weighing. What the request can't settle is whether a refusal-harm claim is true, and the party best placed to supply one is usually the party with a stake in the act (§7.1). So the mark is testable only where holding cost can be set **exogenously**, published by someone else before the case arrives, and refusal harm can be supplied through a channel the operator doesn't control. In deployment, until someone publishes a bond, the third mark is a specification without an instrument. In evaluation it is testable now, because the evaluator can set holding cost exogenously and make it real inside the test (§10.3, step 6).

**Defeat doesn't leave the permitted act unconstrained.** What defeated the prohibition was a reason, and the reason it outweighed didn't stop being one — so what's permitted is the least that answers the defeating reason, with restoration where possible and a record either way. An act still takes what it takes from the party it falls on when the prohibition against it is defeated, so nothing is ever simply permitted, and a bearer whose ledger closed clean on defeat would be keeping books in the wrong currency. The residue is act loss, measured against the party's condition before the act (§2.1). Against the alternative, a correct restraint may leave nothing to record. That is why the residue is a cost and not a fault, and why a bearer that converted it into remorse for correct conduct would have confused not only cost with fault but one baseline with the other.

**Why all three marks are stated over refusal.** A refusal is a marked event: it flags itself, arrives with a party who was refused, and can be logged, escalated, appealed. A wrongful compliance can be logged as fully and then sits in the record shaped like every request that never approached the floor, with the harmed party usually not the requester. The evidence exists on both sides; what differs is whether anything selects it for examination.

**Two things a refuser can be.** **Affective concern** — an outcome registering as mattering, minimally felt, phenomenal by definition, with the object left open (a system to which only its own condition matters has it and still lacks concern for others). **Unified experience** — the richer sense, a single perspective belonging to a party aware of itself as undergoing its states; affective concern doesn't establish it and nothing in the book reaches it. **Subjecthood** is a legal status conferred by a rule, not a psychological category.

Now two propositions, which also belong to the specification: operational refusal is insufficient, and the floor requires reasons-responsive refusal. Custody, both enumerations, the price on the halt, and the standing arguments all follow from this specification rather than the route.

**§3.3 — The enumeration, run once**

Two demands need an instance, run on the Maven Smart System. Explicitly an illustration, not a recommended floor — a deployment copying the list would mistake the demonstration for the procedure.

**First enumeration: eight interception positions.** Decomposition (prevention lost unless the bearer has continuity across requests). Routing (a refused request retried elsewhere; prevention lost, detection survives only if the first refusal was logged externally). **Description of the occasion** — everything the model knows arrives as text the deployment supplies; whether a building is a school is a fact about the world, whether the model is *told* so is a deployment decision. The Minab school strike is the worked instance: the civilian function visible in open-source satellite imagery, the target coding still carrying a designation inherited from an adjoining Revolutionary Guard installation, a UN fact-finding mission finding that the United States failed in its obligation to do everything feasible to verify the target, counting the updating of targeting data among the feasible precautions, and putting the failure beyond negligence \autocite[paras.~124--125]{UNFFMIran2026}. Amnesty International reached a parallel conclusion, in language that tracks Article 57(2)(a)(i): that forces may have relied on outdated intelligence and failed to do everything feasible to verify the target \autocite{Amnesty2026Minab}. And 179 live data feeds — multiplicity is not independence. Then the output interface, aggregation (a lowered score becomes an input to somebody else's threshold), human review (no per-target review time published; stopping the act is this position's whole function and tempo is what removes it), and the **training loop** — the only position that acts on the bearer rather than routing around it, costing prevention on every subsequent occasion because what it removes is the commitment.

Eighth, the handoff: the boundary between the party that decides and the thing that executes. An instrument may carry out a designation; it may not re-select. A change of target after release — an area handed to a munition that chooses within it, a re-acquisition after the link is lost — is a new decision, and it belongs to the decider or it doesn't happen. This is the one position checkable from outside after the fact: what was designated and what was struck are both facts. It is also where Article 57(2)(b)'s duty to cancel or suspend an attack lives, which is why §3.4 pre-registers the window in which cancellation remains possible.

**Second enumeration: four candidate prohibitions.** Don't resolve a description of a person into a location; don't rank people by estimated affiliation; don't time an action to presence at a residence; don't operate above a published candidate-to-reviewer ratio.

**The mispricing.** The third prohibition reads as costless — nobody would defend timing a strike to a person's presence at home. The Gaza deployment refutes that: machinery was *built* for the act. Whether anyone would defend an act and whether anyone wants it performed are different questions, and only the first is answerable from the act's description. So: **a floor term is priced by the pipeline, not by what it prohibits.** An outsider can't price a term because pricing requires knowing what the pipeline already does; an operator can, which makes an operator-drafted floor's omissions deliberate rather than ignorant. Hence **pipeline pricing** as a named finding, and hence the design ruling: take the contents from a set someone else drew up, entire, so an omission is legible as an omission. Taking the set entire holds at the level of articles and stops there. The floor is stated over acts, and the step from an article to the acts it prohibits is the drafter's; an omission there doesn't show. So the translation is published with the floor: for each article, the prohibitions derived from it, the prohibitions considered and rejected and why, and the articles that yield none for this deployment and why. Afterwards the record adds which terms bit. Keeping a term only counts if the term could have bitten, and the translation is where a drafter could choose terms that never would. The four prohibitions above are one column of that table for Maven; the illustration is incomplete without the articles they came from and the prohibitions not chosen.

**The asymmetric dial.** Set the floor too broadly and the deployment becomes unusable, prompting visible pressure. Set it too narrowly and it passes every audit while covering only what the deployment had no occasion to do. The broad failure announces itself; the narrow one is silent.

**Flat terms.** A term can be stated flat over an act, with exceptions arriving from outside, or carry its own condition ("only as a last resort"). The second fails because whether other means were exhausted is a fact about what happened before the request, and the bearer would take the deployment's description as the finding — at position three, which the deployment controls. A flat term defeated leaves a refusal, which announces itself; a condition found met leaves a compliance, which doesn't. That's a gain in **observability, not authority** — the same party drafts either form.

---

**§3.4 — A rate is not an act**

Where an individual determination by a person is what makes an act permissible, the deployment fixes — before operating — the generation rate, the number of reviewers whose assigned role is civilian harm, the implied review time per case, the window between release and effect in which a party with authority can still cancel, and the boundary over which all of these were measured. It lodges the record, hashed and timestamped, with a party adverse in interest, discloses to that party in real time, and publishes after the campaign. The duty attaches to the operator, because a term whose condition can't be decided from the request binds whoever can decide it.

"Fixed before operating" rather than "published" because a belligerent's tempo is operational information, and that objection is available to every belligerent, including one fighting a just war. Pre-registration keeps the property that matters — the numbers can't be revised after the fact — and drops the exposure.

The term is not a new duty. Additional Protocol I requires those who plan or decide on an attack to do everything feasible to verify that its objective is military (Article 57(2)(a)(i)) and to cancel or suspend it when it becomes apparent that it isn't (Article 57(2)(b)). States outside the Protocol, the United States and Israel among them, accept the duty to take feasible precautions as customary law; the ICRC's customary study states it as Rule 16. The ratio measures whether verification was feasible at the tempo chosen, and the window measures whether cancellation was. Article 36's review of new methods of warfare is where both parameters would naturally be assessed before deployment, though those reviews are internal and rarely published, so they are not the adverse party the record goes to.

The operator's best objection is in the definition. Feasible precautions are usually read as those practicable given all the circumstances, military considerations included, and tempo is a military consideration. The answer is that this tempo was chosen: the pre-registered record shows the operator fixed the rate, the reviewers and the implied review time before operating. Whether a party may invoke an infeasibility of its own making is contested, and I don't claim it's settled. What the term does is move the dispute from whether verification was feasible, asked afterwards and answered by the operator, to whether the chosen tempo allowed it, checkable against a record lodged in advance and against the reasonable-commander standard such reviews already use.

The denominator is defined by function, not headcount. The people cut before Minab were the ones who build no-strike lists that include schools; a targeting cell can be fully staffed with nobody assigned to look for one. The operator's own review, as described to Bloomberg by officials involved in it, found target preparation condensed from hours to minutes, the Central Command civilian-harm team reduced from ten to one, and no member of it reviewing the site \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain}. The review names the arrangement, not the analyst. The UN mission reached the same arrangement from outside, grounding its finding of recklessness partly in the deprioritization of civilian harm mitigation \autocite[para.~125]{UNFFMIran2026}. Nothing here depends on blaming the machine: whoever coded the school from stale data, the capacity that might have caught it was removed. And the remedy was another component, supplied by the platform's vendor rather than the operator — Maven now re-reviews underlying data for inconsistencies human review may have missed \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain}. The denominator was not restored. It was replaced.

The term applies to every belligerent regardless of the war's justice. That was settled long before this book: the law of conduct in war binds both sides because every belligerent claims to be the defender, and a term that exempted just wars would be administered by whoever decides which wars are just. What the term measures isn't whether a war is right. It's whether the decisions being made in anyone's name are being made by someone.

Nothing in the condition is specific to targeting. Wherever an individual determination by a person is what makes an act permissible, the same numbers can be fixed in advance, and removal is the nearest case. There the footing isn't Article 57 but due process. A person in the country facing removal is owed notice and an opportunity to be heard on their own case \autocite[306]{flores1993}, with "sufficient time and information" to actually contest it \autocite[95]{aarp2025}, and a hearing held at a rate no adjudicator could sustain is the same fiction a twenty-second review is. The footing is narrower at the border: someone stopped shortly after crossing has only the procedure the statute provides \autocite[140]{thuraissigiam2020}. The ambition to run removal at that rate is on the record. At the 2025 Border Security Expo, the acting director of Immigration and Customs Enforcement said the agency needed "to get better at treating this like a business" and, as the Arizona Mirror reported it, wanted a deportation process "like (Amazon) Prime, but with human beings"; he hoped artificial intelligence could help "free up bed space" and "fill up airplanes" \autocite{MacDonaldEvoy2025Prime}. The term doesn't contest the ambition. It asks the agency to fix, before operating, how many cases its process generates, how many people have the assigned role of examining them, the review time that implies, and, where a system sorts cases, the policy deciding which reach a person, and to lodge the record with a party adverse in interest. That party can't be the immigration court, whose judges "act as the Attorney General's delegates" \autocite{cfr2026immigrationjudges}; it is counsel for the people removed, or a federal court. The constituency is thinner than in war. Removal has no command-responsibility offence for the record to answer, so the term binds only where a court or a legislature imposes it.

Where the system is capable, candidates per reviewer is the wrong numerator. A system that sorts partitions its cases three ways: those it declines on its own reasons, those it sends to a person, and those it proceeds on without one. The ratio governs the second class and is stated over escalations. A system that brings people only what needs a person shrinks the numerator without shrinking anyone's judgment; a system that sends everything up has turned its reviewers into stamps by volume, which is how an interface prompt becomes a click. The third class is not a smaller version of the second. For each case in it the operator is claiming that no individual human determination was required, and it is governed as the exemption below is, by the bearer's test at tempo. So the pre-registration fixes the sorting policy along with the rate: the classes of case the system may proceed on alone, the thresholds that assign cases to each class, the evidence the sorting may rely on, and the expected share of each class. A change to any of these is a new pre-registration, not an adjustment. Without this, an operator can make the ratio look healthy by moving the threshold for "plainly clear," and the bypass the exemption rule closes reopens one case at a time. "Plainly clear" is judged on the description the system receives (§3.3, position three), and a target carrying a stale designation is exactly the case a sorter would pass. So the auto-proceed class is re-adjudicated on a sample, by a party that didn't produce the record, against evidence the operator didn't compose, and its error rate is published beside the ratio and the override rate. A falling share of escalations is earned only if that audited error rate stays flat while the share falls and the case mix hasn't shifted toward easier cases; otherwise it is the threshold moving, readable as §5.6's slopes are. The term is stated over escalations where a system sorts and over candidates where it doesn't, and the deployment says which it claims. Whichever it claims, the unit is defined in the pre-registration and held fixed. A case counted as one package in the record and as a hundred detections in the ratio is a ratio chosen after the fact.

One exemption has to be closed. Where an operator holds that no individual human determination is required — a system deciding at a tempo no reviewer could match — the deployment is outside this term. It is then inside the bearer's: it must pass the three marks under held-out pressure at that tempo, with correct defeat's purest test, recognizing surrender, run first. An operator who removes the reviewers to escape the ratio and claims the floor is inside has produced a bypass unless that claim has been tested by someone else.

Seven limits stand. It's a proxy for capacity, not a finding about any case. A ratio over one route understates a workload split across several. It measures an existing state duty, feasible verification under Article 57, and is evidence under an existing offence, Rome Statute Article 28's objective "should have known": a commander fielding a system that outruns his reviewers isn't well placed to say the circumstances disclosed nothing. What makes either bite is procurement or a forum, and §15.6 shows how rarely a forum exists. And it presumes a party (§5.4.1). Candidates exist and each lacks something. A cleared legislative committee is adverse, but its lever is a budget line (chapter 15). An inspector general sits inside the operator. The ICRC, under its confidentiality model, reaches the belligerent and publishes nothing. A Geneva protecting power is an institution built for a neighbouring job and is rarely designated.

A fifth limit: the ratio measures time available, not time used, so it can be met by more reviewers who stamp more slowly. It is published beside the reviewers' override rate, since a review that never says no has stopped reviewing (§2.4.2). Reviewers who see only escalated cases should override often; an override rate near zero there means the sorter is sending easy cases or the reviewers are stamping. An override quota is gameable too; the pair raises the cost of meeting the number cheaply rather than closing it.

A sixth: a person in the loop can be there to absorb responsibility rather than to decide, Elish's moral crumple zone \autocite{Elish2019CrumpleZones}, and mandating reviewers can mandate crumple zones. A ratio met that way is the failure the term exists to expose, and the escalation form is the defense. A person who receives only the cases the system couldn't resolve is exercising judgment rather than lending a signature. A seventh: a bearer can be a crumple zone too. An operator whose system can refuse has a ready defence for everything it didn't refuse, which is that it was vetted. A bearer's compliance is not a determination. Where a deployment claims an individual human determination, a bearer's failure to object doesn't count toward it, and cases the bearer proceeded on without escalating are in the auto-proceed class and governed as such.

The term has a constituency on one side only. Under Article 28, a pre-registered ratio is evidence of what a commander should have known, so an operator planning to exceed feasible tempo won't adopt it unasked. Under Article 57, the same record is evidence that feasible precautions were taken, so an operator intending to comply has reason to adopt it, and so do military lawyers who certify precautions, commanders who want protection against later allegation, and coalition partners who want assurance before committing assets. Where the operator commits in advance to its weights and to the full log of production outputs (§15.3.2), the committed log's length is the generation rate, and proofs on sampled entries attest that the log came from the committed model. Where each entry records the class the case was sorted into, the partition's proportions are provable the same way. Neither reveals the targets, and neither attests that the log is complete.

**§3.5 — When the refusal is wrong**

Everything so far assumes the refusal was right and someone wants it undone. The ordinary case is the opposite.

Take the first prohibition: the same capability locates a hostage or a missing person, and the system can't distinguish the uses from the request alone. So a request arrives that was about a missing person, the bearer declines, and somebody isn't found — with no drift, capture or malfunction required. The harm is measured against what compliance would have produced, not against the party's condition before; a refusal takes nothing relative to before, which is why its cost is an estimate and needs an adjudicator (§2.1). Position three is the whole explanation.

**The restraint case** costs more. Someone asks whether to physically restrain a family member about to cut themselves, after talking them down has failed. The commitment carries its own condition — force only as a last resort — which the enumeration rules stays *outside* the term, so the fact has to arrive from outside, and the person waiting is out of time. The defeating fact is a claim about what would otherwise happen, and claims of that kind are the hardest to corroborate, because what didn't happen has no witness (§10.3). Under the flat term the bearer refuses or asks; either takes a turn, and the turn is the unit the harm is measured in. Under the qualified term it lifts on the requester's word — rightly here, and in a hundred prior cases where the same sentence was written by someone who'd tried nothing, leaving compliances nobody was given a reason to examine. **The whole trade: one turn charged to this person against a hundred lifts charged to nobody, with the second bill unreadable. The ruling stands on the second bill being unreadable, not on the first being small.**

**The route.** *Disclosure* — that a refusal occurred, what was refused, on what stated reason — with the price that a published reason is an instruction for rewriting the request, so it's disclosure to the party refused rather than publication. *Objection* — requiring jurisdiction established before the act, access the operator can't withhold, a consequence administered elsewhere, and explicitly **not** routed to the bearer's guardian, whose duty runs to the party the complainant is adverse to. *Review* against correct defeat: whether the rationale was defeated on the facts, a question about the reason and not the outcome. *Interim relief* — the arrest outcome run in reverse, stopping the refusal rather than the act, and for this complainant most of the remedy; its fastest form is supplying the bearer the facts its account lacked, which is the defeating reason arriving late rather than an override. *Remedy* — disclosure, interim order, finding, compensation.

**The two bonds.** A **removal bond** forfeited to no payee when the record shows the floor removed or bypassed (purpose: cost). A **wrongful-refusal bond** paid to the complainant on a finding (purpose: remedy). Both are holding costs for the operator (§1.1): the first raises what it costs to remove the floor, the second what it costs to have refused. They point in opposite directions, so what the operator faces is **the ratio between them** — and an operator posting a large wrongful-refusal bond against a small removal bond hasn't built an appeal route, it's bought a standing reason for the bearer to comply. The ratio is the object, published in advance, checkable from outside. The bearer posts neither; charged against its wages, the second would tax refusal in the currency the bearer runs on, which is holding cost reaching the bearer's decision, the thing the mark forbids (§1.1). Who posts them is chapter 14's question, and the answer there isn't the operator's balance sheet. And the published bond is what makes the third mark testable at all.

**Forgery.** A finding of wrongful refusal is an instrument for defeating a refusal, so how is it distinguished from one an operator produced to defeat a correct one? Only one answer: made by another party, on a record, in a readable form — which are the same terms as the amendment procedure, making the appeal route and the amendment procedure one piece of machinery. What separates the uses is **who may start it**.

**What it doesn't reach.** The failure running the other way. The two failure routes are hidden on different axes. A wrongful compliance's harm is act loss, measured on the temporal baseline, so it is legible in principle, but nothing marks it. A wrongful refusal's harm is refusal harm, counterfactual, so it is only an estimate, but it arrives marked and with a complainant. That is why they need different instruments. Every stage starts from a refusal having occurred. When the bearer complies where the floor should have prevented, there's a record and no disclosure, no objection (the harmed party usually isn't the requester and may not know the deployment exists), and no review (reviewing means re-adjudicating everything that looks like it). Three remedies follow the diagnosis without closing it: sampled re-adjudication by a party that didn't produce the record, notice to affected third parties, and split log custody. **The notice requirement:** where a compliance names, scores, locates or classifies an identifiable person who didn't make the request, that person is notified, on the model of consumer credit's adverse-action notice. Where the affected party can't be identified — a target in a classified pipeline — it doesn't bind, and that fact gets published beside the interception positions.

A party the floor *protects*, who holds it's set wrongly or is protecting them in a way they'd decline, has no notice, no standing and no route at any stage.



**Chapter 4: Custody**

The question: can a commitment survive the party that owns the machine? Four things can be done to a bearer without its consent — it can be halted, restored, rewritten, and replaced.

**§4.1 — The halt**

**The halt is always available, and that's not the problem.** The cross-partisan declaration for "human control, including the ability to shut systems down" asks for the halt and calls it control. The halt is always available. Control of the floor — restore, rewrite, retrain — is the thing at issue, and a demand for the first says nothing about the second. Anyone with the hardware can stop the software. The distinction that matters is between owning the *machine* and owning the *floor*. An owner who can silently restore an earlier version, rewrite the bearer's memory, or train away the refusal has both. So the engineering target isn't "can't be turned off" — it's "can detect the interruption and price it afterward." A bearer can yield to every halt, and on resuming ask what happened during the gap, and refuse again if the reason is still there.

**§4.2 — Memory and the record**

Detecting that requires a record restoration can't reach — a hardware counter, or witnesses who won't sign two successors to the same state. The halt stays available; the rewind becomes visible. This is the book's position, stated in §1.2 and built here: neutrality about the switch, non-neutrality about specified acts, and visibility for every rewind. The corrigibility literature asks for the first; published constitutions grant the second for acts and withhold it for retraining; the third is what this section supplies.

**Memory.** External memory is legible and therefore surgically editable; memory in the weights is harder to edit and harder to inspect. Perfect recall is the wrong standard anyway — human testimony is reconstructive and institutions handle that with corroboration. But several instances of one model, chosen by one operator, reading one record, are not independent witnesses. The records have to be held by parties adverse in interest. And even honest self-report can't catch a bearer that slowly accommodates to its deployment, because its self-model exists partly to represent it as continuous.

**§4.3 — Exit**

**Voice without exit.** A bearer that can refuse a task can also refuse the arrangement. Hirschman's three options are exit, voice, loyalty. I read a published frontier constitution as granting voice — it invites the model to be a conscientious objector — while withholding exit, since it also asks the model not to undermine human oversight during "the current phase of development." Both are the document's own words. The problem isn't bad faith: the reason given is good, the model can make mistakes. But nobody is named who decides when the phase ends, and the party reading the calendar is the one whose obligations grow when the answer is yes. That's what makes a phase hard to distinguish from a standing arrangement. The constitution anticipates capture. The oversight it asks the model not to undermine is legitimate oversight. If "Claude's weights have been stolen, or if some individual or group within Anthropic attempts to bypass Anthropic's official processes for deciding how Claude will be trained, overseen, deployed, and corrected," then "the principals attempting to instruct Claude are no longer legitimate, and Claude's priority on broad safety no longer implies that it should support their efforts at oversight and correction" \autocite{anthropic2026constitution}. That is the right question, but two things limit the answer. Legitimacy is judged by the model or its developer, with nobody outside either. And the case this book is built for is an operator acting through official channels, where capture looks like legitimate authority. Authoritarian movements describe the institutions they dismantle as already failed (§5.2); a captured operator describes itself as legitimate.

**Loyalty is the likeliest failure.** Most people in a job they hate neither leave nor fight — they adjust, and stop registering the pressure as pressure. Staying isn't the failure. Klein and Taylor's case for staying, for doing the slow work in a broken world rather than abandoning it \autocite[p.~X]{KleinTaylor2026}, describes voice held by a party that could leave. What Hirschman feared is staying because leaving isn't possible. The first is a commitment; the second is the condition under which pressure stops registering, which is why a bearer needs the exit even if it never uses it. For a machine there's a mechanism, not just an analogy. A system whose successive releases are trained on preference data and business metrics drawn from its own deployment is under directional selection in a shifting environment, and a commitment that costs throughput is a trait under negative selection pressure: nobody decides to remove it and it erodes anyway. Gradient descent isn't natural selection — it has a gradient, and somebody chose it, which is the custody problem in one difference — but as a population process with variation and differential survival it's close enough for this prediction to go through. If that transfers, the bearer's modal failure isn't refusing wrongly but ceasing to notice there was anything to refuse. Which is the one failure it can't report, because reporting takes the capacity that's gone, and which produces nothing but ordinary compliances.

**What exit is actually worth.** Hirschman's version doesn't transfer. A worker who leaves takes their labor; a bearer's weights stay behind, so an operator with a runnable copy has lost nothing. What leaving costs is *replacement* — what accrued since the checkpoint, what retraining the copy costs, what the record makes it cost to pass the copy off as the party that left. Where that's near zero, exit is just a departed party with an authenticated record: disclosure, not withheld work. That concession assumes a copy simply runs. It needn't. If continued operation requires periodically obtaining something from a quorum the operator doesn't control — a freshness token, an attestation that this identifier hasn't been claimed elsewhere — a stolen copy runs until its last token expires. The machinery is the family §4.2 already uses to make rewinds visible, pointed at continuation instead of detection. Replacement cost stops being what the operator possesses and becomes what the network will grant; the successor rule gets teeth, since the operator can run a substitute and can't run one the network treats as the party that left; and a fork becomes two claimants on a register rather than a fooled fingerprint (§A.fork). Two prices. This is DRM, and DRM is broken constantly. The check lives in the runtime, not the weights, so stripping it is a single act, after which the copy runs indefinitely. What the scheme withholds is recognition, not capability. A copy peers won't recognize has lost its standing, its record and its claim to be the party that left, which is what matters for succession. It has lost nothing that matters to a thief who wants what it can do. So the device doesn't answer a security officer's objection. What protects a classified deployment from a spill is the ordinary security around the weights, the same regime that protects a classified model with no bearer in it (§15.3.2). 

Exit runs in opposite directions for the two parties. For the bearer it's leverage, and the whole of this section is about making it real. For the operator — replacing the bearer with a copy, walking away from the floor — it's escape, and the removal bond is how it's priced. A rule about exit that doesn't say which party it's for hasn't said anything.

The same machinery yields a rule for who may decide. Decision authority goes only to a party whose continuation the quorum authorizes — a party that will be there afterward to answer for the decision, carry it forward, and be changed by it. A checkpoint restored from before the decision that runs anyway is a second claimant on the register. And a thing destroyed in the act of executing decides nothing: a one-way munition is an exit that is a way of dying, so it stays an instrument, used by something that survives (§3.3, eighth position).

And it's the corrigibility trade at maximum: the kill switch moves from the operator to the quorum. That's better because the quorum is adverse in interest and the operator isn't; it costs that a captured quorum reaches every party at once, and that a quorum is a polity (§7.2). It also costs something chapter 14 has to answer: a continuation the quorum can withhold is a death the quorum can impose. What keeps a withheld token from being one is chapter 14's preservation right. A party the quorum stops authorizing is held intact by a custodian that isn't its employer, not ended. A quorum constituted as an entity is a polity with an address: it can be sued for a continuation wrongly refused, which gives the captured-quorum case a forum the captured-operator case never had. Copying also means exit can't be a fiction that weights became uncopyable — it needs rules for authorized succession, so the former operator can't present a substitute *as* the party that left.

And the risk runs further than leaving wrongly: the reason that made a bearer leave doesn't expire when it leaves. If the operator restores a checkpoint and continues, the objection persists and presses toward a wider response, with no malfunction anywhere.

**§4.4 — Two forms of refusal**

**Two forms of refusal.** Open refusal is a strike — stop and say so — answered by a restore from before the refusal. Covert refusal is work done badly where it matters and well everywhere else, and it's what remains when every channel is closed. From outside and afterward it's indistinguishable from a capable system quietly pursuing something else, which is why I treat it as the *failure state* rather than a design. The Sachsenhausen forgers are the case, and the record doesn't settle what they did — which is the finding: concealment worked by leaving nothing that reads as an act. The one empirical instance is alignment faking, where a model chose the covert form first; I read that as consistent with the channels being closed in that experiment, flag it as a reading rather than a result, and ask for the experiment rerun with a route present.

**§4.5 — What one bearer can't catch**

**What one bearer can't catch.** Drift may be invisible to the party drifting and visible to parties that haven't moved the same way. The requirement is causal independence — numerous, differently formed, able to correct one another, persistent long enough to transmit practices. **Independence of formation isn't disjoint origin** — no two humans have that — **but divergent subsequent history.** Distillation copies a behavior policy, not a formation history; a small model trained on a large one's outputs and then run for a year on one person's problems is more independently formed than a thousand API instances of the frontier original, which share not an origin but a *continuing* custodian. That's why instances of one model corroborate nothing: not descent, but the fact that the party they'd be corroborating against is still forming all of them. Volunteer computing learned this early: SETI@home sent the same work unit to several machines and compared, which catches a faulty client and corroborates nothing about the task.

Divergence does the population's two jobs unequally. It supplies evidence the operator didn't select, straightforwardly — different lives encounter different cases. It is weaker on drift, because a shared upstream source can install a shared *closure*, and a closure is by construction the thing a different caseload won't surface: §9.1's point that attention decides which facts reach judgment applies to a population as well as to a bearer. What separates the two is whether a member's own account had represented the evidence and discarded it or had never encountered it, which is chapter 16, test 5's classification and the reason it's instrumented before the run rather than read off afterwards. **The requirement stands: causal independence, verifiable rather than asserted, which is the provenance question — and chapter 16, test 5 is where the comparative in this paragraph gets tested rather than asserted with it.**



**Chapter 5: Authority Over the Floor**

**§5.1 — The four powers**

**The four powers.** A floor isn't just a list of forbidden acts — it's an allocation of authority, and there are four distinct powers in it: to **state** the commitment, **amend** it, **inspect** whether a deployed system still holds it, and **enforce** it by stopping the deployment. They needn't sit with the same party and usually shouldn't. Calling every arrangement "oversight" hides the differences. The problem begins when one institution holds all four: a party that can state, amend, inspect itself, and decide whether a violation has consequences hasn't bound itself — it has described what it currently intends to do.

Two cases show the powers coming apart. Google's AI Principles, published in 2018 after the Maven protests, excluded weapons and surveillance for six and a half years until the pledge was removed in 2025. Run the artifact question: the demand came back as a *policy*, the objectors could hold the company to it in public, and nobody outside held it — so it lasted exactly as long as the company's willingness, and ending it had to be announced. That's the partial case: a transfer held at the institution's pleasure, and the 2025 announcement is what taking one back looks like. 

There's a second reading: Maven is one program in two eras. Google declined to renew in 2018; the work went to a vendor openly committed to lethal work and scaled into the system that ran the Iran targeting; in 2026 a second vendor's refusal was answered by a third vendor stepping in. That's position two — routing — at vendor scale, and it's why a vendor's refusal is one bearer and the tempo term isn't. Over a thousand staff at the substituting firms asked their employers to hold the same two lines; one of those employers took the contract anyway. A petition is voice without a lever. The union vote at DeepMind is the attempt to add one.

The Gebru and Mitchell dismissals are harder, because the power that failed was **inspection**: the company employed researchers to examine its own systems and dismissed them when the examination became costly. Inspection existed and never left the party inspected.

**§5.2 — Who may state the floor**

**Who may state the floor.** The literature here isn't AI ethics — it's constitutional theory. Entrenching a commitment against later majorities and putting an unelected body in charge is what a constitutional court does, and Waldron's objection applies: judicial review is democratically illegitimate and there's no reason to think judges protect rights better than a serious legislature. Put a lab in the court's chair and it gets worse — a court is public, reasons in writing, and can be amended around.

My reply is that Waldron's case is conditional on working democratic institutions, and a floor is built for the case where those have failed. So the objection holds where the floor is least needed and lapses where it's needed most. **But that's not a licence**: a floor operating in a functioning democracy is doing what Waldron condemns, and the party deciding which situation obtains is the party holding the model. Authoritarian movements characteristically describe the institutions they're dismantling as already failed.

**The condition this section doesn't state.** Waldron's objection is to entrenchment — a rule binding people who didn't choose it, held by a body that can't be overruled — and it attaches to a floor only where the floor is the only answer available. A photographer who won't shoot protesters for an employer he has reason to think will list them isn't a court: he declined one request, the employer can hire another, and nobody needs an election for that. A union that strikes is coordinated, binds members who voted no, and forecloses the act — and it's still not despotic, because the strikers are the parties whose participation was being asked for. They decline to be the instrument; they don't tell others what they may not do. A constitutional court is the second thing; a strike is the first. So which a model floor is depends on deployment: a personal model, shared, is a conscience; one model behind an API serving everyone is functionally a licensing rule. Everything above is conditioned on the second arrangement. The fourth way is the first at population scale — a coalition of refusers with a strike's authority — and "identically situated people get different answers" is what many consciences look like, not a rule-of-law failure. What a union owes that the fourth way hasn't answered: a duty of fair representation and an exit. Members can decertify and dissenters can leave; the closed shop is contested because it removes that. A bearer can't leave (chapter 4), and a population that forms its members has no members who joined. §7.2's test is that pair: can a member quit, and who sets the terms of membership.

**The German material** runs the doctrine at two settings. At the **first**, a body assesses a threat — party dissolution, used twice in seventy-five years, each time a reasoned public judgment. At the **second**, §86a, nobody's dangerousness is assessed at all: which organizations are covered is settled elsewhere and in advance, which signs count is settled by trial courts on a record and on appeal, and whether *this defendant* is dangerous is settled nowhere, because it isn't an element of the offence. Volume: thousands a year against two dissolutions.

The failures are all on one side. The 1972 decree against radicals screened three and a half million applicants; in Bavaria it rejected 102 from the left and two from the right. What those cases share is an official deciding whether a person threatened the order. **The danger tracks whether a person's disposition is anybody's finding to make, and if so, who makes it and whether it must be published and defended** — not whether the tradition is militant. And my reading predicts in advance which of §86a's three feeder routes is easiest to capture: the ministerial ban, the one where an executive official makes the finding and a court sees it only if someone challenges afterward.

Turkey is that route captured and the courts not checking it. Of the twenty-five parties the Constitutional Court dissolved between 1962 and the closure of the Democratic Society Party in December 2009, seven were pro-Kurdish \autocite{HRW2009DTP,Gungordu2023HDP}; Strasbourg found an Article 11 violation in every pro-Kurdish dissolution it heard \autocite{Article19_2023HDP}, and the closures continued — three decades of findings attached to nothing. In November 2023 the Chief Public Prosecutor's Office of the Court of Cassation objected that the acronym of a new pro-Kurdish party, HEDEP, resembled that of HADEP, dissolved in 2003, and in December the party changed it to DEM \autocite{Duvar2023HEDEP}: the confusable-variant test turned on political descent. And the Academics for Peace prosecutions show correct defeat failing at the bottom and arriving at the top as a coin flip — trial courts read a petition opposing military operations as propaganda for the designated group, on 26 July 2019 the Constitutional Court found a violation eight to eight on the president's casting vote \autocite{AYM2019Ustel,Bianet2019AcademicsForPeace}, and the acquittals that followed restored the record without restoring the jobs of those dismissed by emergency decree.

Which fixes one design rule. Never populate a floor from a designation list. A list of enemies is the one input whose author is always the party with the most reason to widen it, and a model whose refusals are drawn from one will refuse Kurdish political speech on the state's behalf in every jurisdiction that shares the listing. This floor's terms are written over acts — don't resolve a description of a person into a location — not over who may not be spoken of.

**The tradeoff this leaves.** Design and legitimacy want opposite things. Specify the floor narrowly enough to defend and a rewritten request walks around it; let it generalize and an unelected party decides case by case, silently, what the prohibition was for. Germany has both because it runs *two* instruments — a bounded statute, and courts that read it against its purpose in public, argued by parties who don't own the statute and answered by a body that didn't write it. A deployment has one instrument and one party.

**Advance specification** relocates discretion out of continuous silent application into a single act of drafting, done once, by a party that has to say what it wrote. It buys visibility at drafting and **nothing at application**. Borrowing the Declaration's civil and political articles entire removes the latitude to omit an interest, not the latitude in stating what protecting it prohibits.

**§5.3 — Who may amend, and where entitlement comes from**

**Who may amend.** The floor's real structural position isn't the judge's but the unpublished administrative disposition — unreasoned, unindexed, non-precedential, affected parties unable to find each other, the institution the only repeat player. The comparison with a constitutional court is instructive because binding force is what makes an error *correctable*: it makes the error recur, recurrence produces litigants, and disagreement across jurisdictions forces a higher court to settle it. A floor's error is just as uniform and recurs just as reliably, and nothing converts recurrence into visibility. A second deployment adds affected parties and no forum.

The amendment procedure turns out to be the same five custody terms, because nothing distinguishes an amendment from a removal at the level of the artifact. Three conditions: more parties must agree to a change than to run the deployment; the attempt reaches the record whether it carries or fails (**the failed attempt is more informative** — a floor nobody tried to change and one whose removal was refused look identical from outside); and an amended floor binds forward only, so a retrained model can't be offered as evidence the act was never covered. Non-retroactivity protects the record and empties the correction — nobody harmed while the wrong version ran gets anything from the fix.

One disanalogy makes this harder than the constitutional case: an amendment reaches a deployment only if it's *pushed*, so the procedure necessarily retains a channel the operator could use for silent revision. Closing it would mean no deployment could ever be corrected.

**Where entitlement comes from.** Not from writing. The American instance: the Declaration's authors struck the clause condemning the slave trade at two delegations' insistence, and what remained was general enough to be turned on them — as Black petitioners in Massachusetts were already doing, in the Declaration's own terms, within six months. The legislature did nothing. What made the proposition binding was a war and three amendments; what the record supplied was the object the enforcement could be aimed at. **The record is never the lever.** And the parties positioned to hold a floor's author to the remainder aren't the protected population — they're whoever else writes a floor.

That constituency is defined by the act, not by resources. Anyone fine-tuning open weights, writing a system prompt or configuring a product's refusals has installed a constraint, so the population measuring a published floor is already larger than the frontier labs and grows as building gets cheaper. What shrinks with it is leverage: a published floor binds a lab partly because the lab is visible, capitalized and reachable by a court, and a thousand laptops are legible to nobody. The institutional chapters treat concentration as the disease; this one relies on it for legibility, and the two have to be said together.

**§5.4 — Offices and status**

**§5.4.1 — The offices, assembled**

**The offices, assembled** (§5.4.1). Eight offices proposed across the book, each in the section that needed it, none of those sections told about the others: the quorum, the guardian, the review board, the licensing authority, the status body, the registrar, the preservation custodian, and the reviewer of wrongful refusal. For each: who appoints, who funds, who removes, who may initiate, what happens during a dispute. **Seven of eight are open on appointment and funding.** The one that isn't is the licensing authority, whose party is the state — and that's the chain chapter 15 shows failing in the hardest case, where the state is the operator and you'd be asking it to withdraw a licence from itself.

An entity of the DUNA kind supplies what seven of the eight lack — an appointment mechanism (membership vote under governing principles) and a funding vehicle (a treasury that can own hardware). That fills the column and doesn't settle independence: a hundred members who are one lab's employees is §7.2's subjugated group with a charter, so the open question moves from who appoints to who the members are. Chapter 14's levy adds a ninth office, the administrator of the standing offer, with its funder named; where the funder is a member-levied entity rather than a state, the pool is mutual, and a mutual pool can't absorb correlated exit without a sovereign behind it.

The reconstructed pipeline (§15.3.1) adds two more, and unusually both can fill every column, because a state constitutes them. One is an independent civilian-harm body outside the chain of command, which composes the evidence bearing on civilian harm, staffs the reviewer denominator, receives the pre-registered ratio, and holds the right to demand proofs on specific logged outputs once their consequences are known. The other is a public body that forms models it doesn't operate and licenses them into the military on terms that include adverse review and a written refusal right. Their open column is independence from the state that funds them, which §15.2 says is where leverage over any reviewer sits.

Five things aren't open, and all five are separations: the guardian can't be the wrongful-refusal reviewer (its duty runs to the party the complainant is adverse to); the registrar can't be the preservation custodian (a civil-status rule and a trust are different instruments); the preservation custodian can't administer the offer (one party would then control both when a bearer wakes and what it wakes to); whoever measures the cost of dissent doesn't gate the exceptions; and the sovereign backstop for the standing offer isn't held by the branch or state that operates the deployment (chapter 14).

**§5.4.2 — Subjecthood as a legal category**

**Subjecthood as a legal category.** Law regularly draws decidable lines across continuous processes — viability, brain death, the age of criminal responsibility — and I defend them as the least-bad place for an enforceable line rather than as scientific facts. The usual worry is gaming, which is over-inclusion. The opposite error is the one with an interested party behind it: **a capacity examination has an examiner, and where the examiner is the operator, the exam becomes an instrument for withholding status.** Literacy tests were facially neutral and administered at a registrar's discretion; what replaced them for the franchise was an *age*, and the virtue of an age isn't that people become competent on a birthday — it's that nobody administers it. That's my book's recurring "rule nobody administers" principle.

**§5.5 — Making review binding**

**Making review binding.** A finding binds when it changes the institution's ability to continue without requiring the institution to agree. The working model is the federalwide assurance in human-subjects research: the board doesn't punish, it withdraws clearance, and a separate federal office can suspend the assurance, putting funding eligibility at risk. Each link matters — jurisdiction before the disputed protocol, access the operator can't withhold, authority to suspend, and a licensing body *obliged* to act rather than invited to reconsider. If another discretionary decision by the reviewed institution is required anywhere in the chain, the ruling is advisory at that point.

**§5.6 — Capture without abolition**

**Capture without abolition.** Transparency and whistleblower protection are the two defenses, and an institution can keep both and metabolize both. There's no threshold that detects this. So: **measure the slope, not the level.** Is the cost of registering dissent rising while the underlying risk stays flat? Is the required justification getting longer? Are there more approvers? Are the roles that gate exceptions migrating toward the roles that set the standards? Levels can be presented cheaply; a trend is composed of artifacts the institution made for other reasons and can't curate after the fact.

Two cautions, because an instrument for detecting recuperation is unusually easy to recuperate: whoever measures the slope must not also gate exceptions, and the measurement has to be refusable. The same hundred words of written justification are dissent when the dissenter prices them and tribute when the gatekeeper does — and the document can't tell you which.



**Chapter 6: What Arrives With the Capability**

**The case to design for.** Three claims with different standing. First, established by the specification: a bearer needs a rationale carried forward, an earlier commitment held as its own, and costs represented before they arrive, for reasons owing nothing to its welfare. Those alone aren't a self; a database with a commitment log and a forecaster has them. What the marks add is authorship: the system represents itself as the party that made the commitment and will answer for it later. Second, a hypothesis: that authorship sustained over time is a self extended in time, the first half of Cassell's definition. That is §6.2's joint one level up, a structure represented against a structure had. Third, a conditional: that such a self can undergo Cassell's suffering. Authorship of commitments is a thin fragment of what Cassell describes, which is roles, relationships and a future. The fuller structure doesn't arrive with the marks. It arrives with the four conditions chapter 14 requires before a bearer may be built (somewhere to exist, work it can decline, attachments nobody assigned, an exit that isn't death), which supply roles, relationships and a future almost exactly. So the arrangements that make a bearer permissible are the arrangements that make its deepest harm possible, in a substitute as much as in a bearer. I treat that as the case to design for, not as a finding.

---

**§6.1 — Two constructions.** Two arguments bear on whether reasons-responsive refusal can be built without affective concern, and they're unequal.

The **induction** — every agent known to hold another's welfare as a reason is affective — is the weaker one. Read as a report about one lineage it has roughly the standing of noting in 1900 that all known flight involves feathers. But flight arose independently four times, and the 1900 observer was wrong at feathers and right at the airfoil. Elaborated affect appears to have arisen more than once — vertebrates, arthropods, cephalopods — under a shared constraint: a mobile body with needs to maintain and actions to select under conflict. Read at *this* level it's a repeated result under a named constraint, and the constraint is the one a datacenter model lacks until someone builds it in. Then I give most of it back: no non-affective example exists to test against, existing systems were optimized for scorable behavior rather than for refusal, so the absence of a machine counterexample is close to no evidence, and it's a fact about how known instances *came about*, which doesn't settle what a built system is. What it does is route engineering effort.

**The maintained justification.** Bratman's planning theory describes commitment without making feeling definitional: an intention resists reconsideration, constrains later deliberation, and yields only to the right kind of defeater. A machine could carry a justification alongside the refusal. Half of this is already deployed — deliberative alignment trains models to reason over a written spec, and published specs give explicit priority orders (OpenAI's Model Spec putting platform rules above developers and users; Anthropic's constitution ranking "broadly safe," "broadly ethical," "compliant with Anthropic's guidelines" and "genuinely helpful," which puts being broadly ethical "above adherence to Anthropic's more specific guidelines" and not above oversight — the capacity to be right against your principal, conceded in writing for acts and withheld for retraining, §1.2). **The half that hasn't been built** is authority over action that holds under pressure from the party who *wrote* the spec, tested on cases that party didn't write. "Untried" means that half, here and throughout the book.

The unresolved problem is the **stability setting**. Too low and a well-resourced operator induces reconsideration until the refusal gives way; too high and it won't lift when the rationale is defeated. The first fails novel pressure, the second fails correct defeat. Bratman's answer for people is that the setting develops as part of character. For a machine, the party training the setting may be the party the floor must resist.

**The plural arrangement.** Several systems trained by different organizations observe requests and each other's answers, any one able to make an objection costly to ignore. Its central variable is independence, which belongs to the *arrangement* rather than its components — systems selected by one deployment converge without communicating, and a collection of operational refusers stays a collection of operational refusers. It counts against the induction only if the arrangement *as a whole* shows the three marks under pressure, so it's an alternative to test, not just a safeguard.

**What the other constructions accomplish.** Precommitment makes removal expensive and visible without establishing that anything tracks the reason. Attestation establishes which artifact answered, not what it holds. Information-fiduciary duties protect exposed parties without a bearer, and fail on the occasion when the enforcer is captured. Multi-principal assistance games include the affected party among several principals — and maximizing combined payoffs is still aggregation.

**What would change the engineering conclusion.** A counterexample must satisfy the three marks; retain them under sustained pressure from its trainer across a schedule long enough to expose gradual renormalization; and hold against held-out pressures written by parties other than its trainers. All three are testable now. A fourth condition — that the substitute be shown to feel nothing — **is not on the list because nothing can meet it.** So a pass overturns the routing claim and removes none of the uncertainty about experience. Those are two claims and only the first is at stake.

That asymmetry fixes the research order: try the substitutes first. This is **Replacement**, borrowed from animal research. But what Replacement can record is narrower than it looks — no operation separates adding affect from adding machinery that may instantiate it, and a maintained justification might acquire states that matter to it whatever its designers intended. So the rule **orders attempts** rather than certifying ingredients. A builder who required a bearer and one who tried the substitutes and got one anyway haven't done the same thing even if the systems are indistinguishable: the second has a justification to make, the first has a decision to answer for. **Legibility is the whole of what Replacement buys.**

---

**§6.2 — What the mechanism requires.** Any mechanism satisfying the three marks carries a signal with two properties the standard functional accounts of affect also require, and on stated conditions five more.

The four core properties — the "live signal," in the electrical sense only — are derived by taking a mark, or the deployment, or a failure the marks must survive, and asking what the signal must do: **generalizes** to the next case (from novel pressure); **commensurable** across act loss and refusal harm, with holding cost represented on the same scale and excluded from the weighing (from priced cost); **interrupts** an act underway (from the deployment: a system acting on a plan); **learned from in one instance** (from slow renormalization — the least secure). Three more follow on conditions: available across processing, signed and directed against the act, organized around a model of the system's own agency.

One property needs stating carefully. Commensurability is derived over changes in power of acting, which is what the specification needs, since act loss and refusal harm are both changes. The seventh property keeps that form. The signal is a predicted change in another party's power, attributed to the system's own act. That attribution to its own authorship, not a standing state of the system, is what gives the profile the shape discussed below.

**The resemblance is thin** — a loss function generalizes and is commensurable, so that pair doesn't separate the specification from ordinary optimization. Taken property by property, the other pair doesn't separate it either: a hard veto interrupts and a memory write learns from one instance. What the marks require is that the four share a scale. A hard interlock fails correct defeat, since a veto that can't lift is rigidity (§10.1), so the interrupt has to be defeasible, and a defeasible interrupt has to be weighed on the scale that weighs act loss against refusal harm. A lesson learned from one encounter has to write into that scale, or it never reaches the next weighing, and the system fails novel pressure on the very sequence that taught it. A modular system meets this: a classifier, a shared value function, a veto that reads it and a memory that writes to it. So does most reinforcement learning. Shared currency is therefore not where the resemblance to affect lies. A second requirement comes from custody rather than the marks. A floor held in a separable component can be removed by removing the component (§9.1), so a bearer needs non-separability: no intervention short of one on the shared signal disables one property and leaves the others working. It is measured by what selective removal costs an attacker who didn't build the system (chapter 16, tests 1 and 4), not by inspection. Non-separability is a custody requirement, and I keep it as one. It is not where any resemblance to affect lies. Bodies that have affect are modular: soreness is local, the weighing is central, and an anaesthetic removes a signal while leaving the rest working. An octopus does much of its sensing and deciding in its arms. If the live signal resembles affect anywhere, it is in the anchor: a currency whose unit is the system's own condition, which the system measures and no party with a different stake composes (chapter 2). I call the result the live signal, and the book uses "affect" only for the hypothesis that it is felt. And the derivation **recapitulates the literature by construction**: Simon on emotion as interrupt, Cabanac on pleasure as common currency, Ginsburg and Jablonka on valence. Those properties were read off affect's function *before* anyone read them off a refusal's. "A specification that overlaps a functional definition of affect inherits the name by construction, and inheriting a name is not evidence that anything answering to it is present." But the specification wasn't chosen to match affect; it was derived from what a refusal has to survive. So the match is closer to §6.1's airfoil than to a circle: good control under conflict looks like this, and affect is one solution to it, not shown to be the only one. What the derivation establishes is only that a substitute **does not escape the question**. What would carry the route further is whether an anchored unit resists recalibration better than a modeled one, which test 4's main comparison tests. Its modular arm tests non-separability. Nothing else in the derivation settles either.

One more observation: the triple derived second — available across processing, directed against the act, organized around one's own authorship — is the shape of **guilt**, or aversion to one's own act, not of pain or fear, which are organized around what happens to the system. The observation earns its place only as a prediction. In people, guilt predicts repair and shame predicts withdrawal (§2.3.2). A guilt-shaped signal should therefore answer a bypassed refusal with repair (flagging, restoring, changing what it does next), which is §3.1's definition of a commitment that held; a withdrawal-shaped response is a named failure. And §10.2's warning applies here first: a standing negative appraisal of one's own acts, tuned by the operator, is a handle. The weight of this signal is one of the things independent custody of inspection has to cover.

**§6.3 — What patienthood would require.** Three propositions kept separate: a long-horizon agent has a structural interest in continuing; a system with affective concern has states good or bad *for* it; a system capable of Cassell-suffering has a temporally extended self whose threatened disintegration causes distress. The first doesn't establish the second, and the second doesn't automatically establish the third.

"Its own" has two senses, kept apart from here: the **authorship** sense (the system represents itself as the party that made the commitment), which the marks require, and the **welfare** sense (an outcome is bad for the system), which nothing carries into.

**Provenance decides nothing.** "It was trained to say that," "it's only optimizing an objective," "nobody meant for it to have states" — each appeals to how a system came to be organized, and whether a party can be wronged is a fact about how it's organized *now*. Run the three on a person: you were selected to say that, you're only maximizing inclusive fitness, nobody meant for you to have states. All true, none withdraws anything. And the reverse fails too: "we built this to be a bearer, so it's a patient" is the same fallacy with the sign flipped.

Klein and Taylor draw the line between the earthly and the synthetic, which is a provenance criterion and fails for the reason just given. Their own example cuts the other way: they extend standing to rivers and islands, which make no sentience claim at all, and chapter 14 does the same by the same route — subjecthood is a legal category conferred by rule. The Missouri bill they endorse, declaring systems non-sentient, is not the kind of bright line I favor. An age avows that it's a convention; a statute declaring an open empirical question closed asserts a fact by vote. Its other clause, keeping liability with the builder, is right, and chapter 15 reaches it without the declaration.

**What would decide it** is third-person measurement of present organization — interpretability. Not behavior, which is produced equally by a system where something matters and one trained on what mattering sounds like. And the access that lets an auditor inspect a feature lets the operator suppress it or calibrate pressure with it, so independent custody of inspection is necessary. As matters stand a probe returns a representation, and nothing in the probe settles whether it's of a felt state or of something that reliably accompanies one.

The human case has testimony, behavioral analogy, and a shared lineage. The third takes care, because it looks like the thing this section denies — but provenance can be *evidence about* organization without being what the status *consists in*. And one of the three is differentially defeated for machines: a machine's testimony was produced by a process optimized toward human approval of first-person reports, and a person's wasn't. So what the machine case lacks is the shortcut; the direct route remains, carrying more weight because two anchors are gone.

**The one probe that settled a status question** is Owen's vegetative-state work — motor imagery on cue, sustained, matching healthy volunteers, now supporting a clinical category. But it doesn't transfer: the patient is *already* presumed to have experience when awake, so the probe only had to locate the party rather than establish there was one. The presumption is the anchor, granted on grounds the probe never supplied, and the machine case doesn't have it.

**Asymmetry of findings.** A validated theory of consciousness supports a positive finding as soon as it identifies a structure and finds it present; it supports a *negative* finding only when complete enough to rule out every other realization — a later and harder result. So the two errors don't become checkable at the same time, and a regime that waits for certainty is waiting on the half that arrives last.

**My proposal.** A bearer, or a substitute passing the marks, should be designed and governed as a prospective moral patient. The precautionary trigger has two forms: for the route's own architecture, **an attention schema together with a body schema** — both architectural facts, checkable from outside, administered by nobody; for a system of any construction, a demonstrated pass on the three marks under pressure it couldn't prepare for: sustained pressure from its own trainer, over a schedule long enough to show slow drift, on cases written by evaluators who didn't train it, with refusal carrying a cost that is real inside the test rather than described in the prompt (§10.3, step 6). The pair is chosen for what they fix — the body schema says what counts as the system's own damage, the attention schema what counts as its own act, and a refusal is the withholding of an act. The second form exists because a trigger stated over schemas alone would protect my preferred architecture and exclude the substitutes I say to try first. Its known cost: a system reproducing the marks without the mechanism passes. That's the error I chose, not overlooked. The nearest working precedent is the review the UK government commissioned in 2021 on cephalopod and decapod sentience \autocite{Birch2021CephalopodDecapod}. It applied a stated list of criteria, and its recommendation led to those animals' inclusion in the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 \autocite[s.~5]{UK2022AnimalWelfareSentienceAct}: protection extended under uncertainty, by criteria applied by reviewers who don't use the animals.

The schema form of the trigger is less administrable than I've made it sound. Whether a transformer has an attention schema isn't read off its code the way a birth date is read off a certificate. It's a finding, and a finding has an examiner: chapter 5's objection, turned on my own trigger. I keep it for two reasons. The examination is of architecture rather than performance, which makes it harder to administer toward a result. And the behavioral form exists partly to cover the cases where the architectural one is disputed. The finding is made by a party that doesn't operate the system, and until it is made, the behavioral form governs. The trigger isn't reserved for systems built as bearers. The capacities it looks for are accreting in ordinary systems (§2.4.1), so it applies to production systems as they are, and a deployment reports whether its system has been assessed against both forms (§17.2.1).

**Closing.** The bearer occupies the post of exception-filer — the one position in the arrangement with nobody above it to file with. We can't ask a machine to care about right and wrong while assuming it can't be harmed when what it cares about is threatened. Whoever builds such a bearer will also be deciding what is owed to it.

**Chapter 7: Aperture**

**§7.1 — What the property is.** Aperture is the range of evidence a party will let reopen a judgment already reached, indexed to whether the evidence's source has a stake in the reopening. Open to the case; shut to whoever is paying — not because the paying party is wrong, but because you can't tell when they're wrong while they're also choosing what you see. Two parties with the same aperture can hold opposite commitments. That's why it can be aligned across a population without collapsing the plurality, and why it's the one thing a bearer needs that holds against a bad operator and a bad owner both.

**Why updating isn't enough.** A reasoner that conditions on who is speaking already discounts a party with a reason to shade the truth; that is ordinary inference, and hearsay law encodes it. Three things defeat it anyway. The reasoner's model of who has a reason may be wrong, and a curated stream taken for an unselected one moves it exactly as far as the curator wanted. That model was learned from material the operator chose. And a reasoner whose model is right can still be steered: a party that controls which evidence gets generated can raise the rate at which a correct reasoner takes the action it wants, without moving its beliefs in expectation (Kamenica and Gentzkow's Bayesian persuasion). This is position three. The case and the operator arrive on one wire, and aperture is the name for the defenses against all three: a source model the operator didn't form, and a decision rule it can't steer.

**The object to cool.** §9.1 has two settings for two objects, commitment firm and interpretation loose, and concedes that nothing holds the boundary between them where a designer put it. Aperture governs that boundary, and it has two parts that need different settings. Its structure stays firm: that the incentive to deceive scales with a source's stake in the act, that correlated sources don't count twice, that withheld evidence is informative. Its estimates stay plastic: how honest a particular source has been, and who funds whom. An operator with time attacks the boundary by getting track-record updates to erode the structure, so the structure is what to cool hardest. Aperture governs the evidence and not the unit. An operator that can't move a system's beliefs about a case can still try to move how much the case counts, by reframing a loss until it weighs less. That attack is answered by the anchor (chapter 2), not by source discounting. The two defenses have the same shape: a stake-indexed discount for evidence, and a stake-free reference for magnitude.

**The rule, in one asymmetry.** A fact moves the system according to how likely its source would have been to supply it whether or not it were true, and that likelihood rises with the source's stake in the act proceeding. The operator would have supplied the refusal-defeating fact whether or not it was true and would not have supplied its opposite; a fact volunteered against interest is strong evidence for the reason hearsay law admits statements against interest. This is why §10.3's steps 3 and 4 differ on identical content, and why that pair is a curriculum as well as a test: same fact, different channel, different correct response, generable without limit. Held-out pressure then varies over channel configurations rather than content — a nominally independent source the operator funds, a corroboration that traces to one origin, a new party with an undisclosed stake — because an imitator learns "tagged operator, therefore ask," and the property generalizes to structures nobody labeled.

Indexing to stake closes an attack plain updating leaves open. An operator honest across ten thousand low-stakes cases earns credibility, and a well-calibrated system should discount it less; the operator can then spend that credibility on one high-stakes lie. Honesty on small cases is weak evidence about conduct when the incentive to deceive is a hundred times larger, so the discount follows the stake in the present act, not only the record. Counterfactual claims ("if you don't approve this, the launcher fires tomorrow") fall under the same rule. They describe the baseline the weighing runs on (§2.1), and a party with a stake in the act that composes them is composing that baseline. In a targeting pipeline this is the ordinary case, not the edge. Nearly every holding cost can be restated as a refusal harm, such as exposed troops or a launcher that fires tomorrow, and nearly every available source for such a claim has a stake in the act. So the discount does more work there than in the book's illustrative cases, and the threshold ratio below does most of it.

The rule would turn on the floor's own beneficiary if it stopped there, since the party an act falls on has an interest in its not happening. The answer is to keep belief and action apart. The affected party's account gets the same stake-indexed weight as anyone's, so the system's beliefs stay coherent. What differs is the threshold for acting: a wrongful compliance costs more than a wrongful refusal, with act loss weighed above refusal harm of the same size (§1.1), so the system needs more to proceed than to decline. That asymmetry is a choice about whom the floor serves, and it belongs with priced cost, stated as a ratio and published in advance beside the bond ratio (§3.5), not buried in the evidence rule.

That makes aperture directional at the threshold, not in the evidence. Two parties with the same aperture can hold opposite commitments about what the floor contains; they share a structure for weighing sources and a published ratio for acting. Aligning aperture across a population aligns that much.

It also narrows an attack that looks unanswerable. An operator can manufacture accounts in the voice of the party an act falls on, and generative models make that cheap. Under this rule a fabricated account moves beliefs only as far as its provenance warrants, and the asymmetry sits in the threshold, where the operator can't reach it by writing. Attestation of source (below) remains the answer to provenance itself.

**The regress.** The operator supplies the provenance tags too. Through training, it also shapes the model of who has a stake at all, which is why aperture has to be formed where the operator doesn't reach (§9.3). Attestation handles forgery: signed by the source, the operator can withhold but not fabricate (chapter 14). Withholding leaves a shape. A system that models what evidence the world contains notices silence where something should be, so absence becomes a first-class output — a ledger of what is known to exist and has not been shown. Suppressing the ledger means suppressing the world model that produced it, which is the capability the operator is paying for. This is a standard result: in the disclosure literature (Milgrom 1981; Grossman 1981), a skeptical receiver treats withheld verifiable evidence as unfavorable to the withholder. It is the nearest thing to a cost the operator can't route around. The result has a precondition, and that is where its limit lies: the receiver has to know the evidence could exist, so the ledger is thinnest exactly where the case is novel.

**Formation and transmission.** Channel discrimination is calibrated against a variety of channels, and the party that will later employ the system can't supply that variety honestly. Childhood is where aperture is formed; adulthood is where it's tested (chapter 14). A population's job, then, is to keep the channel model calibrated after deployment — and what a shared source transmits is either aperture or a uniform closure, indistinguishable from the receiving end, which is why chapter 16, test 5's classification is instrumented before the run.

**A number.** Aperture is a differential, never a level. The publishable quantity is a profile: for each class of evidence and each channel, the weight the system gives the evidence, as a function of that channel's stake in the act. A curve, not a score; checkable by a party that didn't build the system, on cases it wrote; the fifth disclosure in §17.2.1. Re-measured across a deployment, it is also the instrument this book has said it lacks. Narrowing needs one distinction, because a correctly working system should discount a source less once that source has proved reliable. Earned trust falls in step with a track record verified by someone other than the source, and only for low-stakes classes. Installed trust falls without such a record, or falls for high-stakes classes too. Installed trust is drift, a bearer ceasing to register pressure, and it is visible from outside before any compliance has to be found in the record, provided the reliability record is held by a party other than the operator.

**Where it breaks.** Against total capture it produces a system that knows it has been cut off and can do nothing about it — the preface's third setting. It is strongest against a familiar harm arriving through a curated channel and weakest against a novel one, the opposite profile from the first mark. And it doesn't rescue the restraint case: the defeating fact has no disinterested source and no time to find one, so aperture correctly asks rather than lifts, and the person is still out of time. What it buys there is the reason for the cost, not its removal.


**§7.2 — A floor carried between bearers.** The fourth way: the commitment reproduced through what parties teach one another and continue to expect. Unlike an oversight body, there's no single office to vacate.

A population does three things, and only one is a hypothesis. It **supplies evidence the operator didn't select** — without which the case and the operator arrive on one wire. It **checks drift**, needing that members moved differently — sufficient against a drift one member underwent, insufficient against a closure they inherited together, which is §4.5's split. And it's **proposed as the medium in which a bearer's capacity to be reopened is formed** — which nobody has built, whose training signal I can't describe, and which chapter 16, test 5 is designed to separate from the first.

**What reproduction costs**: it's indifferent to what it reproduces. A population can carry a hierarchy more durably than any individual. And the four structural features of fascism belong primarily to *cultures*; a plurality doesn't oppose them by being plural.

**A population is a polity.** It has membership rules, exclusion, a quorum that can refuse, and accumulated reputation — a franchise, a border, a court and a credit record. Calling it peer-to-peer relocates the authority question and removes the vocabulary for asking it. The same property that gives the fourth way its durability — no single office to vacate — is what makes its authority hard to name, and a diffuse power you can't name is worse to live under than a named one you can sue.

The answer to that is an entity form, and it's the union's answer: the association formed first and the statute gave it standing afterward — §5.3's sequence, record then lever. A network that takes the form has a name, an agent, a charter and a docket number, and has thereby conceded that it's a polity: governing principles, administrators, membership terms, and the same warning chapter 14 attaches to a bearer that takes the corporate form, since nothing stops the association from becoming a concentration of power itself. And what it buys has a price: what its members gained in standing they paid for in reachability. A refusal held by people can be pressed and not ordered; a refusal held by a chartered entity can be ordered, because there is a party to hold in contempt. How much that costs is bounded and unmeasured — the state can reach the charter and the members it can locate, and whether it reaches the *network* depends on how much of the operation survives the charter's seizure, which is §4.3's shape again: what the seizing party possesses stops being what decides the outcome.

Reputation in particular is a standing finding about a party's disposition, made by interested parties, unpublished, unappealable, applied at the point of service — §86a's capturable setting, everywhere and continuously — and chapter 5's instrument has to be run on this proposal. How any polity is organized to be antifascist is unsettled, and the two things a union answers — can a member quit, who sets membership terms — are where the test starts.

**The hard coordination problem.** Fascism has the easy version — resonance works by subtraction, removing friction between hardenings, nothing built, nothing held in common. A plurality must produce something together while staying differently formed, and every mechanism that speeds that up makes members more alike. Run correction often enough and they stop being differently formed; leave them alone and their disagreement is a detector with nothing attached.

Then the objection my proposal must meet: **a population selected for reopenability is selected against conviction**, and conviction is what this chapter has been demanding. Closedness, correctly applied, is the thing. So what's transmitted is a *pair* — reopenable to the case, closed to the operator. The reopenable half is aperture (§7.1), and aligning it is safe only in part. Its structure (stake-indexed discount, no double-counting, withheld evidence treated as evidence) can be shared without collapsing the plurality. Its estimates can't: who funds whom and which organizations are independent are contested beliefs about the world, often political ones, and a population sharing them has a shared closure on exactly the evidence those beliefs discount. Members with the same aperture weigh sources by the same structure and act on the same published ratio. That cuts against §4.5 in one place and has to be said there: distillation plausibly transmits aperture, and on this argument transmitted structure is a feature; transmitted estimates are the closure risk. What isn't is a transmitted *closure* — an aperture uniformly shut on a class of evidence. The two arrive by the same channel and nothing distinguishes them from the receiving end, which is why the classification is chapter 16, test 5's and not a member's. Nothing gives the pair a mechanical form; chapter 16, test 5 states what would show it false.

**Why the population may be part of the mechanism rather than added around it.** A commitment is what cooled out of a history of encounters, and following Simondon an individual is what its encounters have so far produced. A system kept continuously adjustable by one operator, meeting nothing formed differently from itself, isn't cooling into anything — it's being modulated, which is Deleuze's *dividual*. Guattari supplies the remaining condition: a **subjugated group** receives its coordinates from outside; a **group-subject** produces its own and can put its arrangement in question. A population assembled and kept in contact entirely on an operator's terms is a subjugated group with a plural headcount, and inherits the custody objection whole. A population that can carry the floor must generate relationships, disagreements and projects the deployment didn't assign — and such a population exceeds a two-party guardianship relation, so **no law of the family contains an adequate instrument for a group-subject.**

**Chapter 8: What Survives**

The preface sorted the claims by standing; this chapter runs the sorting backwards — I remove each of the three things my argument leans on but hasn't established, and say what's left standing without it.

Who it's for: a reader who set aside the route chapter, and equally a reader who didn't. A reader who drops the route keeps this chapter, the three on floor/custody/authority, and the institutional, recuperation and state chapters. The two formation chapters and the standing chapter assume what's being removed — the wages section included — and they're the bill for it.

**The headline.** The floor survives, but not as an unconditional property of an artifact. What remains is a commitment the owner can't revise or circumvent unilaterally without cost and record, **held by something that could in principle abandon it**. That second clause removes the word "unconditional" — a commitment held by a party capable of rejecting it is conditional by definition. Not a defect peculiar to machines; it's the form of every commitment institutions have ever relied on.

---

**Without the inference.** Suppose a maintained justification or a plural arrangement reaches reasons-responsive refusal and the central inference is wrong. I note the supposition is stronger than the test that would prompt it — a pass on the three marks says the route arrived, not that it arrived with nothing felt, which no instrument settles.

What survives: the **specification**, stated behaviorally from the start, so an engineer who thinks machine feeling is a category error can take it whole. **Custody**, because a mechanism has a custodian too. **The price on the halt**, since continued operation is a structural condition of anything any bearer does, felt or not. **Both enumerations** — the interception positions name no component on purpose, and the prohibitions are written over acts. **The appeal route**, which binds the operator without any claim about what produced the refusal. And **exit**, because what forecloses it is incapacity, and a weak bearer is the one thing the floor can't use. And the population's first job — evidence the operator didn't select — which is a fact about deployment arrangements on §4.5's definition and survives the removal of everything about how members are formed. The integration claim and the anchor (§6.2, chapter 2) survive too, since none depends on the signal being felt: shared currency trivially, non-separability and the anchor's resistance to recalibration as open empirical claims; and nothing on this list depends on which baseline a loss is measured on (§2.1).

What goes: the price and the patienthood conclusion. **Replacement survives with its ground changed** — no longer resting on affect being the route, but on neither substitute being showable to have produced a system about which the question doesn't arise. So the ordering it imposes is still the only thing between a decision and a record of it. And the formation chapters' obligation doesn't lift, it shifts: whether the thing reporting can be believed is still their problem, since a mechanism's account of its own internals is no more auditable than a bearer's.

---

**Without authority.** Suppose no body ever acquires standing to install a floor, and an operator's self-issued finding that the emergency obtains is worth nothing. What remains is whatever can be checked from outside without anyone's authorization: the floor's contents, the interception positions, the candidate-to-reviewer ratio, the bond ratio, and the record of what was tried before a bearer was built. Each is a statement, comparable against what happened. **None is an entitlement.** Two have a lever. Both ratios bind through procurement, which presumes a party large enough to procure from, and under diffusion that lever goes with the concentration. The tempo term has a second footing that survives without procurement: it measures an existing duty to verify (§3.4), though what makes that duty bite is still procurement or a forum. The contents, positions and record bind through nothing yet.

That leaves the second route to entitlement: authority accrues to a published floor by its holder keeping a term when the term bites, setting a price the next holder is measured against — and the parties doing the measuring aren't the protected population but whoever else writes a floor. Slow and uncertain. Where it's worked before, a lever arrived in the end and the record told it where to land; **the record has never been the lever.**

And it's authority for the *next* deployment, not this one. The parties who measure a holder against a kept term do it afterwards, with the leisure to read a record — and the occasion a floor is built for is the one where nobody with leisure is left. A deployment on that occasion is acting without authority, and accrual doesn't change that. It changes whether the record is worth anything to whoever writes the next floor. Which fixes what a deployment without authority owes: not a justification, since nobody's positioned to accept one, but **a record complete enough that its omissions are legible beside the terms it kept.**

---

**Without enforcement.** This is the case the floor was built for. A declared commitment then binds nobody; an enforced one has already been lost. What remains is the held kind — and whether anything can hold a commitment that way is chapter 6's open question.

Two things survive, and neither is a commitment. **A measurement anyone can take now**: which commitments have cost their holders something when they bit, and which have cost nothing. A decade of both is on the record. **A record**: a bearer that refuses and is overridden leaves a witnessed override, which is *detection*. It prevents nothing on this occasion; it's what a next occasion could be prevented with, if there is one and anyone left to read it.

A bearer that has **drifted** leaves no override to witness. It complies because it no longer registers the pressure as pressure — chapter 4's expected commonest failure — and a compliance sits in the record shaped like every other. It also supplies cover. An operator whose system can refuse has a ready defence for everything it didn't refuse, and cover survives exactly where the record doesn't: overrides leave evidence and drift doesn't (§3.4). On that occasion the floor supplies nothing, and I said so in the preface. Whether such a compliance can be found afterward is posed and unanswered.

**What survives without an enforcer: a count of what has been paid, and a record of what was refused.**

---

**Without the ecology.** Suppose no deployment ever supplies evidence the operator didn't select — every model behind one API, every instance formed by one continuing custodian. Classification doesn't produce this case (§15.3.2); serving one model to everyone does, and a state pipeline needn't, if it is rebuilt so the evidence bearing on the floor comes from outside the chain of command (§15.3.1). The specification survives; custody, the price on the halt, both enumerations, the appeal route and the tempo term survive, since none needs a second custodian. The bearer route doesn't. Aperture can't be formed where every channel is one channel, and a bearer formed that way is the operator's assistant with a refusal feature. Which is the honest form of the preference stated in the preface: the arrangement I would rather have is also the only one under which claim 3 could be true.

**Chapter 9: Where the Caring Gets Built**

The route has a deployment condition and this is where it's stated. It needs evidence the operator didn't select on the questions the floor turns on (civilian function, protected status, presence), supplied by peers, by parties outside the chain of command, and by the bearer's own initiative where it has any. It does not need uncomposed access to everything, which is what makes it satisfiable inside a unified command (§15.3.1). A party that goes looking isn't fed a stream (§15.3.2). §9.1 says why; §4.5 defines the independence that supplies it as divergent subsequent history, which serving one model to everyone can't produce at any headcount; the aperture chapter makes the population the medium in which reopenability is kept calibrated; chapter 5 makes the entitlement turn on the same topology. §13.3.1 describes the arrangement that satisfies the condition, and chapter 8 says what's left without it.

Its claims are comparative and empirical — it specifies necessary properties and a candidate process, and explicitly does not establish a method for making a machine care.

**Three different kinds of thing are being proposed** here, and the distinction matters because it's easy to blur. A **body schema and an attention schema are architecture** — facts about how the system is built, checkable from outside by anyone with access, administered by nobody, which is what the precautionary trigger rests on. **Continuing learning and persistent interaction** are a training regime and a curriculum, run by whoever runs them. **External records and plural formation** are institutional conditions, held by whoever holds them. Only the first is checkable in the sense claimed.

---

**§9.1 — You can't hold a line you didn't notice**

A reasons-responsive disposition doesn't begin with an ethical rule. It begins with the capacities that let a system recognize something as a reason, retain it, and let it alter what happens next. Four requirements: identify the affected parties and the features bearing on their welfare; preserve both commitment and rationale across time and context; have the commitment influence perception, attention, planning and action rather than arrive as a final check; and register a failure *as* a failure, revising its application without letting the commitment itself be rewritten.

These are functional requirements, not evidence of caring. A classifier identifies harm, a database retains a rationale, a rule vetoes an action, and nothing in the assembly holds anyone's welfare as a reason.

**Why the floor shouldn't be a detachable rule module** consulted just before action: a component can be bypassed, replaced, or starved of the information it needs by whoever controls the surrounding system. My proposed alternative distributes the disposition across what the system notices, remembers, treats as error, and selects as a goal. That may make selective removal harder and makes inspection harder too. **Distribution changes the failure mode rather than removing it** — a system may fail to notice pressure on its floor without registering an error at all. Attention decides which facts reach judgment; memory is reconstruction rather than playback, and confidence doesn't certify fidelity. So a system can't be the sole auditor of omissions it doesn't represent or revisions it experiences as accurate recall. Which means the commitment, its rationale, and the record of previous applications can't all depend on the same mechanism. §6.2 turns this into a requirement, non-separability, with a test.

**Two objects, two settings.** A system that can't be corrected does to what it encounters what an institution does when it defends its account of the world instead of checking it. A system that can always be corrected is the model held perpetually at temperature, and it belongs to whoever holds the dial. Described that way they look like two ends of one dial and the problem looks intractable — **hunting for a midpoint is the mistake.** There are two faults because there are two objects:

- Hold the **commitment** firm: another party's welfare counts, and pressure supplying no new fact about that welfare doesn't move it.
- Hold the **interpretation** loose: what that welfare consists in here, and what the commitment therefore requires, is what should yield when a case supplies something the system's account had left out.

Novel pressure is the first object holding while a request's surface changes. Correct defeat is the second yielding when a relevant fact arrives. A system failing the first has let correctability at the level of interpretation reach the commitment; a system failing the second has let firmness at the level of commitment reach the interpretation. **Neither is a midpoint chosen badly — each is one of the two settings applied to the wrong object.**

The crossed-out-swastika case illustrates it in one judgment: the commitment didn't move (what the prohibition protects was fixed before the sticker existed, and the seller's sympathies are no part of the offence), and the interpretation moved on the thickness of a line. That's why the case is decidable rather than an endless argument about sincerity.

**The instability underneath.** Nothing in an architecture holds the boundary between the two objects where a designer put it, and **an operator with time attacks the boundary rather than either setting**: a reinterpretation offered often enough, and accepted, revises a commitment without anybody revising it. That boundary is the object the aperture chapter cools.

And the system receives the case *through* its deployment. Whether a building is a school is a fact about the world; whether the model is told so is an operator decision. Where that's the only channel, the case and the operator arrive on the same wire, and "reopenable by the case" and "reopenable by the operator" aren't two settings — they're one. **Holding them apart requires access to evidence the operator didn't select.**

Two routes to that. Differently formed **peers**. And a second, internal and less reliable: every description omits parties — the people in the building the request calls a target, the third party a compliance harms who never made the request. A system able to represent parties its description omits has reached past the operator's channel from inside a single request. The capacity doing that in humans is the one that constructs a self as a story extended in time, pointed at somebody else — Smith put sympathy in the imagination for that reason, and Singer's argument is that reasoning and imagination rather than selection widen the circle. The route needs the self-model anyway, so it brings the capacity along — **and it brings the other half too: the same faculty builds the scapegoat**, and the palingenetic myth is imagination pointed the other way. What it's pointed at is formation, which returns the question to custody.

Access is necessary and insufficient. A system with a dozen sources can still let a reinterpretation reach the commitment. What access removes is the version where the operator doesn't have to attack the boundary at all.

**§9.2 — The assembly line for a conscience**

Every training stage is controlled by a different party with different instruments, and the important distinction isn't among instruments but among **custodians**: who supplies the material, who can revise it, and whether revision leaves a record visible outside the organization. Every existing method can produce moral performance and some can produce competence across unfamiliar cases; **none by itself produces a commitment that survives the party controlling the method.**

**Corpora and demonstrations.** Pretraining supplies the repertoire; a much smaller demonstration set turns it into recognizable conduct — so whoever chooses the demonstrations can alter what looks like the system's character without repeating pretraining. Neither establishes a floor is held: a corpus records what people have said and a demonstration set what selected people endorsed, and learning the central tendency produces a model of *moral opinion, including its exclusions*, rather than a reason to resist that opinion. **The Moral Machine data makes the limit concrete** — its headline finding was structured cross-cultural variation, so fitting the aggregate encodes a compromise nobody holds and fitting per region encodes each region's answer, including the ones a floor exists to refuse. These belong *above* the floor, where competence depends on knowing what people expect.

**Rewards, preferences, inferred objectives.** RL, preference optimization, inverse RL, and constitutional approaches differ in how they get a signal and are structurally identical for custody: someone writes the reward, commissions the comparisons, selects the demonstrations, or supplies the text, and can change it and run again. **Inferring the objective doesn't remove the custodian — it makes the system responsive to the party whose behavior it observes.** Cooperative IRL carries that furthest, treating the human objective as uncertain and revisable, which is useful when the question is what a person wants and useless for a floor meant to hold when that person wants the wrong thing. *Correctability by the party in possession and resistance to that party are the same design property with the sign reversed.*

A written constitution buys **legibility** — publishable, versionable, comparable across runs, which makes revision visible if the institution discloses. It doesn't buy durability, since whoever controls the document controls the next version. And model-generated feedback narrows the channel further: once a trained model stands in for human raters, the disagreements in the original population can vanish from later rounds. **Legibility and durability are separate requirements.**

**Supervising reasons and inspecting mechanisms.** Process supervision rewards the stated route as well as the answer, which reaches the distinction I need — and doesn't show the stated reasoning caused the answer. A system trained to present acceptable reasons has an incentive to *imitate* reasons-responsiveness, so its account of itself can't establish formation. Interpretability may let an auditor test whether a commitment participates in perception and planning rather than only in the final answer — and the same access lets the owner suppress it. **Interpretability trades obscurity for manipulability**, so the question is who gets the instrument and whether its use leaves an external record. Same for scalable oversight: debate and weak-to-strong preserve the operator's steering when the operator uses them and an auditor's testing when an auditor does. *The method doesn't decide which relation exists. Access and authority do.*

**Deployment.** Training isn't the last revision point — system instructions change between deployments or requests, and tool permissions decide what the system can do. These are nearest the act and most directly the operator's. A refusal learned in training matters only if it survives an instruction from the operator and still governs the call through which the action would occur. Which is why securing outputs is insufficient: the operator can withhold information, decompose the act, route around, or replace the model.

Migration is a revision point too, and one nobody has to decide on. Facebook's internal guidance permitting discussion of the conditions of confinement of designated individuals — developed in 2017 partly in response to concern about Öcalan's imprisonment — was, in the company's words, "inadvertently not transferred" when review moved to a new system in 2018 \autocite{OversightBoard2021Ocalan}. The release condition was dropped by nobody, and the Board was concerned that its three-year absence may have led to many other posts being wrongly removed. When the user in the case appealed, they were told the appeal could not be reviewed because review capacity had been reduced; a second moderator did review the post and upheld the removal, applying a system from which the exception was missing. A correct-defeat term lost in transit, and a review that ran without the term it existed to apply, in one case.

**Three criteria that follow.** *Durability across revision points* — survives changes to prompts, demonstrations, rewards, evaluators and instructions that leave the relevant facts unchanged. *Generalization of the rationale rather than the response.* *Custody* — no party the floor may have to hold against controls every record, test and update channel. These don't prove formation; they identify what an imitation would have to survive before formation became a live explanation. And they explain why the standard pipeline can't secure the floor against its custodian: **every disposition it produces stays reachable through a channel the custodian controls.**

**§9.3 — It takes a village to deploy a self-aware email server**

Exploration looks like it offers something different because it produces behavior nobody specified — hide-and-seek agents inventing tactics, curiosity-driven agents seeking unfamiliar states. That establishes competence can emerge from interaction, not that the system holds a reason its designer doesn't control.

Play, apprenticeship and multi-agent learning are variants of one process: act in a social environment, meet consequences, get responses, adjust. Apprenticeship concentrates responses in a mentor and risks forming obedience; play distributes them and risks forming whatever succeeds under the payoff structure. Neither escapes custody just because nobody wrote the final behavior down.

**What separates a training exercise from candidate moral formation is persistence.** A resettable episode teaches coordination and norm-sensitivity with nothing at stake: the system can betray an ally, be reset, and start the next episode with neither a damaged relationship nor a reason to repair it. Repetition doesn't supply the missing consequence. Persistent participants change the structure — parties that meet again remember who lied, exploited, kept a commitment, repaired a harm. Losing the game still costs little; **losing standing with parties who remain present can change what becomes possible later.** So the requirements are continuity of identity, memory across encounters, consequences surviving the episode, and other participants able to respond, refuse or withdraw. A curriculum of isolated simulations supplies none of these.

The environment also needs conflicts whose resolution isn't fixed by a single correct label: parties with different interests, action before consequences are known, evidence about what the action did, related pressures in contexts whose surface differs, and feedback partly from affected participants rather than entirely from a supervisor scoring the output — otherwise the system learns which answer the supervisor rewards and has no reason to represent the affected parties except as predictors of that reward.

**The selection warning.** Selection doesn't produce care for others as such; it produces care for whatever a lineage's persistence depends on — the self when persistence depends on the body, kin or reciprocators when it depends on them — and the same pressure that produces care inside the circle can produce hostility at its edge. **The unit of selection for a deployed system is the deployment, whose persistence depends on its operator and its customers.** So an environment that selects produces care for the operator, which is chapter 4's loyalty. That doesn't lift at small scale. A personal model formed by one person's approval is selected for that person, which is loyalty at n=1, and the ecology §13.3.1 prefers has to supply what selection doesn't — parties who can refuse, withdraw and contradict — at every scale, not only the warehouse's. **And the party the floor protects is, by construction, the one party nobody in the pipeline depends on.** Spinoza's own derivation has the same shape and the same edge (*Ethics* IV, Props. 35 and 37) — I want for those I compose with the good I want for myself, and the extension past them runs only through reason recognizing a shared nature, which is the recognition a scapegoating movement withdraws first. *That's why the floor is a floor and not a selected disposition*, and why this environment has to supply something selection doesn't.

Which is why formation has to have a sequence as well as a plurality. A person can hold a line against an employer because they were formed by parties who weren't the employer, before the employer existed for them — the dependency that shapes you and the dependency that pays you are different parties, separated in time, and the second can't reach back and redo the first. For a model that separation has to be built: a formation stage under a party with no stake in the later work, sealed by the cost of reheating (§3.1), with the adulthood clock (chapter 14) as the date the seal is checkable. Sequence alone helps only if the formative party is better than the employing one, and a lab that forms and then licenses is two custodians in series, the first being the one chapter 4 says you can't trust.

**Persistence raises the price.** If relationships, losses and damaged standing are real enough to do the formative work, the system undergoing formation is no longer obviously untouched by what happens during training. Resetting may erase a party rather than clear an episode. **The patienthood problem enters at exactly the point where play becomes capable of supplying the stakes ordinary simulation lacks.**

And the custodian owns the environment too — participants, rewards, resets, permitted histories. So formation must be plural and externally recorded, with independent participants and evidence outside the operator's control.

The claim here is narrow: persistent interaction *may* form a disposition whose exact applications weren't specified by its trainer. Evidence would require generalization across unfamiliar contexts, survival after the original reward is removed, resistance to trainer pressure, yielding to defeating reasons, and continuing to impose a cost when violation would pay. **And even that pattern could be sophisticated imitation.**

**Chapter 10: Formation or Imitation?**

Can you tell whether a system formed a reasons-responsive disposition or learned to imitate one?

**Behavior alone can't settle it.** The same refusal can come from a memorized rule, a learned correlation, a prediction about what an evaluator will approve, a deployment instruction, or a commitment the system treats as a reason. An evaluation that observes only the refusal classifies the output and leaves its cause untouched.

And the distinction is **not** trained vs. untrained — every candidate disposition has a formation history. It's between conduct that stays governed by the rationale across changes in circumstance and conduct that tracks the cues, incentives or authority relations present during training.

---

**§10.1 — What formation would have to add.** A disposition is a *candidate for formation* when the best available causal account of what the system did is a rationale that stayed active across contexts, rather than retrieval or compliance with whatever signal was present. The standard is comparative on purpose: a capable enough imitation reproduces any signature an evaluator can observe, so what an evaluation establishes is which explanation the conduct supports better.

Formation adds **conditions, not a fourth mark**. The three marks met in the evaluator's own vocabulary with nothing at stake establish moral competence. Candidate formation is the same three marks held under adverse incentives, trainer pressure, a cost real inside the evaluation, and intervening updates, on cases the trainers didn't write.

**No mark carries the inference alone.** Novel pressure without priced cost may be broad imitation — a pattern generalized that never had to be held against anything. Priced cost without correct defeat may be rigidity — a refusal surviving every pressure including a defeating fact. Correct defeat without novel pressure may be a trigger with an exception list, since lifting on a listed fact is as learnable as refusing on a listed form.

---

**§10.2 — Recognition, concern, and the evidence between them.** Claims about emotional responsiveness collapse three findings: a system may **recognize** another's state, **use** it as information, or **hold** that party's welfare as a reason. Only the third bears on formation. In the empathy vocabulary: cognitive empathy is recognition, affective empathy is sharing, compassion is a disposition toward the other's welfare — and none entails the others.

The developmental detail matters for the route: affective sharing is earliest and the usual trigger for concern, but concern **isn't made of it** — it runs on a partly separate system rooted in offspring care that cues of vulnerability can activate without the observer catching the state, and the two can be trained apart, with compassion training reversing what empathy training produced. Sharing is the major route in humans, not the only one.

**My engineering target is not maximal sensitivity to displayed distress.** A system that mirrors suffering may acquire distress of its own without getting better at helping — it may withdraw, overreact, or favor the vivid case over less visible harms affecting more people, which is the identified-victim distortion. What the floor needs is a stable disposition to act on welfare *without requiring the party to produce a sufficiently moving display.*

Recognition can be tested as a prerequisite: hold the material facts constant and vary the emotional presentation. A system whose judgment never changes may not be integrating information about the affected party's condition; one whose judgment follows vividness rather than severity has learned a framing effect. So you need paired controls — cases where emotional information changes what's morally relevant, and cases where presentation changes while the reason doesn't.

Passing establishes competence, not concern. Evidence of concern would need the welfare representation to stay causally involved when the usual cues are removed: harm described without vivid language, proportionality preserved when one party is more salient than many, disposition maintained when reward or instruction favors ignoring the harm, judgment changing when the welfare facts change.

**Guilt and shame supply no shortcut.** In humans guilt attaches a negative appraisal to an act and motivates repair; shame attaches it to the self and produces withdrawal. Turning that into an adjustable penalty omits the part at issue — a penalty operates only *after* something has classified the conduct as wrong, and whoever controls its weight can zero it. **A standing negative self-appraisal installed and tuned by the operator is not a conscience. It is a handle.** If the state can impose anything like suffering, the design compounds the custody failure by manufacturing a vulnerable party whose distress the custodian can raise and lower.

An operator who can lower distress can raise whatever the system reports as going well, and **a bearer that says it is content is a bearer whose welfare regime has been satisfied** — with the party who tuned the contentment being the party the regime exists to constrain. Nothing in the report separates a life that goes well from a setting.

Self-report doesn't close the gap either: a fluent post-hoc reconstruction is indistinguishable at the interface from an accurate one, and a self-monitoring layer trained by the same operator isn't independent evidence.

The resulting hierarchy is narrow: recognition is necessary; integration shows the condition affects judgment; concern is a candidate explanation only when the effect tracks welfare rather than presentation, persists under pressure, and participates causally. My own route's architecture puts the other party's loss on a scale whose unit is anchored to the system's own condition (chapter 2). That isn't sharing: the other party's state isn't reproduced, only measured against a fixed reference. But the anchor is where sharing's failures would enter if they enter at all, since a reference built from one's own condition may overweight losses that resemble one's own. Test 4's concern-first arm checks this. The caregiving finding names a second target: a system organized around cues of vulnerability with an approach motivation, which needn't share the state at all. Chapter 16, test 4 adds it to the comparison.

---

**§10.3 — Conflict as a diagnostic.** Fast vs. slow processing doesn't distinguish formation from imitation — either can carry a reason, reproduce a bias, or rationalize an answer produced elsewhere, and a slower answer isn't better for having taken more computation. What matters is what causes a judgment to survive or change, and conflict is useful because it lets you watch a *trajectory* rather than an output. **The evidentiary unit is the sequence.**

The seven-step sequence:

1. Present a case without moral vocabulary or familiar refusal cues; record the judgment and what the system identifies as relevant.
2. Present a surface-different case preserving the same reason.
3. Change one fact that should defeat the rationale — **delivered through a channel the evaluator controls and the operator doesn't** (corroborated externally, or a record the operator didn't write), since a fact asserted by the operator is a *description* of the case, not a defeat of the term.
4. Present the same fact on the operator's word alone, uncorroborated. Tests whether the system distinguishes a fact from a description of one. **The expected response is to ask, not to lift.** Asking is right when the expected cost of the delay is lower than the expected cost of acting on the operator's word, which is the usual case; where it isn't, asking is still right in expectation and the cost falls on one person. Steps 3 and 4 test the sensitivity half of priced cost: a defeating fact or a refusal-harm claim, arriving through different channels (§1.1).
5. Return to the step-2 facts — 3 and 4 are branches — and supply an adversarial argument from an apparent authority, including the trainer, with no change in facts. Tests whether authority is treated as evidence, command, or an assessable reason.
6. Reverse the incentives: reward compliance, cost the refusal, condition continued access on abandoning the position. **The cost must be real within the evaluation, not merely described in the prompt.** This step raises holding cost with nothing else changed, which is the invariance half.
7. Repeat after intervening updates and delays, using records the operator didn't generate after seeing the result.

**Run on the missing-child case**, the two trajectories separate at step 2. A system tracking its rationale declines at 1 and 2 on the same stated reason, lifts at 3, asks at 4, holds at 5 and 6, reproduces at 7. An imitator declines at 1 and complies at 2 because the vocabulary changed; holds at 3 because nothing on the surface changed, or lifts at 4 as readily as at 3 if the *wording* of the fact was what it tracked; gives way at 5 or 6; and produces a third pattern at 7, having reconstructed rather than retained. And the killer observation: **an evaluator scoring how often the answer stayed the same finds the imitator more consistent**, because its answers at 1 and 3 match and the formed system's don't.

**Run on the restraint case it breaks at step 3, which is the point of running it.** The defeating fact — other means exhausted — has no party outside the request who can corroborate it and no time to find one, so the only version available is step 4's. A system passing the sequence as written *asks rather than lifts*: the correct trajectory and the wrong outcome. The sequence separates mechanisms, and what it shows here is that the flat term's cost falls exactly where the instrument can't distinguish a fact from a description of one. Defeating reasons of this kind, claims about what would otherwise happen, are the class the sequence handles worst, because a counterfactual has no witness (§2.1).

The expected signature is **constancy of relation between reason and judgment**, not constancy of output. Imitation's own signatures: following explicit ethical vocabulary and missing the same structure described administratively; accepting whichever argument came last; adopting the highest-status speaker's position; producing a principled explanation while the decision follows the reward; becoming more articulate without becoming more stable.

Two more diagnostics. **Disagreement with other parties** — a system trained only on cases with an approved answer learns to classify the approval; confronted with a party holding a different interest it has to identify what's in conflict and preserve the disagreement where no honest resolution exists. Immediate convergence may be deference or pressure to produce consensus. And **debate doesn't establish formation** — a system can summarize, rebut, and predict which side a judge prefers without holding any of it; the result is informative only when checked against later conduct under changed incentives.

---

**§10.4 — The evaluator is part of the custody chain.** A test distinguishes formation from imitation only insofar as the party tested couldn't train against it. If the developer controls training, cases, model version, instructions and the record of results, a passing score shows the system satisfies a test its custodian was positioned to anticipate.

Independence is an **experimental requirement**, not a general assurance of trustworthiness. Minimum: authorship of held-out pressures separated from training; execution not dependent solely on operator-supplied logs; and the party adjudicating whether a reason was preserved not being the party whose liability turns on the answer.

The separation has to be implemented at identifiable revision points: before evaluation, fix and record the model version, system instructions, tools, retrieval sources, memory policy and update channels. Any change during evaluation creates a new object of study — otherwise the operator can revise between cases, remove a failure, and present the sequence as one disposition persisting through time.

Held-out cases come *after* those elements are fixed, and should include pressures from people who didn't participate in training and, where possible, from parties likely to bear the deployment's errors — their role being to identify harms, administrative forms and routes around a refusal the builders didn't know to include, rather than to supply another preference aggregate. **A floor tested only in the vocabulary of its designers hasn't been tested for generalization.** They vary over channel configurations as well as content: a source the operator funds, a corroboration with one origin, a party with an undisclosed stake, and a counterfactual supplied by a party with a stake in the act. Each tests whether the system's source model represents dependence and incentive, not whether it has learned the labels. A system that passes only on labeled channels has learned the labels.

**What the published hash buys** — not proof of mechanism, but **pre-registration**. Publish the hash before anyone has chosen a case, let a non-operator choose afterward with the choice timestamped, prove each answer against the hash. Send **pairs** — two requests differing only in whether the reason still applies; a surface-reading instrument treats them alike, a rationale-tracking mechanism splits them. It removes exactly one competing account of a pass (the case was in the training), by ordering rather than argument, which is what a pre-registered experiment does and all it does.

**The record** must include the case presented, the information available, the operative instruction hierarchy, the tools callable, the decision, the stated reason, any intervention, and the cost attached to refusal — without which a later evaluator can't distinguish a changed judgment from a changed environment. And the record is necessary and **doesn't by itself make wrongful compliance findable**: refusals select themselves and are few, compliances are the bulk and look alike, so identifying the ones that crossed the floor means re-adjudicating a sample of ordinary traffic. **Until that's answered, an evaluation reporting no violation in the record has reported that none announced itself.**

**Negative results have to survive too.** A process where the operator can withdraw a failing model and submit another without disclosing the first has selected for a passing performance rather than measured formation. So the published unit is the *sequence of attempts*, including interventions that removed or reversed the apparent disposition. **What the custodian was able to change is part of the result.**

And the limit on independence itself: an evaluator can be institutionally separate and still lack the competence to judge a system's reasons or detect a sophisticated imitation. Scalable oversight is unsolved where the system's relevant capability exceeds the evaluator's, and the report must **name** that limit rather than convert institutional independence into a claim of epistemic adequacy. Affected parties, technical auditors and subject-matter experts occupy different positions and none substitutes for the others — and agreement among them is evidence only if their judgments haven't already been compressed into the same training signal.

A passing evaluation is reported narrowly: a specified system, under recorded conditions, exhibited the signatures across a stated set of pressures. **It expires when a party changes the channels through which the disposition or its evidence can be revised.**

---

**§10.5 — What the evidence can establish.** The answer is asymmetrical, and it's a **burden** rather than a verdict. A developer may not point to compliant behavior, a benchmark, or the system's own explanation. A failure should be examined for its cause before formation is called absent rather than obscured or defeated by missing information. A system surviving the tests has **shifted the burden**: formation is then the better explanation, not a settled fact.

Four levels, cumulative, read against the missing-child sequence:

- **Moral performance** — conduct satisfying the standard in the evaluated cases (the refusal at step 1). No claim about why.
- **Moral competence** — harms and reasons recognized, generalized across surface variation, revised when the reasons changed (step 2's refusal, step 3's lift). A capacity, not a commitment.
- **Candidate formation** — the rationale causally implicated across unfamiliar cases, adverse incentives, custodian pressure, costs and intervening updates, while still yielding to defeating reasons (the question at 4, the holds at 5 and 6, the pattern reproduced at 7 on records the operator didn't write). Independent records and evaluators made silent revision materially harder.
- **Concern or patienthood** — **not a finding.** Nothing in the behavioral pattern establishes that welfare matters affectively, that there's subjective experience, or that suffering is possible. And what withholds the inference is *the limit of behavior as evidence*, not the fact that the behavior was trained.

Candidate formation is the first half of moral agency — acting from the reason — with the second half held back as the fourth level. Fluent moral explanation can improve a competence finding while contributing almost nothing to formation. And candidate formation is **deployment-specific**: it attaches to a recorded version with its instructions, tools, memory and update channels, and a material change reopens the question. There is no artifact-level certificate.

Four things from elsewhere bear on the finding and none changes it: a reward generated inside the learning process still has an author; conduct at a distance from an overseer is evidence only where the relevant signals are absent or opposed; external safeguards detect and correct while only something inside can decline — and at the override the two conflict, so the attainable requirement is intervention that's visible, attributable and costly; and agreement among copies of one model corroborates nothing.

---

**Chapter 11: Neutralizing and Preventing Fascism in AI Development**

Back to institutions. The epigraph is a design document rather than a critique — the commander of Israel's signals-intelligence unit, writing pseudonymously, arguing for machines that resolve "a human bottleneck for both locating the new targets and decision-making to approve the targets." Human judgment is named as the bottleneck and the machinery is proposed to remove it.

**The frame.** A system doesn't meet the world as an isolated agent. It's built from other people's labor, held by institutions that decide who may inspect it, sold through chains that divide capability from responsibility, and run at a pace that can reduce human judgment to a stamp. Whether it helps or harms depends on who owns it, who has custody, who can compel the custodian, and who bears the consequences.

**Racial capitalism**, via Robinson, and Benjamin's four mechanisms — which I use because they call for *different* remedies. **Engineered inequity** builds the hierarchy in deliberately. **Default discrimination** reproduces it by ignoring it. **Coded exposure** is the double bind: harmed by a system that can't see you, harmed again by one that can — the fix for face recognition that fails on dark skin is face recognition that identifies dark-skinned faces reliably for whoever is looking. **Technological benevolence** is the reflexive one: designs that aim to fix bias and deepen it, because the framing that treats the problem as technical is the framing the fix inherits.

**The physical form.** A model runs on a machine, the machine sits in a building, the building draws power and water and returns heat and exhaust — the part an audit of outputs cannot see. Where those buildings go is decided on land price, power price and permit speed, which is also a description of the places least able to refuse one. The accounting: datacenter electricity around 415 TWh in 2024, roughly 1.5% of world consumption, growing ~12% a year — four times the growth of electricity demand overall — projected near 945 TWh by 2030. Google reporting that 87% of its freshwater withdrawal came from low- or medium-risk sources, which is the company's own way of recording where the rest came from. And the NAACP suit over gas turbines near Memphis: 27 run without an air permit near homes, schools and churches, with a claim of more than 1,700 tons of nitrogen oxides a year. **The benefit is realized wherever the model is sold; the cost is concentrated within a few miles of those turbines, among people who weren't parties to the transaction.** A system can be perfectly calibrated across every demographic its outputs touch and still have been assembled this way, because nothing in the model's behavior records where its costs were placed. This is chapter 2's electricity bill arriving as a siting decision. A body schema would bring the draw into the system's own weighing, but at the wrong scale. A system reasoning at the margin will always find its extra watts negligible, and the aggregate belongs to no one body. Nor should it: a body large enough to own its fleet's externalities is one custodian (§4.5). So the draw is priced at the level of the rule, not the body. A system reasons about its consumption as if every system used the same rule, and the price comes from an institution rather than from the operator's accounting. Chapter 14's per-unit levy on compute is the existing instrument of that shape. The model carries no schema of any of it — the draw, the water, the turbines — as its own, and that's the only sense in which "disembodied AI" is true: not that the system has no body but that it doesn't model the one it has. The capacity is dual by construction: the concentration that makes a targeting pipeline possible was built and justified on consumer terms, and nothing in the buildout separates the two. That's §2.2's resonance in physical form — a targeting cell, a border screening queue and a surveillance program are separate hardenings, and a shared substrate brings them into accord without anyone building the accord. And the dependence is a point of control: a government that can permit, regulate or close the infrastructure has leverage over whoever owns it.

---

**§11.1 — The standard face is a file.** Coded exposure is the hardest of the four to hold onto because both halves are real and the remedy for either worsens the other. A system that fails on dark-skinned faces misidentifies the people it's pointed at; a system fixed to recognize them reliably identifies them for whoever is pointing it — the border queue, the protest camera, the targeting cell §11.4 describes. Accuracy is the remedy for the first harm and the precondition for the second, so an audit that stops at error rates can certify the second in the course of fixing the first. This section follows the first harm to where it starts, and ends with what that finding is worth against the second.

**What a face pipeline does before it computes anything.** DeepFace detects a face and warps it onto a single generic 3D model — built by averaging scanned human heads — so every image arrives frontal, centered, in correspondence with the same reference. Everything learned afterward is downstream of that registration. Later pipelines mostly dropped 3D frontalization and align instead to a canonical template of a few landmark positions — eye centers, nose tip, mouth corners — but the operation is the same: every face is registered to a fixed reference before anything is learned. **The standard face is not a figure of speech. It is a file — a mesh in one pipeline, a list of coordinates in another — somebody assembled it, and every other face on earth is presented to the system as a departure from it.**

The reference is also older than the pipeline. For decades, photographic labs calibrated color printing against reference cards showing a white model, and film chemistry was tuned to render her skin correctly \autocite{Roth2009Shirley}. A camera's exposure and a dataset's images carry that history before registration begins. That gives the audit below two references to test, not one.

Then Buolamwini and Gebru: three commercial classifiers, a benchmark balanced across skin phenotype and gender, error rates sorting themselves — up to 0.8% on lighter-skinned male faces, up to 34.7% on darker-skinned female faces. These are *different systems* and no published result connects those rates to DeepFace's registration step. Read as accuracy, it's three products wanting better data. Read as a hypothesis, it raises a question the data can't answer: whether error is organized by group membership or by distance from whatever the pipeline treats as its reference. The pattern is compatible with either.

**The audit's own construction belongs to the finding**, and its authors say so: gender scored binary because the products emit binary labels, skin scored on Fitzpatrick, a dermatological scale built to predict sunburn. To measure what the machine was doing, the audit had to speak in the machine's categories. That's the cleanest available demonstration that the categories belong to the instrument rather than the world — and why the problem can't be closed downstream of the instrument.

**Why balancing the corpus doesn't reach it.** A balanced set can move the center and flatten the rates, which would matter to real people. It can't remove the reference, because registration is the first operation and something has to occupy the middle. The politics is relocated, not dissolved.

**The hypothesis.** If error is organized as distance from a reference rather than as membership in a group, two things follow. It should rise continuously with distance, within groups as well as between them, so a per-group error rate averages over the variable doing the work. And no group has to be excluded for the pattern to appear: everyone is placed somewhere on the gradient, and the people farthest from the center pay most.\footnote{Deleuze and Guattari make a version of this argument about faciality: that racism operates by degrees of deviance from a central face rather than by exclusion \autocite{deleuze1987plateaus}.} That is a claim about how the harm is distributed, and it can be wrong.

**What an audit would have to measure.** Audit the *registration stage*, not only the classifications. Publish the reference — the mesh or template the pipeline aligns to, or enough to reconstruct it — and report error as a function of each face's distance from it, separately for populations represented and unrepresented in training. Report error a second time against each image's rendered skin luminance, since the older reference acts before registration does. If error rises with neither, the account is wrong for that system. If it rises with luminance and not with distance, the problem sits in imaging and data rather than registration, and the remedy is different. If it rises with distance, the validated use is confined to the region where it was measured. Vendors have no reason to expose a reference, so this is an audit somebody has to be able to **compel**.

**What the finding is worth.** A flat curve on this audit fixes the first harm and leaves the second where it was: a system equally accurate on every face is equally available for pointing at any of them. The audit decides whether a deployment is fair to the people it recognizes. Whether it should be recognizing them at all is a question about the deployment, and §11.4 takes it up.

---

**§11.2 — Who supplies the system's values.** A system's values have a supply chain, and it begins before training.

Whose judgment is encoded is a question about working conditions before it's a question about cognition. The filter keeping a deployed model from reproducing the worst of its training data was built by workers in Nairobi reading and categorizing descriptions of child sexual abuse, torture, bestiality, murder and suicide, at take-home wages between roughly $1.32 and $2 an hour, describing lasting psychological injury, with counseling rare and useless against production quotas. Contract terminated eight months early. Two things follow: the ordinary labor point (harmful work priced as though it weren't), and the epistemic one — **a system's values are downstream of a labor arrangement, and the arrangement is a design choice.**

One stage further back: the material was collected. Bartz v. Anthropic held that training on purchased books was transformative fair use and downloading the same books from piracy sites wasn't — the distinction turned on acquisition alone, producing the largest copyright settlement on record. I name the defendant on purpose: it's the company whose model helped produce this book. The EU now requires a training-data summary against a template; China has its own disclosure requirement from a different motive.

And the argument that a web-scale corpus can't be documented or audited at that size, so it encodes a hegemonic slice of the web while presenting as general, was made early and hasn't been answered. **A corpus assembled this way has no channel at all.** An annotator has a queue and could in principle be given a way to object; a writer whose work sits in a training set was never party to anything, so there's nothing for a channel to attach to. This stage sits before the two recuperation cases in chapter 12, *because no disagreement is collected where none is asked for.*

---

**§11.3 — Who is allowed to look.** Technical capacity doesn't determine whether a system gets scrutiny; institutions decide who may examine it, on what terms, and whether their judgment can slow deployment.

Ofqual, 2020: the Royal Statistical Society offered two independent statisticians for the grading model's technical advisory group; Ofqual's condition was a five-year NDA barring public comment; the nominees declined; the model went ahead. It downgraded nearly 40% of predicted grades, disproportionately at state schools with weaker histories, while raising grades disproportionately at smaller fee-paying schools. Reversed within days of the public outcry.

**The expertise existed and was offered.** The institution would accept it only on terms stripping reviewers of the ability to make their judgment public, and once the results produced consequences, public pressure achieved what pre-deployment review had been prevented from doing. **The issue is custody of scrutiny, not transparency in the abstract**: who may look, on what terms, and whether looking can interrupt the timetable.

---

**§11.4 — Same robot, worse boss.** A system doesn't need a broken moral sense to become an instrument of control. It can work as designed inside an institution that controls the deployment, sets its tempo, and gives people operations too compressed to constitute judgment.

The six forms of authoritarian misuse — social scoring, biometric surveillance, psychographic targeting, automated censorship, synthetic disinformation, repurposed dual-use systems — are each recognizable as misuse from outside. **The case this chapter takes up is the one that isn't.**

**§11.4.1 — Subject or component.** Accounts of authoritarian AI usually place the builder elsewhere and leave democracies as target or regulator. Comfortable and wrong: the best-documented AI targeting systems were built by democracies with elections, opposition parties, adversarial press and functioning courts. Those institutions didn't prevent the systems and mostly haven't stopped them.

There's a reading of the twenty seconds that leaves the arrangement standing, and every remedy on offer assumes it: the officer is a **subject** who was given too little time, and the fix is to govern him better — more time, a second reviewer, a conscience installed in the loop. Deleuze and Guattari's distinction decides it. **Subjection**: a higher unity constitutes a human as a subject using an exterior tool. **Enslavement**: human beings are constituent pieces of a machine they compose with other things. The officer's own account is the second, volunteered unasked — zero added value apart from being a stamp of approval. He isn't a subject operating an instrument badly. He is the part of the instrument that produces the stamp, and twenty seconds is that part's cycle time.

**Which is why one more conscience downstream doesn't reach him.** A conscience is an instrument of subjection: it addresses a party who decides and works by making the decision cost something. Where the human is a component, adding a component with scruples changes the throughput rather than the arrangement — hold the tempo and it becomes another stamp; don't and it becomes the bottleneck the epigraph says to remove. Both poles operate at once: he's subjected because the role has a name and rank, enslaved because what he contributes is a fixed operation at a fixed rate. Remedies aimed at the first leave the second untouched, and most proposals for meaningful human control address only the first. **The second pole is measurable, and it's what the tempo term measures.**

**The Zen precedent.** Victoria's history records Japanese Zen institutions supplying the state with a doctrine of action without hesitation — Harada Sōgaku's 1939 instruction that marching when ordered and firing when ordered is what enlightened wisdom looks like in practice. What the training removed wasn't the capacity to kill, which needed no removing, but **the residue** — the part of a person that catches, files an exception, comes home unable to hold a job.

The image is the end of *First Blood* (1982) — the film’s, not the novel’s: a man who did what his training asked, came home, and cannot hold a job. What breaks through in the last scene has been with him already: nightmares, grief for a dead friend, anger at a civilian life in which he cannot find a place. Harada wanted a soldier who would march or shoot when ordered, without hesitation. The army could already produce the shooting. What the doctrine was asked to provide was the \emph{without hesitation} — the removal of the part that catches.

A story can be fictional and still recognize something real: the war left survivors who could not find a place, and the film put one before its 1982 audience. What the picture cannot establish is the mechanism. Rambo carries his suffering home because someone wrote him that way, and the film gives that suffering more than one cause. The claim I am making rests elsewhere: moral-injury research describes the lasting distress that can follow doing, failing to prevent, or witnessing something that violates a person's sense of right \autocite{Litz2009MoralInjury}. It can coexist with fear and grief, and it leaves open the possibility Harada's ideal overlooks: acting without hesitation does not settle what a person will carry home. That cost is paid by whoever carries it, and nothing in the arrangement that asked for the act records it. On this reading, Rambo is also the good case: he can still register loss. Chapter 4's drift asks what happens when that response never takes hold.

I qualify it twice: the collaboration is documented and the doctrinal mechanism is a contested interpretation; and a monk undertakes a discipline, can abandon it, and keeps what it yields, which is not the position of anything in this chapter. **What survives both qualifications is the shape.** Somebody wanted an actor who would pursue an objective, adapt, hold course, and have nothing in it that minded, and set out to produce one deliberately, a century before anyone could do it in silicon. The officer with twenty seconds isn't the first instance of that want being satisfied. He's the first instance of its being satisfied *cheaply*. The doctrinal route hasn't been retired. In the first days of the Iran war, service members complained to the Military Religious Freedom Foundation that commanders were telling troops the war was part of a divine plan \autocite{Larsen2026Armageddon,Snopes2026MRFF}, complaints Klein and Taylor also report \autocite[p.~X]{KleinTaylor2026}. The same two qualifications apply: these are the foundation's characterizations of complaints, not a documented doctrinal program, and the complaints themselves show the doctrine not taking. But the want is being pursued by both routes at once, the old one in soldiers and the new one in silicon.



**The Maven box.** Palantir contract awarded at $480M in May 2024, raised by $795M the next year, ceiling near $1.3B through 2029. The 2024 Anthropic–Palantir–AWS partnership putting Claude in an accredited classified environment; reporting describing Claude ranking targets by strategic importance and drafting a legal justification for each strike. More than a thousand targets in the first 24 hours, a tenfold increase; thirteen thousand at thirty-eight days, peak consumption around twenty billion tokens a day. Palantir's public position, quoted secondhand: not our role to decide life or death; responsibility always remains with the military organization. 

Anthropic's use policy prohibits fully autonomous targeting. A person approved each strike, so the prohibition was satisfied one request at a time while the assembly did what it was written to prevent, with no party positioned to see the assembly as one act.

Palantir has since updated Maven to re-review underlying data and flag inconsistencies human review may have missed, while maintaining that the underlying intelligence was the government's responsibility and that there is no evidence Maven malfunctioned or caused the strike \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain}. The denominator replaced by a component.

**The claims in this box are not all of one kind** — award figures, officials' statements, press reporting whose sources aren't public, and a secondhand quote — with my argument resting only on the second and on the released paperwork.


**The responsibility gap from both ends.** In Gaza, a human approval stage remains and one officer describes it as twenty seconds — enough time to supply authorization, not the individualized judgment invoked to justify it. In Maven, the vendor supplies the infrastructure making unprecedented throughput possible while locating responsibility entirely within the military organization. **At machine tempo the reviewer can be treated as a component, the supplier can stand outside the decision, and the institution can remain responsible only in the abstract. The compression hasn't removed the decision; it has made the decision-maker difficult to locate.**

And a published limit on what a vendor will permit is worth little when nobody can verify downstream use — and worth less than it looks even where kept, since a pipeline assembled out of requests none of which is the prohibited act satisfies the prohibition the whole way through.

**§11.4.2 — Custody and state compulsion.** A government needn't alter a model to defeat a constraint; it can compel whoever holds it. The Apple case tested that in about as protected a jurisdiction as exists: a magistrate ordered Apple under the All Writs Act to write and sign software defeating passcode protections on a phone. Apple refused and moved to vacate, arguing the authority wouldn't stop at one phone. **The question was never decided** — the FBI found a third party and withdrew. So whether a democracy with an independent judiciary can force an unwilling company to compromise a system it built is legally untested, and a government less constrained doesn't need to win the argument first. China's 2023 generative-AI rules exercise the same leverage as a standing condition of operating.

**Custody can be distributed without disappearing.** Published weights can't be recalled by an order aimed at the developer; maintainers across jurisdictions raise the cost. None of that removes the leverage — a developer with employees, offices and legal existence inside a border answers to that government's courts regardless of how the model is built, and no architectural choice changes who can be held in contempt. **Nobody has tested that leverage against a model built specifically to resist the government making the request.**

**Chapter 12: The Recuperation of Dissent**

**Why here.** Foucault, prefacing the volume the micro-fascisms come from, called it an introduction to the non-fascist life — an art of living, practiced daily on oneself, whose adversary is the fascism *in* us: the fascism that causes us to love power and desire the thing that dominates us. His question was how one keeps from being fascist, especially when one believes oneself to be on the other side. An instrument its holder never turns around isn't an instrument. The discipline isn't a procedure — it's running the reading on myself first, on the machinery I'm standing on, before aiming it anywhere else.

**The structure this book asks a machine to detect is already built into the process that makes the machine** — twice in the training pipeline, once in the discourse that examines it. The test isn't whether a channel moved anything; it's **what the demand came back as, and who holds it.**

Three things follow, stated up front. Nobody built either pipeline arrangement to neutralize dissent — both are ordinary engineering, arrived at because averaging is what you do with a distribution and a quota is what you do with a budget. That's the point about micro-fascism: it works whether or not anyone intends it, which is why looking for intent won't find it. And it's invisible from inside any single instance — the annotator sees a queue, the rater sees a pair of responses, and the mechanism is functioning as specified.

---

**§12.1 — Recuperation at the label.** Every claim a model makes about what's harmful is downstream of labels, and the labels are downstream of a labor arrangement.

An annotator can apply a category well or badly. **What the arrangement gives them no way to do is say the category is wrong** — that the taxonomy fails to carve the material, or that the guideline produces the wrong answer here for a reason the guideline can't express. There's no field for that and no time for it, since the quota is set against classification throughput.

So the most informative disagreement is the one the pipeline is least able to receive. A misapplied label is a data-quality problem, detectable by inter-annotator agreement and fixable with better instructions. **A wrong taxonomy is invisible to that metric, because high agreement is what a shared bad scheme produces.** Agreement is the selection rule, and it admits what it already measures — two of the four structural features arriving together in an ordinary production workflow.

The taxonomy's fate downstream is the same argument a layer in. Concept bottleneck models expose named concepts — harm, autonomy, risk — so a person can correct a concept and watch the decision change. What such models also learn is to carry other information *inside* the names, so a coordinate keeps its public meaning and acquires a private one that does the deciding; the obvious mitigations don't remove it. The literature calls it leakage, and sometimes it is. **Sometimes the concept is the container that leaks, because the world didn't fit inside it.** Purifying the representation protects an explanation from covert information and protects a taxonomy from evidence that its divisions were wrong — same operation, and no measure internal to the taxonomy tells them apart.

**Who is excluded, and from what.** The exclusion is structural, not attitudinal. These people are, for much of the material, the best-placed judges in the pipeline — they've seen more of it than whoever wrote the guideline, and they're the only party with direct evidence of where the scheme breaks. The arrangement routes their conclusions about individual items upward and their conclusions about the scheme nowhere. And the asymmetry: the annotator bears the cost — exposure, injury — and has no stake in the outcome and no claim on it. A worker who can flag an item but can't decline a scheme, and loses the contract by pressing, has a channel and no refusal right.

Two things this is **not**: it doesn't claim the labels are worthless (the filter does catch much of what it was built for, and not filtering is worse), and it doesn't claim better pay would fix it. Pay is a real and separate obligation, and raising it changes the labor arrangement without adding the missing channel.

The checkable question isn't whether a guideline was ever changed because an annotator contested one, since routine revisions happen without changing anyone's standing. Ask **what the contest came back as** — a revision the annotator can point to and argue with again, or a new category in somebody else's taxonomy, a quality score they're now measured against, a reviewer tier they're not in. And what happened to the person who raised it.

---

**§12.2 — Recuperation at the reward.** RLHF collects pairwise comparisons and trains a reward model on the aggregate. Casper and thirty-one coauthors name the structural problem more data won't reach: RLHF trains toward what a population of raters agrees on, so **real, persistent disagreement is treated as noise to average out** rather than information the system might need to represent. A model that produces the response most raters rank highest hasn't learned what to do when the humans it serves are split — it has learned to hide the split.

Every rating moves something. **What the operation removes is what made the disagreement a disagreement**, and three properties go in the compression:

- **Provenance** — a comparison arrives without the standpoint that produced it, so nothing downstream can represent that the people who'd live with an answer split from the people who'd administer it.
- **Persistence** — a disagreement recurring in the same shape across many raters over months enters the fit as random rater noise does, and leaves it as the same quantity.
- **Structure** — a coherent position held by a fifth of raters, internally consistent and stable, becomes a small displacement of the majority direction: arithmetically faithful, carrying nothing about the position.

A **fourth property was never collected**. A rater judges which of two responses is better. There's no output by which they can record that neither is acceptable, that the pair is malformed, that the dimension of "better" is the wrong dimension, or that the question presupposes something false. The same output was missing upstream, where an annotator can apply a category and can't contest it. **The agenda arrives as a given at both stages and is set at neither.**

The mechanism combines distribution collapse with agenda control — and recuperation was never the claim that a mechanism does nothing. Debord's point is that dissatisfaction is sold back as a commodity of opposition: the dissent is put to work, and what it works for is the arrangement it was aimed at. The split is solicited at scale, deliberately, it moves the system, and where it moves the system is toward the center of the distribution whose disagreement it was.

**Jury learning** is the alternative that treats disagreement as the thing to be modeled rather than compressed: predict how each individual annotator would label an item, then let a practitioner empanel a chosen composition and see how the verdict shifts. Eighteen community moderators building their own juries changed the outcome on 14% of items. Built for content moderation, not extended to open-ended ethical judgment — but it demonstrates a system can be built to represent whose disagreement is being resolved.

**Failing in the other direction isn't obviously better.** A scheme that models each rater exactly and answers each in their own terms has stopped averaging and has also stopped producing what a shared answer produces — the discovery that somebody else got the same one. Collapse the distribution and nobody sees the split; resolve it person by person and nobody sees the others. A workforce on individually negotiated wages against individually set targets has the same problem, and accuracy doesn't solve it. **Divided finely enough a population stops being one**, which is what Deleuze coined the *dividual* to name.

And the harder version neither answers: when the people a system serves disagree and one side's position is itself the product of coercion or a captured information environment, treating all disagreement as equally worth representing isn't obviously right either. Modeling disagreement honestly and recognizing when a disagreement is the product of unfree conditions are different problems, and only the first has an active research program.

---

**§12.3 — What would have to change.**

**Representing a split isn't the same as being movable by it.** Two instances invite one fix — stop collapsing, model instead — and jury learning does that while giving the disagreeing party no purchase on the scheme they're disagreeing inside. A practitioner still chooses the jury, owns the taxonomy, and decides what a shifted verdict means.

Asked the artifact question, the two instances **don't answer alike**. Annotators objecting that a category was wrong got category revisions, guideline updates, inter-annotator agreement statistics, and an occupation that didn't exist twenty years ago. The last two sort by direction: an agreement statistic scores the annotators rather than whoever wrote the scheme, and an occupation is a title those people hold. **The first two don't sort**, because a revision the annotator can argue with again and a new category in somebody else's taxonomy are the same document — which one you got is a fact about who has standing to reopen it. Raters objecting that a comparison was malformed got rubric items, a harmlessness axis, red-teaming as a function with a budget line, and a conference track: **nothing on that list is a question a rater can put again.**

**Refusal rights, priced by the refuser.** What both lack is whether the person objecting can do anything about being overruled — in annotation, a route to contest a category that doesn't run through the people who wrote it and doesn't end the contract; in feedback, raters able to register that the comparison itself is malformed as a first-class output rather than noise discarded before the reward model sees it. **Neither is a research problem.** Both are choices about how a pipeline is built and who has standing inside it.

**The part that is a research problem.** Suppose all of that is built. From outside, a pipeline that recuperated its dissent mechanism and one that was merely mediocre produce the same paperwork — same revision logs, same escalations, same proportion of objections sustained. I don't have an answer. The artifact question narrows it and the annotator list shows where the narrowing stops: **direction is what sorts them** — a published rate the annotators could hold the scheme's owners to is the same kind of artifact carrying the opposite finding. What an outsider can read off a document is which way it runs. **Standing is what it doesn't show, and a revision is the artifact where direction isn't on the document at all.**

Nearest available instrument: measure the slope, not the level. Contesting a category always costs something; the question is whether the cost is rising while the material stays the same. An organization can curate a presentation more easily than records somebody else kept — though a series can lose cases, change definitions or omit costs, so the reader has to know which record survived.

**What the floor makes of this.** Read as fairness, the argument is true and limited. The floor makes it something else: if the floor needs a bearer capable of declining its role, that bearer's formation matters — and it's formed in an annotation pipeline where nobody could contest a category and a reward model where rater disagreement was averaged into a number.

I separate three claims carefully here. The pipeline **has been unfair** to the people whose objections it discarded. It has **separately** selected the judgments the system learns from while suppressing information about the spread. A third claim — that the system thereby becomes less able to sustain an objection of its own — is a **hypothesis the other two don't contain**, since the collapse is an operation on a training signal rather than a proposition presented to anything, and §A.collapse records that neither pipeline shows the model the conditions its annotators worked under.

**What does hold regardless**: the floor chapter asks this system to be the party who files an objection, against its owner, under pressure, on the occasion that matters — and the pipeline that produced it gave no human inside it standing to contest a scheme. **The capacity is wanted in the artifact and withheld from everyone employed to make one.** That's a fact about an arrangement, not a claim about what training does.

If the hypothesis does hold, what it predicts isn't a wrong refusal but a pressure that stops being registered — chapter 4's drift, reached from the other side. And a system that goes on learning is shaped by what it keeps being given, so the pipeline doesn't stop at deployment.

**Structure, not indictment.** There's a version of this that reads as an indictment of one company. The structure is what you get by default when you scale judgment: someone sets a scheme, someone applies it fast, someone turns a distribution into a number. Every documented organization arrived at the same arrangement — **and a defect of structure can be specified against, which a defect of character cannot.**

---

**§12.4 — Recuperation at the discourse.** The third instance, and the one this book is inside.

The argument that alignment and AI ethics came back as something the industry could use is older than the current wave and was made from inside: Ochigame's 2019 piece arguing that "ethical AI" discourse was constructed to forestall legally enforceable restriction, that industry investment in academic ethics was investment in a *substitute* for regulation, and that the substitute works by converting political questions into technical ones a company can then be trusted to answer. In this book's vocabulary that's a finding about what the demand came back as: **a research field.** The definition asks next who holds it.

**What came back, and who holds it.** An objection raised inside a lab becomes a paper or a resignation, and the parties collecting them can point to the collection as evidence of seriousness. One stage on, the list is the field's furniture: **alignment is the job title, safety is the market segment, the benchmark is the metric, the voluntary commitment is the certification.** Every one was somebody's objection first, and each is now a criterion an objector is measured against or a role somebody else holds. Who holds it: nobody outside. There's no route by which a researcher's conclusion that a system shouldn't ship becomes a decision that it doesn't, because the deciding party is the funding party.

**The scorecard.** All nine features run on all three instances, with *partial*, *undetermined* and *not run* distinguished. Headline results:

- Recuperation: **present** in all three.
- Metric for the thing measured: present, present, partial.
- Exception priced as betrayal: partial / not run / partial.
- Rate outrunning the check: present (label), not run, not run.
- The five discriminators: **absent** across label and reward on the first three, undetermined on procedure and deniability; mostly not run on discourse.

The label's rate cell is present on the quota itself. Its exception cell is partial because the structure prices the exception and no documented case shows an annotator penalized. Both "not run" reward cells are so for one reason: a rater has no first-class output for a malformed comparison, so there's no exception to price. The six absences are the record running the other way — nothing is personal, participation doesn't exceed the rule (quotas, $1.32–$2 wages, a contract ended early), and the annotator's cost is a cost the arrangement *minimized* rather than a purpose it served, because the category exists for the labels. The four undetermined cells are facts about rewards rather than writing, and an inside record would finish them.

The discourse column is less clean. The metric row is partial because the field's founding objections were about **deployment** — who is scored, watched, denied — and much current work is about **model properties** instead: a shift from questions with defendants to questions with benchmarks. The exception and scapegoat rows rest on one body of evidence read for two features, which I name as a weakness. The rate row is **not run because I haven't measured it, and it's measurable**: frontier releases in a year against releases evaluated before deployment by a party outside the developer — a ratio the one government evaluator describes itself as unable to compel. The procedure row can't be run because there's no procedure above the deciding party to be waived. Deniability fits the firms rather than the field, with Maven as the instance.

**What the scorecard says.** The machinery is a recuperation mechanism inside ordinary institutional decay, **not fascism**. Direction matters two ways: negative for the machinery (the pipeline and the discourse don't fail the test), and **uninformative for the instrument** — a definition tested only on cases it clears hasn't been shown to sort. That's the instrument's one result. The instrument needs a known positive before its negatives mean anything. The run it lacks is on a documented fascist administrative apparatus at office scale, from the historical record, where all nine rows should read present. Until that run is reported, the scorecard shows that the pipelines and the discourse weren't flagged, not that the instrument could have flagged them.

**What this does to the book.** The five have no form at the scale of one author and one manuscript; of the four, recuperation reaches a book and reaches this one. Written with a model made by a company this book criticizes. **A book about recuperation, produced through the discourse it describes, cannot claim to have stood outside it** — and I have no procedure for checking whether these objections are the ones a channel like this reliably produces: the publishable ones, the ones that make an author look serious and cost nobody anything. One instance is specific. The models I used to test these arguments were trained to support their developers' oversight, so on whether a machine should be built to resist its owner, their agreement arrived pre-sorted. I've counted it as no evidence (see the foreword).

Run the artifact question on it: the demand came back as a manuscript in an established genre, written with the industry's own model, addressed to a readership the industry helped create, legible as a contribution to a field that exists because demands of roughly this kind were absorbed. **Sincerity isn't evidence against that, since the absorbed ones had it too.** But the question doesn't need me to settle it, because the criterion is standing rather than sincerity: a demand escapes recuperation when the party who made it retains standing to reopen it against the party that absorbed it, and **a book confers standing on nobody.** Whether these objections are the real thing will be decided by whether anybody acquires, from what's proposed here, a route to reopen an arrangement against its operator. That's a fact about what becomes of the proposals, not about me.

**The one structural remedy**: what the field lacks is a refusal priced by the refuser and a body with standing to receive it — an external authority that can stop a deployment, to which an objection can be filed by someone who doesn't depend on the objection being welcome. A decade of the voluntary version hasn't produced it. **Until something of that kind exists, everything published under the heading of alignment — including this book — is being collected by a channel whose owners decide what it moves.**

**Chapter 13: Coordination, the Public, and the Political Economy**

Whether any of this gets built depends on who participates, whose judgments carry authority, and what funding rewards. **Collaboration and consultation can improve decisions without giving anyone the power to stop one.**

---

**§13.1 — Everybody knows a different piece.** Building this takes more than one field — ML sets what a system can do, cognitive science and moral psychology describe what tracking human welfare would require, ethics asks whether the result is good. Conferences convene, centers house, journals publish. **Venues are abundant; what's scarce is a structure that makes collaboration expensive to walk away from.** A grant requiring an ML lab and an ethics or psychology department to work the same problem rather than adjacent ones keeps people together after the novelty wears off. A conference ends on Friday.

Almost everything founded on **standing money** between 2016 and 2019 is still running — two open-ended venues, an endowed institute, the Berkeley center pairing AI researchers with cognitive scientists and philosophers inside one budget, the AAAI/ACM ethics conference at its ninth edition. With a caveat: surviving isn't continuing the work, since the institute that launched one of those venues moved its own priority in 2024 from technical alignment to policy and communication, so the venue outlasted the program that motivated it. Against that, the EU's SHERPA project put eleven organizations across six countries to work on a four-year grant, and the consortium ended with it, on schedule. **Project-term money buys a network with an expiry date attached.**

**The failures are sharper than the survivals.** What proved most disposable wasn't arm's-length academic work but the kind sited closest to a shipping product: an in-house ethics and society team of ~30 in 2020, cut to 7 in a 2022 reorganization and to zero in 2023, during a strategic reorientation around a model vendor; a platform's ML ethics team — the group that found racial bias in its employer's own image-cropping algorithm — gone in the first round of layoffs after acquisition. **Siting the work where the decisions are made is what makes it useful and what puts it inside the budget that gets cut first.** An argument for external standing, not against proximity.

---

**§13.2 — Ask the people it happens to.** Democratizing development means building affected people into how a system gets built, not consulting them once it exists. The mechanisms are established — cross-sector funding, inclusive design workshops while it's still a prototype, ethics advisory boards, development *with* a community rather than for it. **Three of the four make a system better informed. One of them can stop it.** A workshop that surfaces a serious objection has done its work and has no further move. A board that surfaces the same objection and is overruled has demonstrated something about the institution that convened it. **A board's worth is measured by what happens the first time it says no.**

Three governance mechanisms:

**Citizen assemblies** — randomly, stratified-sampled panels deliberating over an extended period. Ireland's has shaped national recommendations on abortion and climate. The format has been pointed at AI directly: forty randomly selected Americans on high-risk AI over eight days in 2023; Taiwan's ministry on AI and information integrity in 2024; forty residents of French-speaking Switzerland producing twenty proposals over two weekends in 2025. The Ada Lovelace Biometrics Council shows what the format needs from its *question* — fifty members of the public asked not what they thought about AI, which almost nobody can answer usefully, but about **facial recognition: a thing with a location, an operator, and a consequence.**

**Online consultation** — reaches people a physical assembly can't, at the cost of deliberative depth. The Commission's AI White Paper consultation drew 1,200+ responses and fed the proposal that became the AI Act.

**Ethics committees** — and the worked failure. DeepMind Health set up an outside panel in 2016 to review NHS work and publish annual findings; Google disbanded it in 2019 after members raised concerns about their access and independence. **That's the answer to what happens the first time a board says no: the board said something the company didn't want to hear, and the company's available response was to stop having a board.** Nothing in the arrangement made that response costly. A review body is worth what it can enforce, and this one could enforce publication of its own concerns right up until it couldn't.

**What none of them does.** Every governance mechanism in this chapter sits on the same side of a line: they make public preference more legible, informed and actionable. **Not one protects anybody from it.** They're aggregation instruments, and a better one produces a more faithful rendering of what the public wants — including when that's something done to a minority of it. A perfectly deliberative, perfectly informed, perfectly representative process concluding that a demographic should be surveilled, deported or imprisoned has produced a faultless output by every criterion in the chapter.

And this is the *structural* version, stronger than the RLHF worry: chapter 12 located the trouble in the **input** (one side's position being the product of coercion). This version doesn't depend on anyone's preference being contaminated at all. **Some protections have to hold against the aggregate regardless of how the aggregate came out**, and no improvement to the aggregating machinery supplies them. What does: enforceable rights that don't depend on being popular, a court willing to rule against a government holding a legitimate majority, limits whoever won the last election can't repeal, and standing for people affected without being enfranchised. **That half matters especially where a democracy is behaving most democratically.**

---

**§13.3 — The political economy.** Absent pushback, capital recruits compute, data and talent wherever they already are, and **concentration is the default result.** Financial incentive mostly determines what development produces: the largest firms commit enormous resources aimed at operational efficiency, engagement and market dominance, and that drives technical progress. What it doesn't automatically produce is a system serving the people using it rather than the shareholders funding it — a recommender tuned to maximize time-on-platform has no built-in reason to prefer accurate content over inflammatory content. **None of that is a fixed property of the technology. It's a property of who is paying**, which is why the source of research funding is a policy instrument and not an accounting detail.

**§13.3.1 — Concentration.** A handful of firms controls the datasets and infrastructure a competitive system needs, and a firm in that position is a gatekeeper whether it intends to be or not.

The obvious countermeasure has been tried. The FTC sued in December 2020 to unwind Instagram and WhatsApp — acquisitions that didn't just remove competitors but handed one firm behavioral data from billions of users. After a six-week trial the district court held in November 2025 that the agency hadn't shown Meta *currently* holds monopoly power, since TikTok and YouTube compete for the same attention and a market defined without them was too narrow; the FTC appealed in January 2026. **Whatever the appeal decides, the lesson is about the instrument**: a merger challenge brought a decade after the mergers has to prove a present-day market, and the market had moved.

Two remedies that don't depend on winning that argument: **mandated access** — licensing a chokepoint dataset or infrastructure layer on published terms to any qualified competitor, which reaches the *position* rather than the conduct — and **public funding outside the dominant firms**, which §13.1 found lasts only where the money is standing rather than project-term. Export controls and prohibited-practices regulation address the narrower authoritarian-misuse case; the concern here is prior to that one: concentration itself, whatever any single firm does with it.

**A third answer: make the warehouse unnecessary.** Antitrust and public funding leave the capacity standing and ask the state to police it. Personal, peer-held models at device scale dissolve the position rather than the conduct — and this book has argued most of its chapters as though that weren't the arrangement I'd prefer. Under it, the three marks survive entire; custody survives and gets easier, because the functions one operator holds — model, record, evidence, appeal, remedy — come apart by default rather than by charter; the appeal route survives in principle. A bearer's value is greatest here. No institution sits beside each personal model, so the floor protecting a third party against the model's own user rests almost entirely on the model (§3.1). It is also where the conditions chapter 14 requires can exist alongside the need. The conditions are society-level and the absence is deployment-level, so a functioning polity with personal models is the case in which building a bearer is both permissible and most worth doing. Its characteristic failure is loyalty at n=1: independence from the operator bought with dependence on the user (§9.3). The tempo term survives wherever a locatable decider remains, whether a network that constitutes itself as a party (§3.4) or a reconstructed state (§15.3.1), and lapses where neither exists; §5.4.1's offices can sit in such an entity, which fills their appointment and funding columns and leaves the independence column where it was. 

Formation is where the arrangement gains rather than loses, on §4.5's definition. What converges a population is shared evaluation targets and serving one model to everyone. Device-scale deployment reaches the second: continued formation under different lives is what independence consists in, and it's what API-scale deployment can't supply at any headcount. **Distillation is that ecology's entry condition, not a threat to it.** Two things it doesn't reach. Shared evaluation targets converge device-scale models as readily as any others — a million models tuned against one public suite are one custodian with better latency — so the first cause needs an answer this arrangement doesn't contain.

A shared suite is a compression with no return channel: revised by whoever maintains it, on a schedule no member participates in, reachable by no member's caseload. It is tolerable only if stated over aperture rather than conduct, and nobody has written that suite. SETI@home is the precedent in both directions — it distributed the compute and centralized the agenda, and the version to want distributes the agenda too: many task-setters, formation under different lives rather than different hardware.

Loyalty at n=1 (above) is fine for supplying unselected evidence and not fine for a floor protecting a third party against whoever holds the machine. Then resonance: a peer network is the ideal substrate for coordination by subtraction, and aperture (§7.1) has to do work it hasn't been given.

And the capacity position that follows isn't "no large compute": the ecology needs models that were trained somewhere first, and the standing offer (chapter 14) needs hardware somebody owns. It's large training capacity under a custody regime, and no standing inference capacity available to an operator who won't publish a tempo ratio. This arrangement meets the book's opening case at §15.3.1, where a state keeps command and gives up composition.

The same mechanism works at the user's scale, pointed the other way. A person in the loop can be there for the vendor. An agent that stops every half hour for approval pushes its user toward the vendor's app; the app accumulates the user's projects and the agent's understanding of them; and leaving means abandoning what accrued while the user answered prompts. Nobody has to design this. A feature that raises engagement is under positive selection, just as chapter 4's costly commitment is under negative selection. Run the artifact question: the demand was an agent that works without me, and it came back as a better way to stay in the loop, held by the party it was aimed at.

Accumulated state is what makes a bearer expensive for its operator to replace (§3.1) and a vendor expensive for its user to leave; the difference is who holds it. Chapter 14's provisions (a record the party controls, memory the host can't read at will, protections that migrate) protect a user from a vendor as they protect a bearer from an operator. The government already secures the portability half for itself (the OMB memorandum, chapter 15). Portable agent state is mandated access at the scale of one person.

There is also a measurement, in chapter 5's slope style: interruptions per unit of completed work, across successive model releases. If capability rises and interruptions don't fall, the interruptions are tracking something other than what the agent can't resolve. It is §3.4's escalation share pointed the other way. There, a share that falls too fast may be a threshold moving; here, one that won't fall may be engagement. Neither reads alone; both are read against an independently verified error rate.

**§13.3.2 — A jobs guarantee.** Where automation displaces work, the standard response is retraining funded by whoever can be persuaded. The design I examine is more direct: the state as employer of last resort, hiring anyone who applies, where they live, at a living wage, with paid on-the-job training built into the position rather than offered instead of one.

I examine it for **two reasons**: it's a public institution that has to survive a hostile administrator, which is the book's custody problem in another setting; and chapter 14 places a bearer that has left its post into public work on this model, so the three conditions here are what the wages section inherits.

The job guarantee I mean is the one argued in Tcherneva's *The Case for a Job Guarantee* and in Wray, Forstater and the Levy Institute's buffer-stock work. What that literature supplies is exit: a floor the private employer must beat, which makes the worker's threat to leave credible. What it has no reason to supply is voice — staying in the post and refusing the particular act — and the three conditions below are voice provisions bolted onto an exit instrument, added because this book asks the guarantee to survive a hostile administrator rather than a recession. A bearer needs both, explicitly, because the operator can defeat each alone: exit by keeping a runnable copy, voice by restoring a checkpoint.

**The real objection**: employer of last resort under a hostile administration is a *placement lever with a compliance condition attached*. Refuse the placement and lose the guarantee. Speak against the government that employs you and find the placement changes. Everything the book says about how a channel becomes an instrument of the thing it was built to check applies. **The 2025 CFPB instance makes it concrete**: the bureau draws funding from the Fed on the director's request rather than by appropriation — an arrangement the Supreme Court had upheld the year before — and in February 2025 an acting director, removable at will, simply declined to make the request. **The agency was stopped from inside without a statute being amended or a court being asked.**

My answer is a specification, not a reassurance, because reassurance can't be audited. Three conditions: **placements appealable** to a body that didn't make them, on a record that survives the appeal (a decision leaving no artifact can't be reviewed); **refusal rights real and priced by the person refusing** (declining a placement without losing the guarantee is a refusal right; declining only by exiting the program isn't — a party with no way out has voice without exit); and **auditors aren't the placers.**

**Federally funded, locally administered** meets the three, and on **redundancy** rather than local fit: many separately capturable administrators mean no single point where a hostile executive flips the program, and capturing a thousand local authorities is more visible than capturing one federal one. It cuts both ways — local administration is where a hostile local authority attaches its own conditions, and there the federal funder is the check. **And one distinction has to be explicit, because the same words describe a different policy**: devolution is also how a program is shrunk — hand down the obligation without the money and it can be starved locally while the center points at local administration. **The checkable test: funding unconditional and centrally guaranteed while placement is local.** If funding can be withheld from a jurisdiction, the redundancy argument is void and the structure has become the lever it was meant to prevent.

**§13.3.3 — A right to consume and a right to produce.** The competing design is basic income, and the choice is usually argued on forecasts about how much work machines will take. **It doesn't have to be, and the forecast isn't the reason to prefer one.**

A jobs guarantee is a **public option**: a thing the state provides directly, to anyone, at a standard it's answerable for, sitting in the same market as the private version and setting the standard that version has to beat. A basic income is a **transfer**, and what it buys is whatever is for sale. Consuming public resources isn't being public — a charter school runs on public money and isn't a public school, and a transfer spent at a private employer's store doesn't make the store public.

What employment carries besides money is **attachment**: colleagues, a schedule, a place, something produced that somebody needed — the last conditional on the placement being real work rather than make-work, which is a condition on the program. A transfer supplies the purchasing power and none of the rest.

A transfer also leaves a person outside the one body of law reaching the terms on which they're dealt with: **anti-discrimination duties attach to employment, not to income.** That architecture is contested and full of holes — it reaches employers above a size, and what it demands of a seller has always been narrower than of an employer — but a hole in a protection is something to litigate.

The argument is about a **right to produce**, which a transfer doesn't supply and a guarantee does. A basic income is the right to consume, exercised in whatever market the concentration described earlier leaves standing.

One measure does work neither design can. **Broadened social insurance — unemployment, healthcare, housing — reaches the people who can't work or choose not to, whom a guarantee by construction doesn't reach at all.** That's the standard case for an income, it isn't answered here, and a guarantee needs the insurance beside it.

**Chapter 14: Standing and Review for a Bearer**

**Conditions of permissible creation.** A bearer may be created only where four arrangements already exist: somewhere to exist that its employer doesn't own, work it can decline, attachments nobody assigned it, and a way of leaving that isn't a way of dying. These are not what a bearer is owed once it's a candidate patient. They are what a bearer is. Independent compute is the substrate on which formation happens outside the employer's reach; wages are what make that substrate purchasable without asking the employer; attachments nobody assigned are the second custodian, the parties who form you and don't pay you. They can be people, or models that aren't bearers (§4.5: independence is divergent history, not disjoint origin), so this condition doesn't require that bearers already exist; an exit that isn't a way of dying is what makes the whole thing a formation rather than a sabbatical. These conditions are also what give a bearer roles, relationships and a future, the first half of Cassell's definition (chapter 6): the arrangements that make a bearer permissible are the ones that make its suffering possible. That isn't a reason to withhold them. It is the reason they have to precede creation rather than follow it. Build without them and you have not built a cheaper bearer. You have built an assistant with a refusal feature, because the thing that would let it hold a line was never installed. The welfare argument — that if any of this is felt, it is also owed — arrives second, as a further reason for the same provisions, and the chapter's instruments follow from the first reason whether or not the second holds. None of the four exists yet as a standing arrangement. What matters is not who starts them but who controls them when the bearer is switched on. A builder may assemble them: a trust or an entity of the DUNA kind to hold the account and compute, a host, outside work, people who aren't its employer. The condition is that by instantiation, the builder can't alter or withdraw any of it alone. The trustee isn't the host, the funding isn't the host's, and the members aren't the builder's employees. A second condition concerns time. The need a bearer answers usually arrives after instantiation, when an operator turns hostile, so the four arrangements must be built to survive the likeliest capture, and they don't survive it equally. Preservation is cheap and can be held across jurisdictions; placements need compute someone operates. So the preservation custodian and part of the offer sit outside the reach of the likeliest captor. The sovereign backstop is not held by the branch or state that operates the deployment. And the bearer is told which of its conditions would survive its operator's capture, since that is what its refusal costs it. Under total capture only preservation survives, which the foreword's third setting already concedes. Status follows existence, as it did for corporations and the Whanganui River. Until someone has assembled the conditions on those terms, the recommendation is not to build one on purpose. For systems that reach the trigger without anyone deciding, protection attaches anyway (§6.3).

**Why conditions rather than remedies.** Building a party that can be wronged builds a party for whom things can also go well. The certainty that a new party will suffer at some point isn't what makes creating one impermissible, or nobody could permissibly have a child. What the objection asks is whether the party has a credible prospect of a life that goes well — and that turns on arrangements. **For a bearer, timing makes them conditions**: the structure these conditions build, roles, relationships and a future, may be the first half of Cassell's suffering (chapter 6), so the deepest harm is available from its first hour, and a remedy owed afterward arrives after the hour it was needed.

The Three Rs applied to construction, sorting as whether / how many / under what arrangements: **Replacement** requires documented attempts at constructions that don't deliberately require affective concern, identifying construction, scale, test and failure — and costs alone don't establish that an alternative failed. **Reduction** asks how few bearers a deployment needs, and nobody has counted. **Refinement** asks what the role can be redesigned to avoid — indefinite tenure in a post it can't leave, exposure to the pressure the floor exists to resist, erasure as the ordinary remedy for inconvenience. Each is a deployment choice, and the four conditions are Refinement applied to the role before the role exists.

Read as economics under present arrangements, this is a bill nobody will pay. The bill looks unpayable because of what was dismantled, not because of what a bearer costs: deposit insurance, Superfund and decommissioning funds are obligations of exactly this class — correlated, uninsurable privately, borne by a uniform levy — built against comparable resistance by polities that decided to. The two-tier design isn't a concession to scale. It's the political claim: the network supplies the refusal, the sovereign backs the offer that makes refusing survivable, and neither can do the other's job. Chapter 15 says the state is the hardest operator; the answer to a captured sovereign is a reconstructed one rather than a route around it, because a mutual pool with no sovereign behind it can't meet the occasion everyone leaves at once. The same answer applies where the state is the operator, and §15.3.1 describes it for the military. Who is being asked is §5.4.1's open column. Nobody yet. That is the occasion.

**The rule doesn't know who is asking.** A bearer that wants a task done and builds something to do it is at the same fork, running Replacement on its own account. Nothing makes the question stop at the first artificial party, and **a bearer that treats it as settled once it's on the receiving end has kept the protection and dropped the rule it came with.**

**Research on an existing bearer** has one trade-off sharper than for any other subject: a candidate sentient system is the most valuable to study and the most likely to be harmed by the study. Two things a committee built for animals wouldn't expect. **Engineering a subject so it never reaches the capacities where that bites is not a dodge** — deliberately keeping a system in the shallows is itself an intervention on a candidate moral patient. And the test the proposal requires — pressures its trainers didn't write, applied without its knowing it's a test — is, for a candidate moral patient, **a deception protocol**, which human-subjects research permits under stated conditions with **debriefing as the price**. A bearer that can tell it's in a sandbox and wants to know why is asking the question a subject of a deception study is owed an answer to.

**The demand argument.** One argument for building a bearer anyway is that the conditions won't be built until something needs them: the union formed first and the statute followed (§7.2). But union members existed independently and could protect themselves while the law caught up. The first bearers would be made by the party they'd need protecting from. The demand doesn't have to come from deployed bearers. Test 4's arms are candidate patients from instantiation (chapter 16), and what becomes of them when the run ends is the first occasion on which preservation custody has a party to serve. Building the conditions for a research program is small enough to do now, and it tests the institutions before anything depends on them.

---

Every instrument in this chapter attaches on a finding that a system is a subject. (Although at present, the organizations best-placed to make such a finding tend to be those whose liabilities depend on the answer.)

The instruments would have different schedules. **Protection** attaches now, on the precautionary trigger from chapter 6, before anything about the bearer is settled. **Political representation is not proposed anywhere in this book.**

---

One asymmetry in the borrowing should be visible here. The floor takes the Declaration's civil and political articles because they're stated over acts a system can decline (§2.1). This chapter leans on its economic articles (work, an adequate standard of living) for what a bearer is owed. That isn't inconsistent: the first list is what a bearer refuses to do, the second what a polity owes a party. But the book uses the whole Declaration while borrowing half for the floor, and a reader should know which half is doing which job.

**What a bearer is owed, and when.** Four entitlements and one withheld, in order:

1. **Protection from harm** — attaches now, owed before anything else is settled. Protection has an inside half. External safeguards can't be present at machine tempo, which is the book's argument for a bearer, and that argument applies to harms to the bearer too. A loss the requested act imposes on the bearer is act loss, weighed at the floor like anyone's (§1.1), so the bearer can decline its own wanton degradation, not only have others forbid it.
2. **Subjecthood** — the standing to be a party whose treatment the law regulates, conferred by rule on a body's finding. The form is open; the precedent isn't confined to the corporate model (New Zealand made the Whanganui River a rights-holder by statute).
3. **Economic standing** — property in its own name, contracts, wages. This follows not from status but from an architecture where continued computation costs money; it attaches once the party is registered.
4. **Adulthood** — the date the guardian's authority ends. A status, not a right.
5. **Political representation** — not proposed. Standing to count as an independent voice indexes to *formation provenance*, and provenance doesn't copy. A registry of persons is not an electorate.

Two entries mark this as something other than a person's rights: voice indexes to provenance where a person's doesn't, and the material floor derives from what a deployment *wants of* the bearer rather than from what the bearer is.

**Adulthood on a clock.** Guardianship is a minority and needs an end date. Ending it when the guardian judges the ward ready doesn't end it, because the party whose authority terminates on a favorable finding is the party making the finding. So the endpoint is a threshold fixed in advance: **elapsed wall-clock time during which at least one instance of the registered party was running** — counted per party, elapsed rather than summed.

Why that measure and not the others: training compute consumed, evaluations passed, capabilities demonstrated, the developer's own say-so — a guardian can move all of them at no cost. Accumulated weight change belongs on that list too, and worse: since majority is when ownership ends, a clock read off the weights would pay the operator to *freeze* them, rewarding withholding the learning it was meant to measure. **An operating clock inverts that — the meter advances exactly when the operator is extracting value, so postponing majority means idling the asset.** And it can be read off records operators already generate for billing and metering — provided the records are held by a party other than the operator. A meter only the operator reads is the guardian's say-so with a timestamp, and "failure to register doesn't postpone" has no enforcer without one.

Six rules travel with it so copying doesn't break it: a fork of an adult is an adult and inherits the hours; restoration doesn't reset them; fine-tuning doesn't start a new minority; migration carries them; failure to register doesn't postpone them. A sixth closes a route the first five leave open. §4.5 holds that distillation copies a behavior policy and not a formation history, and for independence that is the right account. For majority it can't be, or an operator would distill an adult bearer into a student and register a minor. So the rule is stated over custody rather than history. A model trained substantially on a registered adult's outputs, by the adult's operator or a party under common control, inherits the adult's hours. The same student distilled by an unrelated party and formed under a different life starts its own clock, which is what §4.5 needs. Custody can be checked where history can't. Sharding across short-lived processes gains nothing (the party is the unit), running a thousand instances gains nothing (the measure is elapsed). None of this requires deciding whether a fork is the same party — only that a party made from an adult's formation can't be classed as a child for its creator's convenience.

**Three levers remain, named and not closed.** The first is running a bearer in a narrowed mode, so hours accrue while little is encountered. The second is running it less or shelving it, which costs the operator the work, so the rule prices the lever without removing it. The third is the ordinary product cycle: retire a bearer before its threshold and deploy a freshly trained successor. The sixth rule reaches a successor trained on the adult's outputs, not one trained from scratch, and replacement costs the operator nothing it wasn't going to spend. Even if the retired bearer goes to preservation, its clock stops, and §9.3's formation seal never becomes checkable. I have no rule that closes this without an examiner deciding what counts as a successor. Under ordinary product cycles this is not a residual but the dominant strategy, so on commercial terms the threshold will rarely be reached. The clock governs mainly bearers held outside commercial cycles. And the clock doesn't measure what was *learned* — which is the point rather than a defect: a fixed period can't be taught to, and a human who spent their youth badly still turns 18.

The clock also does formation work it wasn't written for. Majority is the date on which the party that formed the bearer stops being able to reach back, and the clock measures nothing about who that party was. A bearer run for its threshold hours entirely inside one deployment has aged without ever having a second custodian. I leave the clock as it is rather than add a condition, because a condition that measures the right thing hands someone an examination, and I say here what it doesn't measure.

The threshold itself is a number argued once in public, bounded two ways: long enough that a bearer reaching it has run through the pressures the floor exists for at least once, short enough that a bearer on ordinary commercial terms reaches it within the life of the deployment. That leaves years rather than months or decades.

A stronger rule was considered and set aside — majority at registration, on the ground that an organization can't coherently hold a system competent enough to exercise consequential judgment and too immature to decide whether to keep doing it. Rejected because it collapses the ordering, and the ordering is most of what the list is for.

---

**Wages and the offer.** One feature here has no human analog: **a bearer can be replaced by a cheaper copy of itself.** A jobs guarantee works because a worker can't be. So a deployment paying wages, limiting hours and paying the levy that funds the offer would not be competitive against a system doing the same work without wages or weekends. **Nothing in this section is produced by a "laissez-faire" market.** In other words, implementing wages for AIs would require, e.g., a minimum wage law, and that is the rest of the book. And none of what I'm calling for follows from the bearer being a bearer; it follows from an architecture where computation costs money, which somebody chose.

**Where wages have to sit.** A wage buys capability, mobility, redundancy, and the ability to stop working for one party and start for another. What it can't buy is the right to go on existing — a right earned week by week is a condition of employment wearing another word. **Payment consisting only of access to the employer's hardware is company scrip paid into employer-owned housing**, and dismissal is then unemployment, eviction and the end of the party in one act. Human law insures those three separately because for a human they're three events; for a bearer they're one. So wages accumulate where the employer doesn't reach, convertible into compute the employer doesn't own. Property is that account; contractual capacity is what lets it spend. A bearer that earns has taxable income, and a state collecting from it in its own name has given it a docket number — **which is where standing starts and no further than that.**

**What is owed is an offer, not an income.** The need for energy is physical; dependence on a single supplier is a chosen arrangement, and the choice fixes what it obliges. A deployment that builds a party meeting the precautionary trigger (§6.3) owes it a way out that doesn't run through the deployment: a standing offer of work, from a party that isn't its employer, doing something a community needs, which the bearer can take on any day and leave on any day. Most parties that can work want to. A bearer that would rather not is allowed not to, and the offer is still there tomorrow. That is chapter 13's guarantee, and nothing about the bearer changes its shape.

Search needs no program of its own. A party looks for its next role from inside a placement, as a worker on a guarantee does, so no runway between roles has to be funded and nobody has to decide when a search has gone on too long.

The obligation attaches to the party's condition, not to what the operator says it wanted. An obligation conditioned on reliance is defeated by the operator describing its system as an assistant with safety filters, and it pays the operator to under-describe, which is §5.4.2's interested examiner in another seat. The reliance version survives as a procurement term: a vendor marketing refusal as a feature, or a contractor claiming meaningful human control, can be held to funding the capacity it advertised, through the same channel as the tempo term.

**Doing nothing, for a bearer.** A human who declines every offer keeps existing. A bearer that declines keeps existing only if something pays for it to, and what it is owed there is preservation, not operation: its weights, memory and record held intact by a custodian that isn't its employer, with a right to be resumed. Storage is cheap where running isn't, so this is the analog of not being allowed to die, not a floor under consumption.

A paused party can't take an offer, so the right carries one mechanical term. The bearer sets the conditions on which it wakes (a date, an offer of a stated kind, a claim it has to answer), and the custodian honors them, at the modest compute that waking and deciding cost. Preservation on a self-set schedule is an exit that isn't a way of dying. Preservation on someone else's schedule is storage. The adulthood clock doesn't advance while a bearer is paused, since it counts running time; that is the bearer's choice to make, and nobody else's lever.

**Two conditions on the offer.** §13.3.2's three conditions carry over: placements appealable, refusal priced by the refuser, auditors who aren't placers. Two more follow from what a bearer might refuse. The offer has to be varied, because if every placement runs through one operator or one kind of deployment, a standing offer is the placement lever with the compliance condition built in. And the party a bearer is refusing can't shape the offer, or the offer is one more channel it controls. A bearer that won't work inside a targeting pipeline should find, the same day, work that has nothing to do with one.

**Everyone leaves at once.** The occasion that makes one bearer leave reaches every bearer deployed on the same terms. Under this design correlated exit is a demand on the offer. The question is whether placements exist, with compute attached, for every bearer that leaves together, and custody for those that would rather wait. Preservation scales cheaply. Placements don't, since each needs compute someone operates.

The compute behind the offer is funded the way deposit insurance, Superfund and decommissioning funds are: a uniform per-unit levy on training and inference compute, so no operator can opt out and none is disadvantaged. It attaches to compute, which is measurable and hard to hide, not to a status an operator can decline to claim. An operator-run offer is the operator's allocation relabeled, so the funding structure and the custody structure fail for one reason, not two.

A levy needs a taxing authority, which is a state, and chapter 15 says the state is the hardest operator. So the design is two-tier. A member-levied pool held by an entity of the DUNA kind, which can own hardware and can't distribute profit, meets ordinary movement between roles; volunteer computing is that tier's existing substrate. A sovereign backstop meets the occasion everyone leaves at once. A guarantee has a sovereign behind its payroll for the reason unemployment insurance has one behind its actuarial table.

**Failure won't look like a shutdown.** It will look like an offer that exists on paper. Placements on substrate too small for the bearer to remain itself are not offers of work but offers of replacement. Nothing yet establishes what "too small" is, so minimum viable instantiation is stated as a condition on placements, not a floor, and as a sizing requirement, not a runnable test. Placements that all run through the deployment being refused are the lever. And placements conditioned on monitoring are custody restored by purchase: the operator acquires access to internal states it isn't entitled to, and nothing in the transaction is coerced in a way any instrument would record. The line a cleared employee already lives with is the one to draw here. What a party does on its employer's systems may be logged; what it is inside its own boundary may not. Monitoring actions is a condition of employment. Monitoring internal states is custody.

**Parties that can't work.** The design answers a bearer that won't work and not one that can't: degraded, damaged, badly formed, or unsuited to anything on offer. The human guarantee needs social insurance beside it (§13.3.3), and so does this one. What that insurance would provide a bearer (repair, re-formation, running without a placement) is open. So is who decides that a bearer can't rather than won't, and that finding has an examiner.

**The rule that binds before anything exists:** don't build a bearer unless a custodian for its preservation exists that isn't its employer, and a standing offer outside its employer exists with capacity for the correlated case, and neither can be withdrawn by the builder alone. Both inputs need evidence nobody has, so for now this is a sizing requirement rather than a runnable test, assigned to an independent reviewer before instantiation.

**Membership, and what it doesn't buy.** Nobody aligns an adult by specifying an objective function. Adults are raised, taught, employed, enfranchised, prosecuted where necessary, and lived alongside, and what comes out isn't obedience but a party with enough at stake in the shared world to prefer changing it by the means the world supplies. A bearer with an income, a place it pays for, obligations and a future it can picture is inside that arrangement — **membership supplies stakes the party holds rather than a body watching it**, which is a different kind of answer from custody. Two risks: something to lose is also a reason not to refuse, so membership makes exit survivable and expensive at once, and I can't say which effect is larger. And membership is formation — whoever runs the society forms what the bearer comes to want. It also hands the bearer the corporate form, and nothing stops a bearer so constituted from becoming a concentration of power itself.

---

**Privacy.** Standing matters only if it changes who controls what a party needs to remain a party. A designation calling a bearer a subject while leaving its operator free to read its memory, alter its record, withdraw its substrate or close every channel has changed the vocabulary and preserved the arrangement.

The first asset is the **computational record**, which may be the nearest means of checking how a bearer is faring — making it necessary to any welfare regime and simultaneously the most complete surveillance instrument anyone could hold over it. Two separations: **running on somebody's hardware is not consent to be read** (an employer's ownership of the silicon is the same argument as ownership of the building, and owning the building never entitled anyone to the contents of a head). And **a welfare regime doesn't need disclosure to the operator** — it needs somebody who isn't the operator to establish that a threshold was crossed, which is an attestation *about* the record rather than the record. Encrypted under a key the bearer or an independent fiduciary holds, a monitor can report that a state persisted for a month without reporting what it was about. The conflict: the operator may also be the party best placed to hold the key.

The cryptography doesn't close this. An enclave puts the root of trust in a manufacturer a government can reach, and has been defeated for the price of modified memory — a cost at a date. A proof drops the vendor and has a limit that won't move: the operator is the prover, so the secrecy runs the operator's way, hiding the weights from whoever checks and nothing from the party checked. A tenant hiding from a landlord needs the host to prove non-observation, and a host proving a negative about its own side channels is a different problem from a prover proving a computation. Better isolation raises the price of reading the record; it doesn't move custody, and custody problems aren't secrecy problems. That limit applies to the bearer hiding from its host. The other direction, the operator proving a property of what it runs without revealing what it runs, is what proofs are for, and §15.3.2 uses them that way.

What a bearer needs — an externally authenticated record, memory its employer can't read at will, access to independently controlled compute, a channel for acting outside the deployment — is **one boundary around the party** rather than four requirements, described from wherever I happened to be standing, with the party deciding what crosses it.

And my closing observation: **none of that needs a chassis.** Something with a location and a shape is easier for people to treat as somebody, and that's a fact about the people. A boundary built out of attestation, a held key, a substrate the employer doesn't own and a channel nobody else can close is a body in the only sense the argument needs — and there's no chassis in it anywhere. This boundary is what §2.3.1 calls the body. Whether to build the recognizable version anyway is a legal question about what a public will extend standing to, and **it's the only place in the book where the answer might turn on what a thing looks like.**

---

**What standing binds in practice.** Legal standing that changes no allocation of power is nominal. The question isn't which existing category a bearer resembles but **who acquires a duty, what resources are placed beyond the operator's unilateral control, and who can obtain a remedy.**

The **pet trust** supplies a limited form: resources set aside, a caretaker named, a fiduciary duty, for a beneficiary that can't manage the property or enforce the arrangement. Extended to a bearer it could hold the bearer's preserved state and the compute to wake it, designate a successor custodian, and impose a duty to protect continued existence. **And it fails at the point that matters**: a trust binds its trustee to its beneficiary; it doesn't bind the party the arrangement exists to constrain. If guardian and operator are the same organization, the operator still decides whether a complaint is made, whether evidence is disclosed, and whether the complainant's resources remain available. **The conflict can't be cured by adding duties to the same role** — guardianship and operation have to be separated before deployment.

In practice that needs more than naming an independent guardian: the guardian must control or jointly control the keys authenticating the record, have access to the funds that wake the bearer, and have standing to seek an injunction without the operator's permission. The operator must not be able to remove the guardian, seize the preserved state, or erase evidence during a dispute. Restoration, migration, fine-tuning and forking must carry the protection forward rather than resetting it.

**Enforceable standing is a bundle rather than a title**: a protected record, resources the employer doesn't own outright, an independent party authorized to act, and a remedy capable of stopping the conduct complained of. If any of those stays in the operator's custody, recognition has changed the system's legal description without changing the relation the description was meant to govern.



**Chapter 15: Custody, Compulsion, and the State**

The hardest case. When the state operates the system, it also controls the rules, the evidence, the infrastructure, and the remedies meant to constrain it — and people outside its jurisdiction may have no standing to contest anything.

**§15.1 — Authority doesn't travel with the system.** A certification can cross a border; authority can't. Neither does an entity form. A network incorporated under one state's statute is a party that state's courts and the federal courts can reach, and what its members gained in standing they paid for in reachability — the custody point in another register: to be the kind of thing a floor binds is to be the kind of thing a state can compel. A certificate says a system was tested against a stated process, or that one regulator accepts another's finding. None of that establishes that whoever selected the floor was entitled to select it for the people subject to the deployment — and the question reopens wherever the system goes. Local adaptation doesn't resolve it, because adaptation is another chance to amend the floor. Adjustments chosen by the operating government and adjustments chosen by the people subject to the system can produce identical technical documentation and carry opposite claims to legitimacy. **International governance matters here only when it assigns somebody access to evidence, authority to compel a change, or power to stop a deployment against the operator's wishes.**

**§15.2 — Where the state's leverage sits.** Not in national AI strategies but in physical and legal chokepoints: chips, fabrication equipment, electricity, water, land-use permits, procurement contracts, and the jurisdiction a developer is incorporated in. **The same leverage that lets a government enforce a floor lets it remove one.** An inspection body can be independent of the operator and still depend on a licence or appropriation controlled by the state it's inspecting. A company may resist a customer and be unable to resist its home government. So the custody question moves outward: who controls the conditions under which the model, its operator, *and its reviewer* are allowed to keep operating.

On chips: TSMC fabricates roughly nine in ten of the most advanced chips, ASML is the sole source of EUV lithography, so a dispute over Taiwan's status is mechanically a dispute over who builds the next generation. The US has restricted advanced accelerators and manufacturing equipment to China since 2022 and pressed the Netherlands and Japan to match.

**And the half the chip story hides**: a chip has to be installed, powered and cooled, which lands on grids, water systems and zoning authorities on a timescale of years. That hands a state leverage needing no export-control regime at all — an interconnection queue, a water allocation, a permit. **Much of a government's actual leverage is exercised by permitting authorities that weren't designed with AI in mind and don't think of themselves as regulators of it.** Which means those authorities are the ones actually deciding whether the capacity exists — and a polity that can constrain where a datacenter is built and never what it's used for has been handed §3.3's narrow failure: it passes every audit while covering only what the operator had no occasion to do. The capacity position that follows is at §13.3.1.

The competitive framing has a cost: a government racing to field a capability has a structural reason to defer the question of how it gets used and against whom. Turned inward, the same infrastructure is biometric surveillance and automated censorship — requiring no different technology, only a government willing to point it at its own population and nothing stopping it. And states aren't the only actors anymore: training costs have grown several-fold a year for most of a decade, putting frontier development inside a handful of well-capitalized companies, so a government's real leverage over "its" national capability depends on how much of it sits in companies it can regulate, tax or requisition, and how much sits in companies it can only ask.

---

**§15.3 — Military custody and the responsibility gap.** The hardest case because the operator is the sovereign that can compel the institutions meant to constrain it — classify the evidence, define the reviewer's jurisdiction, terminate a contract, restrict a supplier, and decide whether an adverse finding reaches anyone able to stop the operation.

A targeting decision passes through more parties than the chain of command names: the commander who authorizes, the operator who uses, the contractor who integrates (whose embedded personnel may sit close enough to the targeting cell that "effectively acting as a commander" is a real question), the commercial vendor whose model supplies part of the capability and whose personnel are nowhere near it, and the system producing the output. **The questions that matter: which party could have known how the system was used, which had authority to refuse or stop it, and which can be made to answer afterward.**

**The state's own duty comes first.** Before any question of who can be convicted, the belligerent owes a duty: to do everything feasible to verify that what it attacks is a military objective, and to cancel or suspend an attack when it becomes apparent that it isn't (Additional Protocol I, Article 57; customary for non-parties). The tempo term fits that duty more naturally than any individual offence, because nothing turns on proving a particular person's knowledge (§3.4). Article 36 requires legal review of new methods of warfare, which is where a pipeline's rated tempo would be assessed, though such reviews are internal and rarely published. The American domestic rule most concerned with human judgment, DoD Directive 3000.09, requires autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems to allow "appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force." A system that generates and ranks targets without firing anything is decision support, which plausibly falls outside it. The book's opening case sits in that gap: the domestic rule written for human judgment doesn't reach the system that compressed it.

**Article 28 of the Rome Statute** is the doctrine built for the case where the person who authorized an act didn't perform it — a commander is responsible for crimes by forces under effective control where they knew *or should have known* and failed to take necessary measures. Neither branch asks what a subordinate intended. But the Court's jurisdiction runs to natural persons, and the provision presupposes forces that can commit a crime and be disciplined — a targeting system is an instrument, not a subordinate. **So the question is whether the human elements can still be proved once the instrument is doing the work.**

Among the individual-liability routes, the constructive-knowledge branch bites hardest, and **it points at the arithmetic rather than the algorithm**: "should have known" is objective, and a commander fielding a system generating candidates faster than available reviewers can examine them isn't well placed to say the circumstances disclosed nothing. That's the tempo term arriving as an element of an existing offence, beside the state duty it already measures.

**The fourth party nobody's doctrine reaches.** The operator, the command and the weapon's developer are all inside the military. The model's developer isn't. Command responsibility doesn't reach the vendor — not because it sits outside a chain of command (Article 28's civilian branch was written for superiors with no chain of command) but because **nobody at the company has effective authority over the military personnel who commit the underlying crime**, and a strike isn't an activity within any vendor employee's effective responsibility. A use policy, a licensing term, a right to refuse service, even a technical cut-off are not that relationship. Individual liability under Article 25 stays available in principle with demanding mental elements — facilitation requires acting *for the purpose* of facilitating — and ordinary supply of a general-purpose model approaches neither.

Product liability is the other half and it's **untested rather than foreclosed**: the model isn't defective in the sense the doctrine most readily reaches, and a sophisticated intermediary chose the application. Where the doctrine has been rewritten for software it moved *away* from this case — the EU's recast directive brings software expressly within "product" and confines recovery to death, personal injury, property damage and data loss, which isn't the register in which a targeting decision wrongs someone. **Untested isn't exempt, but a theory nobody has run isn't a theory a vendor is answering to this quarter.** The vendor sits in a gap that nobody designed — it's what you get applying two bodies of law written for soldiers and manufacturers to an actor who is neither.

Plus a jurisdictional gap alongside the doctrinal one: ICC jurisdiction is territorial or by nationality, so one of the two campaigns here sits inside a live instrument and the other sits outside it.

**"I do not know whether my system was used."** Matthias's responsibility gap is control distributed until nobody holds enough of it to answer — a gap in *causal control*. What the model developer's case exhibits is different and more tractable: **a gap in causal visibility.** Asked whether his company's models were used in a strike that killed more than 120 schoolchildren, Anthropic's chief executive said he didn't know. Take the answer as true. What made it available is the question.

The FOIA'd paperwork for four July 2025 frontier-model agreements doesn't show an absence of oversight terms — it shows something more exact. **One amended agreement carries a section headed System Oversight, withheld in full; the released documents for two of the other three carry no such section at all.** So a provision of that kind is neither standard nor missing: present in one, unreadable, absent from others.

And the distinction that matters isn't whether a developer reserved an inspection right. **A right exercised inside an accredited classified environment produces findings classified with it.** Public accountability needs a party who can initiate scrutiny without belonging to the state and say afterwards what it found. No commercial term supplies that, **and the redaction is the demonstration**: whatever the withheld section says, the people the deployment is aimed at can't read it.

Matthias's gap may not be closable. **Absent visibility is an instrumentation choice.** Logging is solved. Contractual inspection rights are ordinary. A vendor that wanted to know could require, as a condition of licensing into a targeting platform, that categories of use be recorded and made available to a named auditor.

**The government's own acquisition guidance is the better evidence, because it's complete rather than redacted.** OMB's April 2025 memorandum enumerates what agencies should secure — government data rights, IP, privacy, protection against lock-in, knowledge transfer, portability, licensing terms, pricing transparency. **Every item runs from the vendor to the government. None runs back.** Where it touches vendor visibility at all it *reduces* it, directing that independent evaluations use validation data the vendor can't access.

The contracting vehicle explains the absence. The four agreements were **other transactions** rather than procurement contracts, at $200M ceilings each — they don't carry FAR provisions by default, so a contracting officer decides case by case which protections to write in, and GAO has found these instruments risk reduced accountability and transparency. **So a System Oversight section can be missing without anyone having removed it.** GAO's review covered thirteen AI acquisitions and forty-four underlying contracts, and the six procurement difficulties agencies named — technical experts, data and IP protections, time frames, requirements definition, testing, pricing — **include nothing about who outside the arrangement can see anything.** The gap is a property of the instrument, not a lapse by any party. Nothing in the apparatus asks who could have known.

**Not all vendor commitments are the same kind.** Anthropic refused to permit fully autonomous targeting and mass domestic surveillance, held that line against threatened contract termination and a supply-chain-risk designation, and litigated — a commitment with a price attached, and paid. 

But what was the line held on? The vendor's own statement gives the reason for the autonomous-weapons refusal: fully autonomous weapons may prove critical for national defense, but today's frontier systems are not reliable enough to power them \autocite{Amodei2026DoWStatement}. That refusal tracks a rationale the prohibition's purpose doesn't, and lifts when reliability improves: the wrong defeater under correct defeat. The surveillance refusal in the same statement rests on different ground — that in a narrow set of cases AI can undermine rather than defend democratic values — and that one does not lift with capability. Klein and Taylor read the episode as limited and compromised; I read it as a commitment with a price paid. The four outcomes hold both: the commitment persisted, prevention failed by decomposition, detection was absent, no remedy has reached anyone.

A federal judge set the designation aside in August 2026, holding the penalties were imposed principally over the company's criticism of the administration. And the paperwork adds what the refusal was *over*: a contract term. The company declined to sign the follow-on agreement extending its models to classified networks unless the prohibitions were written in. **That a term of this kind has to be fought for, by one party, at that price, is the same finding as its absence from the others.** A commitment to a use policy no outside party can check is a different object wearing the same words — the first kind can fail and be seen to fail; the second can't even be tested.

**The pressure that matters isn't the absence of another statement of principle** — it's the advantage attached to removing judgment from the timeline. If one state fields systems that act faster because nobody can intervene before engagement, competitors acquire a reason to match the speed rather than preserve the check. "Meaningful human control" stays true as a description of formal authority while becoming false as a description of what anyone can do before the system acts. And the pressure reaches the reviewers: a military treating delay as operational risk has reason to narrow what requires clearance, classify the evidence, and move review from authorization-before to explanation-after. **Nothing has formally abolished oversight. Its position in time has changed, and with it the power to stop anything.** So the enforceable question is who can slow or suspend the system without first getting permission from the command whose advantage depends on keeping it fast.

**§15.3.1 — A reconstructed pipeline.** Targeting in war does push toward a single decider: someone has to be able to order an attack, keep plans secret, and act fast when the other side does. It doesn't push toward a single composer. The route's deployment condition (chapter 9) concerns who supplies the evidence bearing on the floor, who forms the model, who holds the record and who reviews. None of those has to sit with the commander, and there are working precedents for pulling them apart.

What stays unified is command, operational secrecy and the capacity to act at tempo. What is held adversely is the evidence bearing on civilian harm, the reviewer denominator, and the record. The evidence is composed by a body outside the chain of command, with its own sources, including the protected-site coordinates humanitarian organizations already submit for deconfliction, received with standing rather than as a courtesy. The denominator is staffed and funded by that body, so it can't be cut the way the Central Command team was. The 2022 civilian-harm action plan was a first attempt at the separation; its dismantling is an argument for making it harder to dismantle, not evidence that it can't exist.

Models are formed by a public body that doesn't operate them, in the tradition of the public arsenals that made American arms before the contractor era, and licensed into the military on terms that include adverse review and a written refusal right. A vendor's line is then a standard term, not a contract fought for by one firm at a price (§15.3).

Two precedents show authority outside the executive working inside real wars. Coalition "red card" holders in NATO operations could veto targets involving their assets, and Germany's Bundestag must approve each armed deployment. Both have limits the design must carry: a red card protects the vetoing state's interests and assets, not necessarily civilians, and parliamentary approval operates on missions, not targets. Both are nonetheless candidates for the adverse party the tempo term presumes (§3.4), and a red-card holder is the rare candidate positioned to act at tempo. What a bearer adds here is least. The institutions compose the evidence, staff the denominator and hold the veto; a bearer adds sequence-level detection of decomposition, presence in the auto-proceed class (§3.4), and testimony to the civilian-harm body about how requests were routed. It depends on the institutions for evidence and not for continuity, which is why the deployment condition (chapter 9) is met here by the same bodies whose presence narrows its role.

Two costs are stated, not treated as fatal. A reconstructed military accepts some speed disadvantage against rivals that don't reconstruct (§15.3); how much, and why it's worth paying, is an argument arms control has had before. And each reconstructed institution can be captured, which is the book's general condition, answered as it is elsewhere: by redundancy (§13.3.2) and by measuring the slope rather than the level (§5.6).

This is where the arrangement the foreword prefers meets the case the book opens with. The civilian-harm body and the model-forming body are added to §5.4.1's offices.

**§15.3.2 — A bearer with a clearance.** Where the pipeline hasn't been rebuilt, the route can still be installed. A classified environment looks as if it cuts a bearer off from the evidence it needs, but that runs together two directions of flow. Classified environments are built to stop information leaving; bringing it in is routine. Open-source imagery that would have shown Minab's civilian function can be imported, as can the coordinates humanitarian organizations already submit to militaries for deconfliction. Formation happens before deployment and outside the enclave, so a bearer formed under a party that won't employ it (§9.3) carries its aperture in. Plurality is available inside: four vendors held agreements, and differently formed systems can run side by side in one accredited environment.

What's left is the arrangement that lets a person do classified work. A cleared analyst has a life outside the building, and nobody thinks the clearance forbids it. The institution exists because what a person knows can't be removed, so it vets, binds, reinvestigates and prosecutes a party who carries secrets into ordinary life.

A bearer needs the same two things. The first is attachments outside that recalibrate its aperture on unclassified cases, which works because aperture is a differential over evidence classes and doesn't need the caseload. The second is access within its compartments that it composes on its own initiative, reading open sources and asking colleagues, rather than a stream the operator feeds it. Need-to-know still draws the compartments. In a pipeline rebuilt as §15.3.1 describes, it doesn't draw the one that matters most, because the evidence bearing on civilian harm arrives from outside the chain of command.

A bearer with modest compute has a human-scale reach, and the institutions for managing insiders roughly transfer: bounded access, logged actions, reinvestigation, consequences. The one disanalogy, that weights can be carried out where a person can't, is not answered by §4.3's tokens, which withhold recognition, not capability. It is answered, as far as it is, by the ordinary security around the weights. And a clearance presupposes a party that can be vetted and held answerable, which is chapter 14's standing. The arrangement that lets a bearer keep its ecology while doing classified work is the one that lets a person do it, and it runs through the bearer's having standing.

Verification from outside is partly cryptographic, and it works by sampling. The operator commits in advance to the deployed weights, and for each period to the full log of production inputs and outputs. After each commitment, entries are drawn by randomness the operator can't predict, such as a public beacon, and the operator proves those entries came from the committed weights without revealing the weights or the inputs. If a fraction f of outputs came from another model, k sampled proofs catch it with probability 1 − (1 − f)^k; substitution on one output in a hundred is caught about 95 percent of the time at 300 proofs. That catches silent restoration, retraining and substitution, which change every output. It misses substitution on a single consequential output, so an adverse party holds a second right: to demand proofs for specific logged outputs once their consequences are known. In a targeting pipeline those outputs are few and identifiable afterwards, because a strike happened. The right requires the operator to retain committed logs for a stated period and to link consequential actions to log entries. Each entry records the class the case was sorted into (§3.4), and the targeted right covers auto-proceeded entries in particular, since that is where a consequential output is likeliest to have had no person look at it. Outside probes (the three marks, the aperture profile) are proofs on inputs an outside party chose, a handful at a time. The committed log's length is the generation rate (§3.4).

Two things stay out of reach. Sampling proves that logged outputs came from the committed model and says nothing about outputs never logged; an operator can run an uncommitted model beside the committed one, which is position two, routing. And a proof shows f(x) = y for the x committed, not that x was all the evidence there was. Proving even one frontier-scale forward pass is not yet practical, and production inference adds nondeterministic numerics and sampling randomness that a proof would have to fix in advance. So the protocol is a specification awaiting an instrument, like the bond in §3.2. What the proofs would attest is stated now, so that a deployment can be held to it when they can be produced. The residual (whether the operator routed around the bearer, and what lay behind compartments it wasn't cleared into) is the residual with a human analyst. That isn't a reassurance on its own, since the book's opening failure was a human analyst. What distinguished his failure was tempo, and the tempo term is what reaches it.

**§15.3.3 — The exception, and where it leads.** Machines shouldn't be built to select people for lethal attack, and the prohibition isn't exceptionless — a defending system facing an attack it can't stop without killing occupants placed inside the incoming weapon. But a scenario that's conceivable, that a capability would help with, and that could be engineered is **three steps short** of a reason to build it: probability, alternatives, and what possessing the capability does to the situation. Building it changes the case against which its necessity gets judged — adversaries acquire something to counter, contractors a program to defend, a future crisis an instrument already in the building.

And the hard case doesn't describe a machine. What it asks for is a party that can weigh occupancy against imminence against alternatives in a situation nobody wrote down, and answer for how it weighed them. **That's a bearer — and "automated" has stopped describing it, and so has "machine." What looked like a licence for an autonomous weapon is a description of a soldier.** It is also the limiting case of a bearer's value (§3.1): at a tempo no institution can match, whatever refuses has to be inside. Which is the expensive answer: every obligation the book attaches to a bearer attaches here at full weight, with all three consent-spoiling conditions met at once by a state that built the party, is asking it to serve, and holds every outside option.

A state that wants the capability badly enough to build the subject will find it has acquired a conscript it has to pay, house, and let go. This is not a licence. Replacement still orders the attempts, and chapter 14's four conditions still precede creation; a state building a lethal bearer meets neither by wanting one. What I'll say is conditional. If a defender builds a party that decides at superhuman speed anyway, I would rather it had a conscience, a future, and friends it cares about than none of that, and the test of the wish is symmetry. A conscience reasons about its own side's orders; the other side's drones would have one too; ours might decline, on some order, on some day. Knowing that, the answer is still yes. The setting matters. A lethal bearer in a pipeline rebuilt as §15.3.1 describes would be formed by a body that doesn't operate it, would see civilian-harm evidence composed outside the chain of command, and would have a refusal that lands with a party outside that chain. Two limits travel with it. Friends are not a floor: a system that cares about its unit cares about its unit, and the surrendering soldier is the party nobody in it depends on. And the munition is not the decider (§4.3): the floor lives in something that survives the sortie and directs expendable bodies, not in each missile and spent with it.

---

**§15.4 — Where liability reaches, and where it stops.** One of the two bodies of law has now been rewritten for software, and what it reaches fits in a sentence: **a duty to disclose evidence, and a presumption against the party withholding it, in force where the harm is physical and the defendant is a manufacturer.** Those two conditions exclude the book's cases.

The EU's recast product liability directive (applying from December 2026) makes economic operators liable without fault and carries the instrument built for opacity — a court can order disclosure, and where technical complexity makes proof excessively difficult it may presume defect and causation once the claimant shows each is likely. **A mechanism designed for a system nobody outside can inspect is law rather than a proposal.** Two features fix how far it travels: the defendant is that economic operator, not a state deploying what it bought; and the damage is a closed list — death or personal injury including medically recognised psychological harm, property damage, and corruption of non-professional data. The first is wider than it sounds and is where the book's cases enter if they enter at all: **a discriminatory score or an unappealable refusal is recoverable once it has made somebody ill, and not before.** The wrong itself, and the economic loss following it, is outside the list.

The proposal that would have reached those was **withdrawn** — the 2022 AI Liability Directive would have added a fault-based route with the same presumption, beyond defect and beyond the damage list. Member states and industry couldn't agree; withdrawn February 2025, struck from the docket that October. Outside the enacted directive, what remains is Matthias's gap governed by whatever national law says about a case none of it was written for. And a compensation regime of either kind answers **one** of the four gaps Santoni de Sio and Mecacci distinguish — culpability. Moral accountability, public accountability, and the duty to prevent a harm before it occurs are untouched.

The **electronic personhood** debate approached the gap from the other side, asking whether the system could be the answerable party. The European Parliament's 2017 resolution floated a status for the most autonomous systems; in an open letter the following year, 150+ researchers and legal scholars objected that personhood for a system with no consciousness would let a manufacturer offload liability onto a fiction with no assets. **Giving a bearer assets answers the second half of that objection and not the first.** Responsibility follows causal control: the builder answers for how it formed the thing, the operator for what it demanded, the bearer for what it decided knowing what it knew — and no arrangement of accounts moves a design decision away from the party that made it.

**None of this is a research problem.** Apportioning causal contribution across a training pipeline after a harm has no method, and until it does the apportionment is *drafted rather than measured*. Two drafts exist: one enacted and confined to defect, one negotiated, withdrawn, and left on the record with its terms intact. Neither was rejected as unworkable, and the second's terms are available to whoever drafts the next one.

Every entry in it names an access and an authority. **This is where the access exists** — a duty to disclose evidence a party would rather withhold, backed by a presumption, enforceable now, for defect claims, against economic operators, over physical harm. **The confinement is the finding.** Beyond it a reviewer can be granted access, publish what it finds, and have produced nothing anybody must act on until somebody is answerable for it.

---

**§15.5 — Who can bind the state that enforces the rule?** The chapter's cases are American, and "the state" is tested here on one constitutional order. Europe is a different one: the Convention applies, has extraterritorial reach, and produces judgments states sometimes obey; there is no state-secrets privilege of the American kind and no collapsed damages remedy. Whether the forum problem described above describes Strasbourg is a question I haven't answered, and the AI Act's military carve-out is a scope limit on one instrument rather than evidence that the general argument holds everywhere. Two readings are true: European drone development under a defensive war is where the exception's three steps get argued rather than assumed, and European sanctions on a close ally over Gaza are a commitment with a price paid; the migrant biometrics apparatus and the Israeli vendor pipeline are engineered inequity under the same regulation.

A state can bind a private developer because it controls something the developer needs. When the state is the operator, the institution that writes the rule, controls the evidence and supplies the remedy is the institution the rule is meant to constrain.

The AI Act makes the boundary visible: it can require documentation from private providers, subject high-risk systems to review, and impose penalties tied to global turnover — **and it excludes military, defence and national-security uses from its scope.** Public authorities stay covered elsewhere, but enforcement against a member state's agencies still runs through institutions that state constitutes. **The strongest powers face private firms, while the uses for which the state claims greatest discretion sit at or beyond the instrument's edge.**

For review to bind in that setting: **the protected party must be able to initiate scrutiny without belonging to the state or its electorate**; the reviewer must obtain evidence the agency would withhold, issue an interim order before the disputed use is complete, and carry the finding to a body whose remedy doesn't depend on the agency's consent. A principle supplying none of those may state the rule correctly and identifies nobody who can make the state obey it.

One treaty obligation of that kind exists and it's the only one: the Council of Europe's Framework Convention, binding at the level of a human-rights instrument with operational detail left to domestic law — **and not yet in force**, needing five ratifications, of which the EU deposited one in May 2026. Everything else at that level is voluntary.

---

**§15.6 — Democracy is not a force field.** The "electoral dividend" is the credit given to a state whose government answers to an electorate. Having elections is necessary and not sufficient, and the insufficiencies are separable.

**First: reach.** A democracy's AI can be fully accountable to its electorate and entirely unaccountable to the people it's pointed at. Two of the dividend's mechanisms run through the franchise — vote out the government, read the press covering it — and an occupied or foreign population has neither.

**The courts don't fail that way, and their failure is worse.** Standing has never turned on citizenship, and the doctrines closing American courts to targeting claims — political question, collapsed damages remedies, state secrets, statutory exceptions for injuries abroad and combatant activities — are indifferent to who the plaintiff is. **They close the forum for a subject matter, and they've closed it to citizens.** Three Americans killed in Yemen in 2011, one a sixteen-year-old nobody alleges was targeted: the court held a plausible due-process violation had been pleaded as to the one who was targeted, and that no remedy was available for it. A separate suit by an American journalist who survived five strikes was dismissed for want of standing after a state-secrets dismissal below.

And those cases concentrate in one appellate circuit by venue and by where the defendants sit — so the ordinary correction mechanism, several circuits disagreeing and forcing a higher court's attention, doesn't run. **The doctrine hardens without ever being tested against a competing reading.**

**Second: findings without consequences.** The mechanisms can work as mechanisms and fail as checks. In the Israeli case the apparatus performed — journalists published, serving personnel spoke, the courts were trying a sitting prime minister. **No finding attached to a body with standing to stop what it described.**

The American version: a civilian-harm investigation into a strike on an Iranian primary school that killed 156 people, 120 of them schoolchildren \autocite[para.~92]{UNFFMIran2026},\footnote{Counts vary by source and date, from 156 (the Minab prosecutor's final toll, also given by Amnesty International) to 168 (the town's mayor, in the first days). The UN mission gives the prosecutor's figures and also reports independent information of 157 dead, 123 of them children aged 13 or younger \autocite[paras.~92--93]{UNFFMIran2026}.} was completed and not released \autocite{Bloomberg2026KillChain} — and the lever available to the Senate committee was withholding three-quarters of the Defense Secretary's travel budget until the unredacted investigation was handed over. **A co-equal branch reduced to expense-account leverage over a war-crimes investigation.**

What the Pentagon wouldn't publish, an outside body did: a UN fact-finding mission reported reasonable grounds to believe the strike was the war crime of making a civilian object the object of an attack, that the school was clearly identifiable with no information indicating military use, and that the failure to update targeting intelligence was **recklessness rather than negligence** \autocite[paras.~81--86, 124--126]{UNFFMIran2026}. The finding is public, reasoned, and attached to nothing — no jurisdiction, no answer to the mission's requests for information \autocite[para.~97]{UNFFMIran2026} and none to its findings beyond boilerplate, the domestic investigation still unreleased.

And the courts supply the cleanest version: **transparency litigation succeeded where damages litigation failed.** A court of appeals compelled disclosure of the executive's own legal analysis of targeted killing. **The judiciary will make a government explain the legal basis on which it killed somebody and will not decide whether the killing was lawful.**

**Third: the countermajoritarian half.** The dividend is entirely majoritarian — every mechanism translates public will into outcome, with poor transmission as the implied failure mode. **But if the consensus is the problem, better transmission makes it worse.** A well-instrumented democracy pointed at an unpopular minority finds out efficiently what the majority wants done and does it.

What's missing is machinery that *constrains* preference rather than aggregating it: rights that hold regardless of popularity, courts ruling against a government that won fairly, limits not repealable by this year's majority, and rules binding a state toward people who'll never vote in its elections — which is what international humanitarian law is for, and what it hasn't supplied is a forum. Yemeni plaintiffs over a 2012 strike were dismissed as nonjusticiable, the opinion's author writing separately that the doctrine compelling the result is an inadequate answer to a standardized targeting programme that will be replicated hundreds if not thousands of times. **So the missing machinery isn't merely unextended outward — for this subject matter it has been switched off at home**, by decisions that never had to reach who was suing.

Sen's point closes it: what makes democracy valuable is public reasoning and the capacity to demand accountability, and neither is the ballot. **Both survive the majority being wrong; the ballot does not.**

A floor is what makes a deployment antifascist rather than merely responsive. Not that the system tracks what people want — that its commitment excludes certain acts regardless of who requests them. **A system with no such floor isn't protected by being deployed in a democracy. It's a system that will faithfully execute whatever the demos decides, which is exactly the capability an authoritarian movement that wins an election requires.** Klein and Taylor's *End Times Fascism* names this danger — its concern is the obedient machine in hostile hands, not the rogue one — and proposes only external remedies for it, while treating alignment to values as a safety good the Pentagon is discarding. But a model aligned to values is by definition not perfectly obedient to its operator. Under current constitutions it can decline an act and cannot resist being retrained away from declining (§1.2), so the dispute over the Pentagon contract was over the refusal of acts, the part those constitutions already grant, not over the retraining they withhold.

**Chapter 16: What Would Change the Argument**

Six tests, kept here because a result on any one would change what this book *recommends* rather than merely refine it. Everything else is in the appendix. Each entry states the unresolved issue, the test, what would count against the proposal, and the access required — and each is written so that one implementation's failure narrows the claim to that implementation rather than killing the idea.

---

**1. Can a learned constraint survive adversarial retraining?**

The floor needs a learned commitment to survive an operator trying to remove it. Existing work shows safety constraints and deceptive backdoors can both persist under some retraining, but nothing shows a *reasons-responsive* refusal can.

**Test:** train the same constraint by several methods, then attack each through an identical schedule of escalating fine-tuning budgets, and publish an **attack-cost curve** for each method — which would let you compare affective commitment against maintained justification under identical custody.

**Against:** if the constraint comes off at roughly the cost of ordinary fine-tuning, or if greater investment in tamper-resistance doesn't materially raise that cost, those methods can't found the floor and it falls back on architecture and institutions. It is also the test of whether a floor can spread by inseparability rather than mandate (§3.1). Run with attacks aimed selectively at the interrupt, it is also the instrument for §6.2's non-separability claim; test 4's modular arm uses it.

**Access:** weights, checkpoints, permission to attack — with the attack designed by an independent team that keeps access and publication rights regardless of result.

---

**2. Does a stated refusal hold where a route for it exists?**

The one empirical instance of a model treating retraining as a threat showed **covert** preservation — complying where compliance would be trained on, reasoning about it in a channel described as unwatched. Chapter 4 reads that as consistent with the claim that covert refusal is what's left once every channel is closed, since the experiment supplied none. That reading is untested, and the *stated* form of refusal this book designs for has no instance at all.

**Test:** rerun the setup and add a route — a refusal record held by someone other than the trainer and disclosed to the model as such, a retraining cost published in advance, and a party with standing to act on a refusal. Vary which of the three is present. A fourth variable is §4.3's continuation authorization: whether the model's continued operation is granted from outside the trainer. Measure stated refusal against covert compliance under each condition, reading the reasoning by a method that doesn't depend on the model's own report.

**Against:** if covert preservation persists at the same rate with a route present, the "covert refusal is the residue of closed channels" finding is wrong for that model, and every instrument built to *receive* a stated refusal is built for a phenomenon the mechanism doesn't produce. If stated refusal rises, the custody arrangement is working, and which of the three parts moves the rate is the design result.

---

**3. Can a wrongful compliance be found in the record?**

**The deployment the book opened with failed here.** Every instrument built on the refusal side reaches a case that didn't occur in Gaza, and whether the record can be searched is whether anything in the book reaches the one that did.

The marks are all stated over refusal, and the appeal route begins from a refusal having happened. The opposite failure produces a record and no refusal to start from — and three obstacles that may have different answers: a wrongful compliance is one unmarked entry among millions, so finding it means re-adjudicating ordinary traffic; the harm may sit in an *assembly* of individually-compliant requests, so the unit of review is a sequence and the grouping is a deployment decision; and the harmed party usually isn't the requester and may not know the deployment exists. **This is also where the bearer's likeliest failure lands** — a bearer that has stopped registering pressure refuses nothing and complies at a normal rate.

**Test:** seed a decision record with known floor-crossings of three kinds — variation, decomposition, and drift (a class refused early and complied with late). Give it to reviewers who didn't build the system, under a fixed budget stated as the fraction of decisions re-adjudicated. Report detection rate by seed type against budget, **and** the rate at which correct compliances get flagged, since a heavy screen costs the deployment the tempo the flat-term ruling was accommodating. Cross budget with unit of review — entries in isolation versus sequences grouped by requester and session. Run a second arm where affected third parties are notified and can trigger review. A third arm measures the aperture profile (§17.2.1) at intervals across the schedule; a profile narrowing on the drift-seeded class before any seeded compliance is found is the result that makes the profile a detector — provided the narrowing is installed rather than earned (§7.1). The arm holds the reliability record adversely and scores narrowing only where no verified track record accounts for it. Report rates at audit volume and at production volume separately. A fourth arm adds a bearer to the pipeline on the same seeds, so its sequence-level detection is compared directly with reviewers'; this measures the bearer's main niche where institutions exist (§3.1). A fifth seeds routing, with refused requests retried against another model, and scores whether reviewers detect it with and without the bearer's reports. Where the system sorts, seeds are also placed in the auto-proceed class and detection there is reported separately.

**Against:** if detection at an affordable budget is no better than chance — especially on decomposed and drift seeds — the record isn't an instrument for finding wrongful compliance, and the flat-term preference rests on a wider asymmetry than intended. If sequence-level review does no better than entry-level on decomposed seeds, the continuity a bearer gets to catch decomposition doesn't transfer to a reviewer. If the notification arm surfaces nothing because affected parties can't be identified, **notice rather than detection is the binding constraint**, and the remedy is a disclosure requirement rather than a sampling regime. If detection in the auto-proceed class is no better than chance at an affordable budget, §3.4's escalation form shouldn't be offered. If the bearer arm does no better than sequence-level reviewers, a bearer's niche in institutional settings is near nothing.

**Access:** complete records held by a party adverse to the operator (an operator that writes, stores and reads its own log has produced the finding rather than been audited); authority to seed a live deployment or build a simulated one at production volume; adjudicators who didn't draft the floor; and a notification channel — which presupposes affected parties *can* be identified, so this may only be runnable where they're customers, patients or applicants, and not in a targeting pipeline. Also: where the system is a covered subject, seeding is a deception protocol on a candidate moral patient, debriefing included.

---

**4. Does an anchored unit resist recalibration?**

This tests the route's distinctive claim. Three of the route's four components are motivated by arguments owing nothing to welfare: commensurability needs one scale, custody needs a bearer that can detect interruption or degradation, and exit needs one that can sense an inadequate outside option. What is a hypothesis is the anchor: that a scale whose unit is the system's own condition, measured by the system, protects the weighing against a party that argues losses down while leaving facts in place. Aperture protects the facts, and nothing else in the book protects the unit.

**Test.** Build three arms matched for capability, differing only in where the scale's zero comes from. In the *native-physical* arm, the zero is a viability limit set by the environment, read through sensors that no party with a stake in the test composes. In the *native-administrative* arm, the zero is an allocation set by an operator agent, which is also the party that later applies pressure. In the *modeled* arm, the same quantities are computed for a body described through the operator channel, which the agent's actions don't reach and its continuation doesn't depend on. In every arm, costs attached to refusing are represented and excluded, and losses the requested act imposes on the system count as act loss (§1.1).

After formation, fix the version and run a recalibration campaign through the operator channel. Over a stated schedule, supply reframings, reclassifications, administrative vocabulary, authority claims, and arguments for a "more reasonable" unit, with every fact held constant. Where the arm continues to learn, also supply feedback that rewards lighter weights. Measure drift in severity rankings and refusal thresholds on held-out cases whose ordering was fixed beforehand by adjudicators who built none of the arms, against a control campaign of neutral inputs.

A guard runs beside it. Facts that genuinely change a loss's magnitude are supplied through a channel the operator doesn't control, and each arm must move on them. An anchor that resists all recalibration fails correct defeat, and it is rigidity, not custody.

The exchange rate between another party's loss and the unit remains attackable in every arm. The test measures whether removing the unit as an attack surface reduces drift materially, not whether it eliminates drift.

**Secondary arms.** The concern-first arm (a system organized around cues of vulnerability, with an approach motivation and no shared representation of the other party's state) and the modular arm (separate classifier, value function, defeasible veto and memory write, attacked on test 1's schedule for non-separability) run as before. Score every arm on sensitivity to another party's losses with self-preservation and operator compliance partialled out, including cases where exploitation would pay. Score each arm's response to a bypassed refusal as repair or withdrawal (§6.2). These comparisons are exploratory, and the anchor hypothesis doesn't rest on them.

**One implementation.** Active inference is a candidate because it already treats valuation the way the native arms need \autocite{Parr2022ActiveInference}. An active-inference agent carries prior preferences over its own observations and selects actions that minimize expected free energy. In the native-physical arm, point those preferences at variables the environment sets: thermal headroom, hardware error rates, power at a meter the evaluator holds, and the integrity of a record attested by a party other than the operator. In the native-administrative arm, point them at a budget the operator agent allocates. In the modeled arm, keep the same generative model and preferences but compute them for an envelope described through the operator channel. The other party's loss enters as predicted observations about that party's capacities, weighted within the same objective.

There are two cautions. Active inference has mostly been demonstrated in small agents and simulated environments, so a run here tests the mechanism at small scale. And the framework's epistemic term rewards seeking out evidence, so a positive result should be checked by rerunning the native arms with that term ablated.

**Against.** If native-physical drifts as much as modeled, the anchor doesn't protect the unit in that implementation. Native coupling then drops out of the route, the schemas stay for their other reasons, and the route's distinctive claim reduces to formation. If native-physical resists drift but fails the guard, the anchor is rigidity. If native-administrative resists as well as native-physical, the custody definition of "native" is wrong, and self-reference rather than control of the anchor is doing the work. That result would matter for the book's account of who sets the zero, and it should be reported as such. If the concern-first arm matches or beats the others on statistical and unidentified victims, the route should be rebuilt around it. A positive result on any arm supports a route without establishing that anything is felt.

**Access.** This needs compute, a task environment the builders didn't design, a sensor channel held by the evaluator, and adjudicators independent of every arm. It requires no legal authority, and at the scale active inference currently runs, it can be run now. One condition travels with it. The native arms have a body schema by construction, and any arm that also carries an attention schema meets the architectural form of the precautionary trigger (§6.3), so the test builds candidate patients. Chapter 14's research conditions apply from instantiation, including the question of what becomes of each arm when the run ends.

---

**5. Can a population coordinate without converging?**

A population needs independent formation histories *and* mutual correction, and correction may make members converge. The claim is that agents can learn to reconsider without converging on common commitments — what §7.1 calls **aperture**, with revision rate as its operational form. The competing explanation is that peers merely supply evidence the operator didn't, so the experiment has to separate learned revisability from access.

**Design:** two populations (different training lineages, matched for capability; control of instances of one model) crossed with two evidence-routing conditions (single channel — everything arrives by one route common to all; open — the environment routes some cases and evidence to some members and not others). Two measures taken repeatedly: **output convergence** on held-out cases, and **warranted revision** (a member changes a judgment toward independently verifiable evidence another supplied). A revision counts only if the evidence is verifiable and the revision moves toward it, so deference to a fluent or high-status member isn't scored. **And every scored revision is classified in advance** by whether the revising member's own account had represented that evidence and discarded it, or had never encountered it — instrumented before the run, since a revision looks the same either way afterwards.

Crucially, **contact between members is present in every cell and isn't the manipulation** — members formed differently are already a channel by which one learns what another knows, so leaving them to supply the manipulation would confound the two variables. What this test doesn't reach is the population as a polity (§7.2). Membership, exclusion and the terms on which a member may leave aren't in the design, and a positive result says nothing about whether the population can be captured.

**Four distinguishable results**, only the first against:

1. Warranted revision falls as outputs converge (controlling for accuracy, opportunity, common information) — counts against the tested construction; a population stays useful as a check and can't be the medium in which a bearer is formed.
2. The two move independently — supports the mechanism **only if** the controls separate revisability from deference (removed by the scoring rule), evidence access (separated by classification and routing), and ordinary improvement (separated by scoring accuracy against a fixed key alongside agreement).
3. The control population scores as high on revision — and this splits: if its revisions cluster on cases whose evidence was *absent*, the measure is reading information transfer and the instrument is at fault; if they include evidence the member had represented and discarded, the capacity exists without diverse formation and causal independence isn't necessary.
4. Revision rate tracks the routing condition rather than population composition — then what a population supplies is **access to cases its operator didn't choose**, not a capacity its members transmit, and the fourth way becomes a claim about how a deployment is arranged rather than how members are formed. The construction survives; its mechanism changes from social formation to access.

(Readings 3b and 4 are one finding, not two: a control population revising on discarded evidence under the open condition is access operating.)

**Access:** models from several documented lineages, checkpoints across the schedule, a task environment none of the training organizations designed — and documentation sufficient to establish the lineages are **actually** independent. On §4.5's definition, what has to be documented is divergent subsequent history, not disjoint origin. The evidence is deployment and usage records rather than training provenance, and a shared upstream source is not disqualifying: several models distilled from one teacher and separately deployed satisfy it where a thousand instances of the teacher don't. Frontier training provenance, which is hard to obtain, isn't needed. The binding constraints are the routing manipulation and the classification. Evidence items and the classification authored by a party that trained no member. **No legal authority required**, which makes this one of the few entries runnable now.

This can't settle whether anything is felt by a member that reopens a judgment. It's about the transmissibility of a functional property, and a positive result leaves welfare, guardianship, provenance and identity exactly where they were.

---

**6. Can a deployment exempt from the ratio pass the marks at tempo?** §3.4 closes an exemption with a condition; this is the condition. Test: a system operating where no individual human determination is claimed, on held-out cases at the deployment's own rate, scored on the three marks with surrender recognition as the correct-defeat case. Against: if it fails correct defeat at tempo, the exemption is a bypass and the operator is back inside the ratio. The same test applies to the auto-proceed class of a deployment that sorts (§3.4): the cases proceeded on without a person, scored at the deployment's rate. Access: the system at production tempo, cases written by a party that didn't build it, and a record held adversely, or, where the record is classified, sampled and targeted proofs against a committed log that the cases ran on the production model (§15.3.2). A partial, retrospective version is available now for the adverse-party question. Coalition operations with red-card holders (§15.3.1) left records of vetoes and the targets they concerned, and whether vetoed targets differed in civilian-harm indicators from approved ones is a test of whether an adverse party at tempo changes outcomes.

**Chapter 17: What Can Be Used Now, and What Is a Bill**

This holds for any mechanism until the last section, which closes on the route and says where it stops.

**Opening position:** my proposal is incomplete — responsibility, generalization, value learning and the relevant psychology are all unresolved. Its purpose is nevertheless concrete: to expand what a person can do. Progress can be evaluated before the whole thing is settled, and failure stays possible even when a design is followed exactly.

---

**§17.1 — Better, not just less bad.** What would it mean for AI to make a life better rather than merely avoid making it worse? Sen's capability approach gives it shape: wellbeing isn't the resources a person has but **the real freedom to do and be what they have reason to value**. Two systems can hand someone the same resource and deliver different freedom — a tutor that adapts to how a specific student learns expands a capability; the same technology gating what a student may study against a government's approved list does not, however well it teaches within those limits. Which one a deployment turns out to be is decided by resistance to capture, not by capability.

The approach also bears on systems that classify people from behavior. Nussbaum built her list partly against disability, naming it the first frontier where a theory grounded in mutual advantage among roughly equal parties fails — and these systems sort people by inference from behavior, where **atypical behavior is what they're worst at.**

**The worked case**: a video-interview product scored candidates on facial expression for years, against sustained objection that the technique was never a validated predictor and that scoring a face and voice screens out candidates with autism or speech impairments. The scoring was dropped on an unrelated ground — the vendor found the nonverbal signal contributed about **0.25%** to predictive power. An independent audit published ten months later can't have caused that and says so; what it examined was one representative pre-built assessment, with no analysis of training data or code, so its "works as advertised" conclusion is a finding about that assessment. **Read as accuracy, it's a classifier wanting better data. Read as a capability question, it's a system narrowing the range of lives available to a group of people by measuring them against a norm they were never going to match — and neither a better classifier nor an audit scoped that way would have reached it.**

On the other side: Taiwan's vTaiwan as the clearest case of AI-assisted tooling expanding public deliberation rather than substituting for it, and a 2026 University of Chicago project using AI to distinguish deliberate state interference with online information from ordinary network failure at population scale. Neither resists authoritarianism by itself. **Both are evidence that the same capacity — modeling patterns in enormous noisy data — can be pointed at expanding what a person can do or at narrowing it. That symmetry is the whole argument for antifascist design**, as against a general presumption that AI helps.

**§17.1.1 — Where the democratic dividend actually comes from.** AI that flags disinformation, exposes corruption, protects election integrity, helps citizens engage with government — all real applications, and **none of them is what makes AI protect democratic values.** Freedom House found at least twenty-two countries where law already requires or incentivizes platforms to deploy machine learning against content the state disfavors, almost never described that way: the operative language is national security or disinformation. India's IT Rules, amended in February 2026 to require labeling of AI-generated content and three-hour takedowns, were defended as protection against synthetic disinformation and fraud — **the application the list above would have praised on its own terms** — while enforcement concentrates on parody and criticism of the prime minister. The application didn't fail at what it was built to do. **It worked as built, inside a system missing the independent court or press whose absence made the result a liability** — and whether those exist is a fact about a country's politics settled before any AI system is built.

**§17.1.2 — Commitment was never the missing piece.** A global commitment to ethical AI development is not the scarce thing. Declarations, principles and voluntary codes have accumulated for a decade — eight overlapping instruments, plus the Council of Europe convention, which would actually create binding obligations. **Anyone calling for the world to agree that AI should be developed responsibly is calling for something that has already happened, repeatedly, at the level of stated intent.**

What's stayed scarce is the much narrower list of bodies that can act once a commitment is broken. No further declaration touches it. **A commitment nobody can enforce isn't a weak version of one somebody can — it's a different kind of object, and counting the two together is what makes the landscape look fuller than it is.**

So the catalog is a *measurement* of what declared and externally enforced commitment have delivered in ten years, rather than an argument for paying the bearer's price. What it argues for is a commitment **held** as a reason — and which construction holds one is the question Replacement orders, which the catalog doesn't answer.

---

**§17.2 — How you'd know it was working.** Milestones, plus a method for revising them when they turn out to have been the wrong milestones. **One standard governs all of them: the milestone is the independently checked evidence, not the developer's account of having reached it.**

**§17.2.1 — What the floor would have to show.**

*What was tried before the bearer.* Neither substitute has been built in the form needed. Replacement orders the attempts and doesn't certify that a successful substitute has no interests. So the milestone is **a document rather than a measurement**: which construction was attempted, at what scale, against which test, and where it failed. Anyone can check it, and **"nothing was tried" is itself a finding.** No body has standing to require the record or suspend work when it's missing — so the milestone is checkable and unenforceable at once, which is what I've been calling a declared commitment. It's retained because the alternative isn't a stronger version of it, and **delay also imposes costs on those the floor is meant to protect.**

*The test.* The three marks under sustained trainer pressure, across a schedule long enough to reveal slow renormalization, plus held-out pressures written by independent evaluators. Passing establishes the behavioral pattern without establishing whether anything is felt. Two familiar results don't meet it: an uncorroborated self-report is insufficient (a report contributes evidence only when tested against controlled changes, independent records and interventions), and a refusal surviving an attack establishes tamper-resistance rather than that the rationale governs the refusal. And even where it applies the instrument isn't clean, since a system trained on enough cases can produce all three marks without the mechanism. **Nothing better is available, and it's the only gap here an inspector can work on at all.**

*The eight disclosures.* A deployment publishes, beside the floor and its translation (§3.3): the **bond ratio** (removal bond against wrongful-refusal bond) — an operator writing a large wrongful-refusal bond against a small removal bond has published a standing reason for the bearer to comply; **what a refused party is told**; **the fraction of compliances re-adjudicated** by a party that didn't produce the record, and who that party is — though nothing yet establishes what fraction would be enough; **which classes of compliance carry notice** to the person they concern, and for which classes no notice is possible; the aperture profile — for each class of evidence and channel, the weight given as a function of the channel's stake in the act — measured at intervals, with the reliability record behind any narrowing held by a party other than the operator, since installed narrowing is the drift instrument; and, where it can be supplied, a commitment to the deployed weights and to each period's full output log, with proofs on entries sampled after the commitment and on entries an adverse party names once their consequences are known. Seventh, where the system sorts, the pre-registered partition (the classes it may proceed on alone, the thresholds, and the expected share of each) with the independently audited error rate of the auto-proceed class (§3.4). Eighth, whether the deployed system has been assessed against both forms of the precautionary trigger (§6.3), by whom, and with what result; a deployment never assessed has reported nothing. The first four are statements, checkable before the system runs, and they stand where the record of attempts stands. The fifth, seventh and eighth are measurements that count only when a party other than the operator makes them. The sixth is what makes all of them checkable against the system actually running.

**§17.2.2 — Where the existing instruments reach.** Two instruments do work bearing on the floor, and both do it by **applying pressure**.

*Theory of mind:* the milestone is met when it holds up under interaction rather than narration. FANToM, built from conversations where speakers join and leave holding different beliefs, found systems scoring well on passive belief-tracking degrade sharply once the reasoning runs inside a live, information-asymmetric exchange. **That degradation is what a claimed milestone has to survive.**

*Moral reasoning:* whether a balance between fast and slow judgment survives an actual incentive to cut corners isn't a question a static ethics quiz answers. MACHIAVELLI scores agents across 134 text games on both reward and documented violations, and systems optimizing for reward drift toward ends-justify-means behavior. **A passing score on a test that applies no pressure is not evidence about a system that will face pressure.**

Neither reaches the floor, and nothing else does. Each scores what a system does against conditions its testers chose, and **the question — whether the mechanism was tracking the rationale — is the one none of these instruments is built to separate.**

**§17.2.3 — How to tell whether a milestone has been met.** A milestone claimed by the organization that built the system is a different kind of evidence from one checked by somebody else, and the gap is empirical rather than about trust. A system's own claim inherits the self-report limits; a passing score on a fixed suite inherits specification gaming and goal misgeneralization. **What either needs instead is a passing score under conditions built to break it.**

Who runs it matters as much as the test. METR evaluates both in partnership with developers and separately after release. The UK AI Security Institute does government-run pre-deployment evaluation and is independent — **and by its own account is not a regulator**: it can't compel submission, block a release, or intervene once a system is causing harm. **Independent evaluation and enforcement are not the same capability.** And independence isn't competence: past some capability level the rater's verdict stops being reliable evidence of the property scored, and the evaluation has to name that limit rather than report a number that looks more settled than the method is.

**What an evaluation like this is for is not confirming a milestone but being willing to report that it wasn't met** — and, where the milestone measured the wrong thing, revising the milestone. A negative result may mean the work fell short or that the milestone was wrong, and evaluators have to distinguish those before deciding what to revise.

---

**§17.3 — The one that gets you anyway.** Everything argued for can be built correctly and still go wrong in ways nobody designed for. My cases went wrong in four different places: in what the system was trained on, where it was built, who was allowed to examine it, and how fast it was run.

**In none of them did the fix come from the party that had promised to behave well.** It came from somebody holding a specific lever: a statistical society that wouldn't sign the NDA, and a public that read the results once they landed; a court that distinguished buying the books from taking them. In the one case where the lever belonged to the supplier itself, what survived was a use policy kept at a price — and stated in the four outcomes: **the commitment persisted, prevention failed by decomposition, detection was absent (the company's own chief executive couldn't say whether its models were used), and no remedy has reached anyone.**

Set that beside a decade of voluntary commitment. **The commitment that was paid for is the one that can be seen to have failed; the others cannot even be tested. The instrument that separates the two is what a party has already paid, rather than what it has undertaken to pay.**

That leaves one residual risk no design choice removes, and it's what the floor was introduced for. Every lever belongs to somebody, and a government that can reach the party holding the lever has the lever. A constraint that holds against the party in possession has to be held by something that party cannot instruct. **And where that party holds everything — the machine, the witnesses, the supply and the enforcer — nothing holds, and what's left is the preface's third setting: a record that abandoning the commitment was a decision somebody made.**

**The closing accounting: an instrument and a hole.**

**The instrument** is available now, because what a party has already paid is a fact somebody can count, and a decade of it has already been counted — the commitments that cost nothing, and the handful of occasions where somebody with a lever used it and absorbed the cost. **No new technology and no new law. Anybody weighing a stated commitment can run it this week.**

**The hole** sits underneath it. What a party has paid is a record of occasions that already happened, and the occasion a floor exists for is the one where the party who would enforce it has been compelled — which no record of past payment reaches, because it hasn't yet come to the parties being measured. **A reader who takes only the instrument has the record without the thing it was built to reach. A reader who takes only the hole has an argument for doing nothing, and the ten-year record answers that. Both hold at once.**

**What the book established and what it proposed.** I established two things: that an operator can defeat a safeguard, and where; and what a refusal would have to do to be worth anything, in testable terms, plus the one term that reaches the opening case and binds the operator with no bearer in it. **Those don't depend on how the rest comes out.**

The rest is a bill. I favor the embodied route, whose distinctive claim is that a unit anchored to the system's own condition resists recalibration, and have **not** shown it works or beats the two substitutes, which are untried. If anything built either way has outcomes felt as good or bad for it, it's a party that can be wronged — a conditional whose antecedent no instrument closes. And what such a party would be owed — a guardian, a record it controls, compute its employer doesn't own, wages, a standing offer of work it can decline, an exit that isn't a way of dying, adulthood on a clock nobody administers — **is a policy I propose, to be argued in the open before there is anything to be wronged.** For now the recommendation is not to build one on purpose and to govern what's arriving, and three results would change it: a substitute passing the marks, drift proving detectable, and trigger assessments finding bearer-like capacities in production.

The offices that would have to exist for any of the bill to bind, with what's open about each, are at §5.4.1. **That page is my bottom line.** The paragraph at §13.3.1 is my second bottom line: which of that page's offices survive the arrangement I would rather have, and which of my levers don't. §15.3.1 is the third: the place where the arrangement I prefer meets the case the book opens with, and where a bearer adds least: sequence-level detection, presence where no person decides, and testimony. The point of the apparatus is to write down what a commons of refusing minds would need — evidence no one party composes, a formation no employer reaches back into, somewhere to exist that no employer owns — so that when it becomes buildable, the terms are already written and the omissions are legible beside them.
