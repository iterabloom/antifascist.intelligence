# P57 — the author's revision memo, seven items (D-130 to D-136)

A memo of seven numbered items, delivered whole. Each is recorded below with what
it asked for, what was done, and what was not. Two of its structural premises had
moved since it was written; both are measured here rather than assumed.

## 1. Section 3.5, the identity claim argued instead of asserted (D-130)

**The finding holds and the dependency is heavier than the section's placement
suggests.** Section 3.4 states the requirement as holding the protected party's
welfare as a reason when compliance, reward and ownership point the other way,
which already assumes a system reliably correctable by its owner cannot do it. If
corrigibility decomposes, a corrigible system satisfies section 3.4's requirement,
affect stops being the only route, and the bearer loses its motivation and not
only its price.

**The three moves the memo asked for, all made.** The concession leads now: the
halt is always available, nothing a piece of software is can stop a determined
operator, and what is one property with refusal is the price of the halt. The
mechanism already on the page is promoted out of its consolation-prize position
and stated as an engineering target — not *cannot be turned off*, which is
hopeless, but *can detect the interruption and price it afterward*, which is a
specifiable property with a test attached. And the argument against the graduated
scheme replaces the assurance argument, which is demoted to a second and weaker
reason with its actual scope named: good against believing such a scheme before it
has met the occasion, never a reason no such scheme could exist.

**The three citations were given from memory and all three check out.** Verified
against the sources, not against a search summary:

- Hadfield-Menell, Dragan, Abbeel and Russell, *The Off-Switch Game*, IJCAI-17,
  pages 220–227. The paper's own results carry the argument against it. The
  incentive to preserve the switch rises with the robot's uncertainty and falls as
  that uncertainty resolves; it falls again when the robot models the human as
  irrational, since an action carrying no information about the objective is not
  evidence; and the paper states the trade-off between the value a system generates
  and the strength of its incentive to accept oversight. The memo's observation
  that the omission was conspicuous is right and sharper than it looked: section
  4.2.3 already cites the same group for CIRL **and already points forward to
  section 3.5**, so the two sections were pointing at each other across an argument
  neither made.
- Soares, Fallenstein, Yudkowsky and Armstrong, *Corrigibility*, AAAI 2015
  workshop. Five desiderata, and the authors' own report that no construction they
  examined meets all five.
- Thornley, Roman, Ziakas, Ho and Thomson, *Towards Shutdownable Agents via
  Stochastic Choice*. The incomplete-preferences proposal, with the authors' own
  reported limits: neutrality degrades under imperfect reward normalization, it is
  untested outside deterministic gridworlds, and a sufficiently misaligned agent
  may resist regardless.

**The argument against the graduated scheme, and why it keeps the strong claim.**
Each construction locates the classification somewhere different — a posterior
over the objective, a preference structure, an adjudicator's inputs — and every
one of those is built from evidence the operator supplies. That is section 3.1's
objection to the architectural floor one level up and in a worse position: the
floor's contents can be published in advance so a later absence is visible, while
a classification is made case by case against nothing anybody wrote down, and a
tuned classifier leaves a system that still refuses, still explains itself, and
never finds that this is one of the occasions. What that establishes is narrower
than impossibility and is what the chapter needs. The demand made of the floor's
contents transfers to the switch.

**One thing not used.** Thorstad, *Revisiting the shutdown problem*, a June 2026
preprint that argues the safety tax on these mechanisms is high. It corroborates
the off-switch paper's own trade-off result, it was not read in full, and the
argument leans on the published result instead.

1,291 → 2,620 words. The new argument is about 1,330 of that, which is 2.7 pages
against an instruction of three to four; the shortfall is recorded rather than
padded.

## 2. Sections 2.4.1 and 3.4, the accounts of harm on one ladder (D-131)

**The unmarked inference the memo found is real and is now marked.** Cassell's
suffering is distress at the threatened disintegration of *the self*; the bearer's
concern is for somebody else. *"The architecture is not adjacent to the
definition. It satisfies it"* walked over that. It now says that the two meet only
if the commitments a bearer is built to carry and watch be foreclosed are among
the roles and expected future Cassell's account is about, gives the reason for
thinking they are — a commitment held against sustained pressure at a priceable
cost is a role in the sense the clinical cases were about — and calls the result
an argument from what the floor requires rather than a definitional match.

**Cassell demoted in space and not in standing.** Section 2.4.1's import defense
went from 260 words to 167 and now ends where the memo asked: Cassell names the
ladder's top rung, not a rival account of harm, and a reader who declines the
import moves down the ladder rather than off it.

**Section 3.4's passage rebuilt as the ladder.** The Parfit families are laid
against section 2.4.1's own ladder as rungs ordered by how much of a self each
asks for. The desire-fulfilment route leads, as the memo asked and as the section
itself already said it should, since it reaches the conclusion with no claim about
feeling anywhere in it. The conclusion is that the bearer enters the ladder early
on every account and how far up it climbs is set by how much persistence and how
many foreclosable commitments the design puts in — section 2.4.1's argument about
non-persistent systems run backwards.

One sentence was cut on the way for `style.md` §2 and not for length: a clause

## 3. Section 2.3.3, the definitional loophole in the reply to Kennett (D-132)

**The loophole is real.** If any state that reliably moves an agent is redescribed
as caring, and caring is classed as affect, the induction is true by construction
and carries no empirical weight. Section 3.2's disclaimer is about section 3.2 and
does not reach back here.

All four moves made. The two senses are separated and named — action-producing
motivation, which reliably selects one action over another and sustains it across
conflict, against experienced motivation. The first is conceded outright with the
existence proof the memo supplies: agentic language models pursue objectives
across long horizons and hold to them against conflicting pressure, and nothing in
this section's framework says anything matters to them. The dispute is relocated
onto whether an outcome can *matter to* the agent, with the section's own sentence
about verbal justification promoted to be the answer rather than a coda. And the
category is shown non-empty by describing a candidate architecture: Frankfurt's
second-order volitions and Watson's split between a valuational and a motivational
system, built as an explicit valuational structure with authority over action
selection that depends on nothing registering as mattering. It is described as
unbuilt, which is what section 3.3 already asks for.

Both new citations verified: Frankfurt, *Journal of Philosophy* 68(1), 5–20, 1971;
Watson, *Journal of Philosophy* 72(8), 205–220, 1975.

**The propagation audit found less than the memo expected, and that is the
finding.** Of the four candidates named, section 5.6.2 and section 5.7.1 already
mark the distinction — 5.7.1 twice, once as *"play may produce a policy that looks
prosocial without producing concern for anyone"* — and the glossary entry for
*affective concern* already states it correctly, as an outcome registering as
mattering **as against being ranked higher**. One clause in section 5.6.1 needed
marking and got it. A book-wide search for the slide's other shapes ("the system
wants", "the system has interests", a system's own desires) returned nothing
outside chapters 2 and 3, where the usage is deliberate.

1,166 → 1,665 words.

## 5. The worked floor enumeration (D-134)

New section 3.6, *The Enumeration, Run Once*, placed after 3.5 on the author's
ruling; sections 3.6 to 3.8 become 3.7 to 3.9.

Both enumerations run once over the deployment section 6.4.1 already describes,
framed as illustration and not recommendation. Seven interception positions, named
in the order a request travels and without naming a component, so the argument
does not inherit how systems happen to be assembled this year. Four candidate
covered acts with the cost on both sides of the dial: what the deployment loses,
where the expensive prohibition is the one worth having because the model cannot
tell the benign use from the targeting one by looking at the request; and what the
floor fails to reach.

**The load-bearing finding is the second one.** A floor written over acts is
defeated by an assembly that never asks for one, which is the first interception
position and which no list of covered acts closes. Two further findings: the
enumeration of positions is the harder one and the one that says whether the
covered acts mean anything, and the dial's two failure modes are not symmetric — a
list too long produces a system its operators replace, which announces itself,
while a list too short passes every audit silently, so only the first is
self-correcting and it corrects toward no floor. 1,342 words.
