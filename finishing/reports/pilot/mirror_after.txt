2.2.1. The Role of Empathy and Compassion in Human Social Behavior
Empathy and compassion serve as the linchpins of human social behavior, underpinning cooperative interactions, helping foster meaningful relationships, and spurring altruistic actions. Both are multidimensional processes that involve the mental and emotional sharing of other individuals' experiences and feelings, as well as reacting to their needs with understanding and concern.

Empathy can further be classified into affective and cognitive variations. Affective empathy refers to our innate ability to share and mirror the emotions of others, essentially allowing us to "feel" their emotions. Cognitive empathy, on the other hand, corresponds to our capacity to discern and comprehend another individual's thoughts and emotions from their perspective. 

Compassion, considered a moral emotion, is often viewed as an evolutionary extension of empathy. Compassion manifests as a motivation to not only understand another person's distress but to actively alleviate it. It exudes a sense of value that transcends personal gain, cultivating a sense of universal responsibility.

To illustrate, a physician empathizing with a patient can discern their discomfort which facilitates compassion, pushing them to assuage the patient's distress. Similarly, a teacher's empathy towards a struggling student can catalyze compassion, prompting actionable support that enriches the learning environment.

Several distinct neural substrates in the human brain undergird the functionality of empathy and compassion. The limbic system, involved in processing emotions, plays a central part, and the mirror neuron system is often invoked as the mechanism by which one person simulates another's experience — a claim that has not survived contact with the evidence [[cite:C0275]], and which the box below takes up.

Mirror neurons: a discovery and its overshoot

In the early 1990s a laboratory in Parma was recording single neurons in the premotor cortex of macaque monkeys, in cells that fire when the animal reaches for and grasps an object. The cells also fired when the monkey merely watched someone else perform the same reach. The same neural code appeared to represent an action whether it was performed or observed, and the finding was formally published in 1996 [[cite:C0276]].

The interpretation that followed outran it. If observed actions are encoded by the cells that produce them, perhaps this was how one mind understands another — by simulating the movement and recognizing it from the inside. In the popular literature mirror neurons were described as the neurons that shaped civilization, and offered as the neural basis of empathy, language, cultural transmission, and imitation [[cite:C0277]].

Two problems accumulated. First, the single-cell recordings were made in macaques; in humans they were not, because recording individual neurons requires implanted electrodes, which are not placed for research purposes. Evidence for a human mirror system came instead from functional imaging, which measures regional blood flow and cannot establish that any particular neuron has mirror properties. When single-neuron recordings were eventually obtained in humans, from patients undergoing depth-electrode placement for epilepsy surgery, the results were suggestive but markedly less clean than the macaque data [[cite:C0278]].

Second, the broken-mirror hypothesis of autism — that autistic social difficulty arises from mirror system dysfunction — predicted reduced mirror-region activity during action observation, and repeatedly failed to find it. A 2009 review concluded that autistic people show no particular impairment in action understanding or imitation under explicit instruction, which were the abilities the hypothesis rested on [[cite:C0279]]. Later papers from the original group narrowed the claim considerably, treating mirror neurons as one component of a larger system rather than its explanation.

What survives is real and narrower. Mirror neurons exist in macaques; some analogue probably exists in humans; the system plausibly contributes to recognizing and predicting actions already within one's own motor repertoire. What it does not do is explain empathy by itself. The mentalizing network that supports reasoning about false beliefs is largely distinct from the motor regions where mirror neurons were found. Knowing what someone is doing and knowing what they believe are different computations, and the brain appears to run them in different places.

The episode is worth carrying into machine design for a reason beyond the neuroscience. A single vivid mechanism, well named, absorbed four distinct capacities that later had to be separated again. Architectures for artificial empathy are exposed to the same failure: a component that plausibly does one thing gets described as the seat of the whole faculty, and the description outlives its evidence.

 Compassion, conversely, primarily stimulates the prefrontal cortex, responsible for higher-order cognition and motivation, along with the release of oxytocin, a hormone pivotal for social bonding.

Cognitively, the ability to invoke empathy and compassion hinges on perspective-taking, emotional resonance, and emotion regulation capabilities. 'Perspective-taking' involves the cognitive skill of understanding another's views, experiences, and emotions. 'Emotional resonance' alludes to the process of sharing or reflecting another’s emotions, whereas 'emotion regulation' helps individuals manage their emotional responses to keep them poised and constructive.

Multiple approaches, such as exposure to diverse perspectives, empathetic listening, and mindfulness practices, can nurture and enhance empathy and compassion. For instance, perspective-taking exercises help individuals expand their empathetic understanding, allowing them to figuratively "step into another person’s shoes." Consequently, this not only elevates empathic understanding but aids in countering prejudice and promoting social harmony. 

In light of developing altruistic and anti-authoritarian AI, the role of empathy and compassion in human social behavior serves as an invaluable blueprint. Incorporating these foundational human traits into the fabric of AI systems could open avenues to more ethically-aligned, socially-responsible artificial entities. To replicate these empathetic and compassionate responses, AI developers must delve into the physiological, neurological, and psychological facets that drive these human behaviors. Understanding these intricacies can enable the creation of AI frameworks that mirror the empathy and compassion of human consciousness, grounding the behavior of AI systems in the principles of altruism and compassionate concern for societal good.
