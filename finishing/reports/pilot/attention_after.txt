3.1.2.3.1.1. Attention
In 1999 two psychologists made a short video of two teams passing basketballs, one team in white shirts and one in black, and asked people to count the passes made by the white team. Halfway through, a person in a full gorilla costume walked into the center of the frame, faced the camera, thumped their chest, and walked out. The gorilla was on screen for nine seconds. About half the viewers, asked afterward whether they had seen anything unusual, said no. Shown the same video again, they were astonished [[cite:C0269]].

The gorilla was not a visual trick. It was lit, centered, and in plain view. The viewers' eyes registered the light coming from it; their attention was occupied counting passes, and the gorilla fell outside what that attention was holding. This is inattentional blindness: the failure to consciously perceive a fully visible stimulus because attention was directed elsewhere. The effect is not a curiosity of the laboratory. Radiologists searching lung images for nodules miss a gorilla deliberately embedded in the scan at a rate of 83 percent [[cite:C0270]].

A related body of work on change blindness shows the same architecture from another angle. People giving directions to a stranger frequently fail to notice when the stranger is swapped for a different person during a brief interruption, and continue the conversation without registering the substitution [[cite:C0271]]. Film editors learned the same lesson empirically, hiding changes of prop and lighting across cuts by timing them to moments when the audience's attention is occupied.

The conclusion is uncomfortable and important. What we experience is not the scene in front of us. It is the narrow fraction of that scene which attention has selected and amplified, surrounded by a low-resolution, expectation-filled remainder, and the impression of complete coverage is assembled after the fact by inference. Attention is therefore not a filter placed after perception. It is the selection that decides what gets perceived at all.

Nor is this a defect. The visual system did not evolve to record the world; it evolved to support action in it, and for that purpose the unattended really was usually irrelevant. The system is not failing when it misses the gorilla. The mismatch is between what it was built for and what is now asked of it, in settings where the unattended thing is a car entering an intersection or an alarm in a hospital ward.

That reframing matters for machine perception, because it changes what an attention mechanism is for. If attention is a resource allocator bolted onto a system that has already seen everything, its failures are inefficiencies. If attention is what determines what the system sees, its failures are blind spots — and a system that has learned which regions of its input reward processing has also learned which regions to be blind to. The human case shows that such blind spots are invisible from the inside: the viewers who missed the gorilla did not experience a gap.

Human attention is conventionally divided into two modes. Goal-directed attention is voluntary, guided by task demands, and associated with the fronto-parietal network, including the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and the posterior parietal cortex. Stimulus-driven attention is involuntary, triggered by novel or salient events, and associated with a ventral network including the temporoparietal junction and the ventral frontal cortex. Machine systems implement recognizable analogues of both: attention weights conditioned on task embeddings on the one hand, saliency models keyed to contrast and novelty on the other.

The self-attention mechanisms in transformer architectures are the most consequential instance [[cite:C0272]], and they make the point about blind spots concrete. A model that weights some tokens heavily is a model that weights others near zero, and nothing in the architecture announces which relevant thing was assigned a low weight.

Attention schema theory extends this further, holding that a system which models its own attention — a schematic, simplified internal representation of what it is attending to and what it will attend to next — thereby acquires the materials for a form of self-awareness [[cite:C0273]]. What the schema tracks is not the world but the system's own relation to it. That claim is taken up in the discussion of machine self-models in section 2.3.3, where the same move appears in a different setting: a model of the self is a model, with the simplifications and the failure modes any model has.

For a system intended to act ethically, the design question is not how to allocate attention efficiently. It is whether a system can be built to notice that its own attention has a shape, and to treat the regions it has learned to discount as an open question rather than as empty space.
