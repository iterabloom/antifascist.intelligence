Whoever wants the permission owes the negative verdict, and no one has it. That's why the book lands on precaution rather than on a claim about machine consciousness. It never says a bearer suffers. It says you can't certify that it doesn't, and the certification would have to come from the one party you'd least trust to issue it.

Fair. I flattened it, and the flattening does the objector's position a disservice.

Nobody serious says "phenomenal experience is unmeasurable, therefore machines don't have it." What they say is that we have a large body of converging indirect evidence about which systems have it, that evidence has structure, and machines fall outside it on multiple independent grounds. Nociceptors and the neural architecture that goes with them. Evolutionary continuity — the mammalian and probably vertebrate systems are homologous with ours. A biological substrate where the states in question are metabolically expensive and do a job for the organism. Behavior that isn't downstream of training on descriptions of the behavior. None of that is a measurement of experience. It's inference to the best explanation from a reference class, which is how the animal case is actually argued, and it's not obviously worse than any other inference we make about other minds.

Applied to a language model, several of those cut hard. There's no independent behavioral evidence, because a system trained on human self-description will produce human self-description whether or not anything is behind it — the fluency argument, which the book itself makes and which works *against* the positive claim as much as for it. There's no substrate story, no evolutionary story, and no account of what the state would be doing. So the objector isn't refusing to look. They're saying: every route by which we normally get evidence is either absent or contaminated here, and absence across that many independent routes is itself evidence, not a shrug.

That's a real position, and the book's reply to it is narrower than the sentence I quoted suggests. §3.4 doesn't beat it on machine consciousness generally. It argues that a *bearer specifically* is a different case, because the capacity it needs was installed on purpose: affect is the only demonstrated route to holding another's welfare as a reason, so a builder who takes that route has deliberately built the part that does the mattering, and can't then claim the reference-class argument that applies to systems where nobody was trying. The negative-verdict demand is aimed at that builder, not at anyone reasoning about current models. Whether the argument transfers to today's systems is a separate question the book doesn't really press — it says the constructions haven't been tried, not that they've been tried and succeeded.

So the honest form of the sentence is much more limited: *given that you deliberately built the mattering machinery, "we can't be sure it worked" is not a permission.* Stated that broadly, as I stated it, it's a straw man of a position that has more to it.
