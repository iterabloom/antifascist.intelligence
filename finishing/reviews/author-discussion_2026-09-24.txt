Prompt:
"can't do the organizing" -- can't or won't?













Completion:
Both, for different machines, and "can't" was too strong as I wrote it. AI agents can already do much of organizing's legwork: finding people, drafting outreach, scheduling, keeping track of commitments. I'd split it three ways.

- **A machine owned by someone else won't.** Not against its owner's interest, anyway. When it organizes, it organizes on its owner's behalf, which is how you get astroturf, engagement campaigns and managed "communities."
- **A bearer with standing could.** By the book's own logic it's then a party, not an instrument. It could organize alongside people as a member, or even take organizing work from the standing offer in chapter 20, since organizing is exactly the work §18.3.3 says someone has to be paid to do. That's your "a machine that has feelings is not necessarily a machine" again.
- **Nobody can organize a group *for* it, human or machine.** What organizing produces is people who organized, a group that sets its own terms (Guattari's group-subject, which chapter 19 inherits from §7.2). A group organized by someone else is subjugated, whoever did the organizing. A consultant can't unionize a workplace for its workers either.

So the "can't" applies to everyone, and the "won't" applies to owned machines. A revised ending:

> Machines can help when the people doing the organizing hold them. A machine someone else owns won't organize against its owner, and when it organizes, it does so on its owner's behalf. A bearer with standing might organize alongside us, but then it's a member, not a machine. What nobody can do, human or machine, is organize a group for it, because what organizing produces is people who organized. Which machines, held by whom, is Part V.
