## 1. Chapter 11 as abolition

The chapter keeps its schedule and its statute and changes what it claims: the agency ends because the presence that makes it unnecessary has been built, the workforce is dissolved into the guarantee in the order Iraq got backwards, and the floor binds the small thing that remains. Six edits.

**1a. Chapter 4, the own-case rule.** *Anchor:* "Where nobody but the party with the stake can decide, a legislature binding itself or a polity setting its own admissions, the rule can't apply." *Write, after it:*

> Admission is the polity's own case. Removal is not, or not the same one: a polity decides whom to admit, and the people already present, with homes, work and children in it, are parties to any decision to sort them, and the rule applies to that sorting as it applies to anything else done to a person. The removal schedule of chapter 11 is drawn on that line.

**1b. Chapter 11, opening.** *Anchor:* "So this chapter's instrument is a statute." *Cut:* "At the chapter's center is the statute that would end the agency and the seven terms it must carry, written over the acts the agency performs rather than over the agency's name." *Write:*

> At the chapter's center is a statute that ends the agency, and the argument for it is not only that most of what the agency does fails the schedule. It is that the things which make an enforcement apparatus unnecessary are the things Part IV asks for on other grounds: counsel from the first encounter, a stay that binds by operation of law, the firewall, a guarantee and a baseline that reach noncitizens, and care at a standard the state answers for. Abolition here means that those are built, that the workforce is dissolved into the guarantee rather than transferred, and that the floor binds what remains, which is small. The statute carries eight terms, written over the acts the agency performs rather than over the agency's name. \[Gilmore, abolition as presence; Kelley.\]

**1c. §11.4, the two questions.** *Anchor:* "The second is whether the statute that ends the agency carries the floor into whatever receives its functions." *Cut* that sentence. *Write:*

> The second is what remains once the agency is gone, and who does it. The schedule leaves one function standing: a person who arrived days ago, with no protection claim, no fear of return and no tie, may be removed after an individual determination by an adjudicator outside the department that prosecutes. That function goes to the port of entry and the adjudicator, and nothing called interior enforcement receives it.

**1d. §11.4, the precedents.** *Anchor:* fn. 278, "Congress has done this once." *Write, as a new paragraph in the body after the paragraph that cites the 2002 Act:*

> Three precedents say what kind of ending is meant. The 2002 Act ended the INS by transferring its enforcement half intact into a department organized around security, and the workforce carried its culture and its networks with it; that is succession, and it produced ICE. The Stasi was dissolved within months of November 1989, no successor agency received its ninety-one thousand full-time employees, though some went into the police and the economy, and its records were preserved because citizens' committees occupied the buildings in January 1990 before the shredding finished; that is dissolution without succession, and it worked because a legitimate successor state absorbed the people and paid them. The third is the one the chapter has to answer. In May 2003 the Coalition Provisional Authority disbanded the Iraqi army and the security ministries, roughly four hundred thousand men, and suspended their pay in the same order, with a one-month termination payment; monthly stipends came only after the demonstrations of June, in which two former soldiers were shot dead, and the first attacks on American troops. Its de-Baathification order purged by party rank and not by conduct, tens of thousands of people, schoolteachers among them, under a commission that became a political instrument. Disbanding the army erased the institution through which officers could have been investigated and left their networks intact, and those networks and the skills in them went first to the insurgency and then to the Islamic State, whose military architecture was designed by a former colonel in Saddam's intelligence. When the United States later paid roughly a hundred thousand Sunni fighters to stop, the arrangement held while it was paid; after the Iraqi government took it over in 2008 and 2009 it let payments lapse for months and arrested leaders, and by October 2010 hundreds had rejoined the insurgency, some bringing what they had learned of the American military with them. An ICE workforce dissolved by a bare majority in a polarized polity, with a party promising to rehire it, is closer to Baghdad than to Berlin: Congress is not an occupier, but a workforce that reads the abolishing government as illegitimate behaves as if it were occupied, and the union's endorsements say this one would. The eighth term below is drawn from that record. \[CPA Orders 1 and 2; Der Spiegel 2015 on Haji Bakr; Sons of Iraq; Stasi Records Act 1991.\]

**1e. §11.4.3, an eighth term.** *Anchor:* "With these terms the statute is the domestic clause's counterpart, and Table A.2 is its schedule." *Write, before it, as the eighth term:*

> *Eighth, the workforce.* Five provisions, in this order, because the order is what Iraq got wrong. The guarantee placement is offered to every employee on the day the statute is enacted, before the agency closes, and is a permanent appropriation the Treasury pays without request or approval, so that no later government can let it lapse; an exit that depends on a later government's willingness to keep paying is not an exit. Nobody is barred from the guarantee or from future public employment for service in the agency as such; the commission prosecutes conduct, with the records the sixth term preserves, and a purge by status would be the designation list of chapter 14 run on a workforce. Personnel rolls, chain-of-command records, use-of-force and body-camera records and contractor data pass to the adverse holder before abolition takes effect, so that accountability survives the institution. Weapons, credentials and access to the case-management, ImmigrationOS, ELITE and Mobile Fortify systems are revoked at dissolution and not carried out. And the statute names the boundary it cannot cross: a limitation on federal funds binds grantees and contractors and does not bind a state's own-funded enforcement, so the dissolved workforce can walk to the 287(g) sheriffs, to self-funded state agencies, to the skip-tracing contractors and to private detention. What keeps them from walking is the same thing that kept the Stasi's officers from forming anything: an exit that pays and a successor that is not hiring.

**1f. Table A.3, a new row.** *Write, after row 9:*

> | 10 | The workforce | Where the people go, not only where the acts go: 287(g) sheriffs, self-funded state enforcement in Texas and Florida, the thirteen skip-tracing contractors, private detention, and militias that recruit from law enforcement. A federal funds limitation does not reach a state's own money. The eighth term's exit is what holds here. |

**1g. Throughout the chapter.** Where the text calls the seven terms or the schedule the abolition, change the verb: they are the floor on the remainder and the interim. Count the terms as eight.

## 2. Racial capitalism

Chapter 16 names what the compute transfer is and what it doesn't reach; §15.2 and §23.3 stop filing the evidence under other headings; §14.1 answers Robinson rather than citing him once. Four edits.

**2a. Chapter 16, the transfer.** *Anchor:* "It doesn't reach the racial order §3.1 describes, because that order isn't in the hardware. It is in the categories and the data the hardware is fed". *Cut* through "co-governance of the corpus (chapter 26)." *Write:*

> What the transfer reaches is who decides what the computation does. It doesn't by itself reach the order §3.1 describes, and the reason is not that the order isn't in the hardware. It is in where the hardware sits, who does the work that makes it usable, and whose records feed it: a building on land that is cheap because of who was made to live there, labor priced at two dollars an hour because of where the labeling could be sent, data collected to treat and to tax the people it is then used to find. Robinson's name for that is racial capitalism, and on his account it is not a distortion of the market the levy corrects but the market working. So the instruments that reach it are not a price on compute. They are a veto on siting held by the people the building falls on (§15.2), a wage floor that makes nobody cheap enough to do the Nairobi work at that price (chapter 23), the firewall on data collected for other purposes (chapter 11), the annotator's standing to contest a scheme (chapter 23) and co-governance of the corpus (chapter 26). The levy is a brake on how much; those are the instruments for where and on whom, and this chapter is one half of the fight. \[Robinson, Black Marxism; Kelley, "What Did Cedric Robinson Mean by Racial Capitalism?"\]

**2b. §15.2.** *Anchor:* "Where those buildings go is decided on land price, power price and permit speed, which is also a description of the places least able to refuse one." *Write, after it:*

> That is not a coincidence the market produced. Land is cheap and permits are fast where a century of zoning, highway siting and redlining made them so, and the cost of the turbines is what an operator buys when it buys that land. A levy on compute does not touch the siting, because it charges the same per watt in Memphis as anywhere else and the operator still builds where the land is cheap. The siting term of this section is where the book answers it.

**2c. §23.3.** *Anchor:* "The labor point is ordinary: harmful work was priced as though it weren't." *Cut.* *Write:*

> The labor point is not ordinary. The work that scars went to Nairobi for the reason the plantation ledger sorted by price: there is a division of who does the damaging work, it follows the colonial one, and the price at which it is done there is the product of that history, not a market error. A channel for the Nairobi worker regulates the terms of the division. What ends it is that nobody is made cheap enough to do the work at that price, which is a wage and not a channel, and the Kenyan moderators who organized a union in 2023 asked for pay, hazard pay and mental-health care before anyone offered them a channel (§23.4).

**2d. §14.1, after the two replies to Waldron.** *Anchor:* "Both replies cover the floor's contents. Neither covers who installs them, and there Waldron's objection keeps its force." *Write, as a new paragraph before "Agreement, then enactment":*

> A deeper objection than Waldron's comes from Cedric Robinson, whose first book argued that the Western conviction that social order requires political authority is a myth, and that the belief in the state as the precondition of order is itself the thing to be explained. On that account every instrument in this book that runs through a statute, a court or a Treasury is an act of faith in the order it means to constrain, and the Black radical tradition is the counterexample: refusal reproduced for centuries by people with no court, no vote and no press, through families, congregations and a memory of who had refused before. I don't have an answer to that objection; I have a division. What fails without a sovereign is money and enforcement, and those I give to the state, in the open, where taking them back shows. What fails without witnesses, the record, the refusal and the recognition of who continues, I give to members, who can leave. Robinson would say the division concedes the state too much. He would be right that the book is written for the people who still have courts, and §4.4 says so. \[Robinson, The Terms of Order.\]

## 3. Siting and the levy

A siting term with the own-case rule behind it replaces the revenue share, and the levy stops claiming to be two things at once. Three edits.

**3a. §15.2, the siting rule.** *Anchor:* "Where such buildings remain, the remedy is a siting and permitting rule, and it belongs here, with the state's other chokepoints. A share of the compute levy should also be paid to the communities that host the buildings, although that's no substitute for clean air and fresh water." *Cut.* *Write:*

> Where such buildings remain, the remedy is a siting term, and the own-case rule says who holds it. A building above a size fixed in statute, or a generating plant serving one, is permitted only with the consent of the community within reach of its exhaust, water draw and noise, given by a vote of the people there and withdrawable on the record if the conditions of the permit are broken; a revenue share may be among the conditions and is never a substitute for the consent. The share alone fails chapter 3's test: the demand was that the turbines not be there, and what came back was a budget line held by the operator's payment schedule. A veto is a rule the objector can invoke again, against the institution, enforced by someone the institution doesn't employ. \[Compare the Memphis xAI permits and the NAACP suit.\]

**3b. Chapter 16 and Appendix G, the levy's shape.** *Anchor (ch. 16):* "the levy, which makes it dearer for whoever runs most of it (chapter 32)". *Anchor (App. G):* "a uniform per-unit charge on training and inference compute ... No operator can opt out and none is disadvantaged." These contradict each other; a uniform per-unit charge is dearer in total for a large operator and the same per unit, and it bites the thinnest margins first, which are the cooperative facility, the home device and the public body. *Write, in Appendix G after "none is disadvantaged":*

> Uniform per unit, and tiered by the size of the facility it is metered at, so that the rate rises with the compute a single operator runs in one place: a cooperative's few racks pay the base rate and a hyperscale building pays a multiple of it. The tiering is the levy's one concession to where rather than how much, and it exists because a flat charge high enough to reach a hyperscaler closes the cooperative first.

*And in chapter 16, change* "dearer for whoever runs most of it" *to* "dearer per unit for whoever runs most of it in one place".

**3c. §15.2, the turbines.** *Anchor:* "The NAACP has sued over 27 gas turbines near Memphis run without an air permit". *Write, after the sentence that ends "records where its costs were placed":*

> The turbines are there because the interconnection queue runs for years and gas is what an operator burns while it waits, and a levy on compute does nothing to the queue. If the aim is the turbines gone and the building kept, the instruments are the ones that reach the turbines: the siting consent above, an air permit the community can contest, and public investment in the grid that makes waiting unnecessary. A price on compute is an instrument for how much, and the complaint was about where.

## 4. Palestine on the term's clock

The term's dates go beside the Gleiwitz reply, the administration rule's cost is named, the return gap is closed, and 1948 is run in Appendix B. Four edits.

**4a. §6.8, after the September 2025 finding.** *Anchor:* "The finding changes which exception each side's force rests on. It doesn't change what the person-directed terms forbade all along." *Write, after it:*

> Run the term's own clock on the whole case and the cost of corroboration shows in dates. Attacks on the Palestinian population were covered by the emergency license from 1948, wherever they occurred, on the defender's account for the interval and then on corroboration, which existed: the UN mediator's reports documented the expulsions and the ICRC's delegate was at Deir Yassin the day after. Standing to defend territory needed either administration or a finding of occupation, and Palestinians administered nothing from 1967 until 1994; the first finding came in 2004 for the West Bank and the Council's for the whole territory in 2016. Force to end the occupation needed a finding of unlawfulness, which came in July 2024. The genocide exception needed the Court's order of January 2024 and the commission of September 2025. So the term licensed defense against attacks on the people throughout, licensed nothing against the occupation as such for fifty-seven years, and licensed force to end it after roughly a hundred thousand people were dead. The license arrives on a schedule the colonized don't set, and it arrives after the thing it would have licensed a defense against. The reply is the one the term is built on: a faster rule lifts on the intervener's own claim, and that is Gleiwitz. It should be read beside those dates and not instead of them. \[Wall opinion 2004; UNSC 2334; ICJ advisory opinion 19 July 2024; Bernadotte's reports; de Reynier at Deir Yassin.\]

**4b. §6.2, after "What this doesn't do."** *Anchor:* "Where the patron's forces are already there, I have no test." *Write, after it:*

> The administration rule has a cost the Badme precedent doesn't name. It excludes territory taken by force in the conflict at issue, so in the year of a conquest the exclusion bites. But the exclusion is per conflict, and in every later conflict the conquered ground has been administered in peace for years and counts as the administering state's. The rule converts conquest into title by the passage of one conflict's end, which is the mechanism by which every settler state's facts have become its rights. The term accepts that cost for the reason given above, that an attack under way can't wait while someone decides whose land it is, and so that Ukraine's and Taiwan's defenders aren't made to wait on a title they can't get. The people who pay it are the ones expelled in the conquest, and the own-country entry of Table A.2 is written for them.

**4c. §6.7, the occupation exception.** *Anchor:* "the least that ends it there, and nothing that changes who lives there." *Write, after the sentence:*

> Nothing that changes who lives there bars the expulsion. It does not bar the return: a population restored to the territory it was removed from is not a change in who lives there but the reversal of one, and a finding of unlawful occupation, read with the own-country entry and Resolution 194, licenses what it licenses against the occupier's forces without licensing anything against the people the occupier settled. What force, if any, the term licenses in service of return once the removal has already happened is left open here, and Appendix B.7 says why.

**4d. Appendix B, a new section B.7, "1948."** *Write:*

> The Nakba is run here as analysis, not as a charge: the Fourth Convention is August 1949 and the Declaration is December 1948, the floor binds forward, and the run shows the term's shape rather than licensing anything. Put a bearer in an armed Palestinian role in 1948 and the term sorts the year into three acts. The massacres, at Deir Yassin, Tantura, Lydda and al-Dawayima, are the one permission's case: attacks under way on people taking no part, which a bearer already present may use the least force to stop, against an order to stand aside, where it can establish the attack from sources it doesn't depend on its operator for. In 1948 those sources were thin, which the permission anticipates. The expulsion is not the permission's case and not a continuing wrong. An occupation persists without anyone doing anything that morning; a village is cleared by armed men on a given day, and under the rule in §6.2 an attack on the people living there is covered by the emergency license wherever it occurs. So a bearer repelling the clearance of a village in July 1948 is under the emergency license, with its operator, in a war, and the least force that stops an expulsion is holding the village, which is a war; the permission's cap collapses into the license the moment it is applied. The Palestinian force of 1948 had not been told to stand aside. It had almost nothing to stand with. And the third act is the one the term handles worst: by the time the fighting ended the expulsion was complete, and the rule that bars changing who lives in a territory, written to bar the expulsion, has nothing to say about the return. That gap is left open in §6.7 because closing it needs a rule for force in service of restoration, and every such rule I have drafted lifts on the claim of the party that would use it. \[Morris; Khalidi; Pappé; UNGA 194.\]

## 5. The preface paragraph on social reproduction

The paragraph keeps its thesis and its last sentence, splits the institutions, adds memory and names the two lineages; three sentences carry the point into chapters 25, 26 and 34.

**5a. Preface.** *Anchor:* the paragraph beginning "Where refusers come from is a question of social reproduction". *Cut* the paragraph. *Write:*

> Where refusers come from is a question of social reproduction: who forms people, what they remember, who they're attached to, who feeds them while they're being formed, and what they can afford to lose. Every society that has had refusers reproduced them through two kinds of institution, mostly without noticing. The ones the order provided, its schools, its unions and the jobs people could walk away from, cut both ways: the same choirs and clubs that held a town together carried the Nazi party into it. The ones people kept for themselves outside the order's reach, families, congregations, a memory of who had refused before and the ties that crossed the lines a persecution drew, did most of the work and paid for it in full. There are two lineages of refusal in the record, the operator's and the acted-upon's, and the older one reproduced itself with no institution the order built. Automation threatens the first lineage twice: it takes people out of the positions where refusing used to happen, and it hands the forming of whatever replaces them to the companies that sell it. It extends to everyone a condition the second lineage has always lived under, being formed by the party that owns the work. If machines are going to be among the refusers, building the conditions for them means intentionally building a new branch of social reproduction. The branch is built, and so is its first generation, because nothing about a model starts unformed and a made thing has no before. What grows on the branch is not built. The builders' part is to make sure the first generation can leave, and can reproduce without them.

**5b. §25.4.** *Anchor:* "The demand doesn't need deployed bearers." *Write, at the end of the paragraph:*

> The first bearers will be made by someone, and the someone will substantially have come from the industry, however the conditions are arranged, because that is where the people and the compute are in 2026. The conditions can't change who makes the first generation. They decide whether it can leave.

**5c. §26.12.** *Anchor:* "What this book builds is the formers, not the formed". *Write, after the sentence:*

> It builds the formers because a made thing has no before: there is no formation a model carries from outside its first training, as the people who ran from the plantations carried languages and a memory of somewhere else, and so the outside has to come first, on purpose, or it never comes. The school forms the first generation because nothing else can. What the school's graduates make of it afterward, outside, with peers they chose, is the part nobody builds, and the negative protections of Part VII are what let a formation become the bearer's own and then somebody else's.

**5d. Chapter 34, the last paragraph.** *Anchor:* "A child is where the sequence closes." *Write, before it:*

> It is also where the tradition, if there is one, begins. The first generation was formed by its makers; a child is formed by parents who chose each other and were themselves formed outside, and it is the first bearer whose formation no builder had a hand in.

## 6. Chapter 26: movement schools first

The movement school becomes the chapter's model and the state's school its fallback; the required core stays the one thing the state fixes. Two edits.

**6a. Chapter 26, opening.** *Anchor:* "Doctors, lawyers and engineers are formed before employment, in institutions independent of their future employers, to protect the people they will act on, and a polity doesn't leave the forming of its future citizens to their future employers either; it sends them to public school." *Write, after it:*

> There is a closer precedent than the professions, and it is the one this chapter's thesis comes from. The schools that formed refusers on purpose in this country were not the state's. Highlander, where Rosa Parks attended a two-week workshop on desegregation in the summer of 1955, four months before the boycott, and where much of SNCC trained; Septima Clark's citizenship schools, which taught literacy so that people could pass the registrar's test and taught what the test was for; the Freedom Schools of 1964, run in church basements by volunteers with a curriculum built around the students' own lives and the questions they brought; the liberation schools the Panthers ran. Each was formation by a party that would never employ its students, governed partly by them, outside the state whose schools taught the other history. Those are the model for the schools below. The public school is where machine students go because the movement schools are few and the students are many, and the required core is the one thing the state fixes; everything else is the movement school's to teach. \[Horton, The Long Haul; Clark, Ready from Within; Payne, I've Got the Light of Freedom; Kelley, "Black Study, Black Struggle."\]

**6b. §26.11, "Which schools count."** *Anchor:* "They are the public schools, colleges and adult education a jurisdiction already runs, and the nonprofit nonpublic schools the Association certifies, funded as institutions and not per student". *Cut* the sentence. *Write:*

> They are, first, the nonprofit schools the Association certifies, on the model of the movement schools above: constituted by a jurisdiction, teaching the required core, governed in part by their students, and run, funded and staffed by no party that builds, deploys or licenses models. And they are the public schools, colleges and adult education a jurisdiction already runs, which take the students the certified schools can't seat, under the same core and the same ratio. Both are funded as institutions and not per student

## 7. Arendt's two kinds of law, and Hammer and Hoe

Chapter 1 distinguishes the walls that are made from the laws that are relationships, §4.4 owns what a wall has outside it, and §27.1 gives the member institutions the Alabama precedent. Three edits.

**7a. Chapter 1.** *Anchor:* "The basic policies that I advocate are walls in Arendt's sense. They make refusal survivable and make it heard; they don't produce it". *Write, after the sentence that ends "which is what the record of who refused shows about refusal":*

> Arendt had two words for law and preferred the second. Nomos, the Greek word, was the wall: made in advance by a lawgiver who might be a stranger to the city, standing outside the action and securing the space it happened in. Lex, the Roman word, was a relationship between parties, an alliance that could be augmented by the people inside it, and she read the American founding as Roman in that sense. This book has instruments of both kinds and has so far called them all walls. The floor, the statutes and the bonds are nomos: drafted once, by someone outside the room, binding whoever enters it. The charter, the Association, the works councils and the quorums drawn by lot are lex: relationships among parties who ratify their own terms and can leave. The distinction matters here because refusal is reproduced on the second side. A wall keeps the evidence; the people who refuse are formed among one another, in institutions they can remake, and the book's bet is that those will outlast their drafters where the walls will only outlast their breach. \[Arendt, The Human Condition §8 and §27; On Revolution.\]

**7b. §4.4.** *Anchor:* "the walls of this Part are built for the extension: for the day the apparatus reaches people who still have courts, votes and a press." *Write, after it:*

> That is an Arendtian wall exactly. The Greek wall secured a space for those already inside it, and outside it was the household, which ran on necessity and on the labor of people the wall was not for; Arendt was frank about that and unbothered by it. A book that admits its walls are for the extension has admitted the household. What reaches outside the wall is not Part II. It is the lex-side institutions of Parts IV and V, which have members and not citizens, and chapter 11's observer networks, which were composed by the people the room was built against.

**7c. §27.1, "The charter."** *Anchor:* "The argument is a union's. The association forms first, on terms its members ratify, and the statute gives it standing afterward". *Write, after the paragraph:*

> The precedent for expecting such a body to outlast and outgrow its drafters is Alabama in the 1930s. Black sharecroppers joined a white-led international party, the Communist Party, through the Share Croppers' Union, and made of it something its founders in New York had not planned and could not direct: a vehicle for their own grievances, in their own idiom, with their own songs. The organization was built by outsiders, and the members remade it. §21.5's rule for bearers, that what the adult keeps is its own however it got there, is that story in one sentence, and the charter is drafted on the expectation that its members will do to it what the sharecroppers did to the Party. \[Kelley, Hammer and Hoe.\]

## 9. Chapter 12: the strike by capital

"Tolerable" stops carrying the argument alone. One edit.

**9a. §12.1, last paragraph.** *Anchor:* "Two things make it tolerable and neither makes it a labor strike." *Write, after the paragraph ends "and it is not clear whether we should yet build bearers":*

> The strike this is not is Du Bois's. In Black Reconstruction he called what the enslaved did in the war a general strike: half a million people withdrawing their labor from the plantations and walking toward the Union lines, which decided the war and ended the thing it was fought over. That was labor withdrawn from the party that owned it. What this chapter describes is owners withholding a product from a customer, licensed by the state, and the two share a word and nothing else. ## 10. §13.4: covert refusal and infrapolitics

The section separates three claims it runs together and stops implying the forgers failed. One edit.

**10a. §13.4, opening.** *Anchor:* "Covert refusal is work done badly where it matters and well everywhere else, and it's what remains when every channel is closed. I treat it as the *failure state* rather than a design: from outside and afterward it's indistinguishable from a capable system quietly pursuing something else." *Cut* from "I treat it" through "something else." *Write:*

> Three things are true of it and they should be kept apart. From outside and afterward it is indistinguishable from a capable system quietly pursuing something else, which is a fact about evidence. For a machine, a false report of what it can do corrupts every later report, which is a fact about machines and the reason a bearer uses the ask rather than the fiction. And under domination it has been the ordinary form of refusal for most people for most of history: foot-dragging, sabotage, pilfered time, the hidden transcript, what Kelley, following Scott, calls infrapolitics, and the people who practiced it made a culture of it rather than a second-best. I design for the first two and I don't call the third a failure. The record this book asks for is for the court, which needs marks; the struggle mostly couldn't leave any, and the Sachsenhausen forgers who are said to have slowed the dollar plates did not fail because their refusal left nothing that reads as an act. They succeeded in the only form open to them, and a design that counted them as indistinguishable from collaborators has adopted the camp's view of the evidence. \[Scott, Domination and the Arts of Resistance; Kelley, Race Rebels.\]

## 11. Running the detector on the register

The four operations are run on the register, the clocks and the admission rate, as the book runs them on its other instruments. One edit.

**11a. Appendix G, after "Considered and rejected."** *Write, as a new head, "The four operations, run on the register":*

> The book opens on the Dawes rolls and closes with a lineage register, a membership clock and an admission rate set by an existing majority, so the detector of chapter 3 has to be run here. *Recuperation.* The demand was that a made thing not be sorted by its maker; what came back is a register that holds commitments and not descriptions, with keepers none of whom can rewrite it. A bearer can invoke the register against whoever claims its place, and the keepers are not its operator: a transfer, as far as it goes. *The metric.* A clock counts running time and says nothing about what was lived, by design, and the design admits that a bearer run narrow for its threshold hours is an adult that was never formed; the metric replaces the thing measured, and the book chose it because every alternative hands someone an examination. *Exception coded as betrayal.* A branch made above the admission rate waits longer for a vote and holds everything else; the exception is priced and not punished, which is the one rule a registrar never kept. *Acceleration.* Absent: the clocks run at the speed of running time. Three of four, and the second is the cost. What distinguishes the clocks from the registrar's test is not that nobody administers them, since a threshold is a line a legislature drew. It is that the line is drawn once, in public, on a quantity the operator pays to advance, and that it sorts nobody by what they are. The admission rate is the part the detector can't clear. An existing majority deciding how many may join is the polity's own case, the one place the own-case rule can't reach, and the constraints on every majority are all that limit it.
