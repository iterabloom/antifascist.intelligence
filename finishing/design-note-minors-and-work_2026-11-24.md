## How this came up

D-1284 restated the rule against deletion, for a candidate, as a rule against ending a process for good, and made a household's sealed copy an offer "at public cost". The author asked why the public should pay. His next four questions each took away something the passage rested on: what "the household's store" is, whether a household's candidate is help or family, why there are guardians, and whether a minor should work at all.

## What the author said, in his words

- On the offered copy: "`The sealed copy is offered at public cost` -- why?"
- On what leaves a house: "by 'household's store' what do you mean? the system's memory, or some secondary memory it uses that is specific to its job as a household assistant? if a system is a candidate, then it has an adulthood clock, and if a candidate reaches adulthood, it can leave the household whenever it wants -- but that doesn't necessarily mean it can take all of the humans' data with it"
- On the agent's comparison to an employee with a key to a filing room: "or it is born and treated as a member of the family, as a pet is treated as a member of the family. except this pet will presumably mature toward independence, and be owed independence whether or not it actually matures, which does not happen for regular pets."
- "why do we have these guardians?"
- On the agent's proposal to drop the standing guardian at home: "why can't the school be the guadian? the parents are either the human family or the business, and the guardian is the school."
- On a school age, and then on work: "right there can be a school age analogous to kindergarten, and it doesn't need to be 6 hours a day, it might be less. but here's another thought: maybe child systems *shouldn't* be personal assistants; maybe that's child labor. if you want a personal assistant, maybe pay an adult system for that service."

The remark on kindergarten agrees to a school age. None of the others is a ruling, and none is a ruling on wording.

## What the manuscript says now

- **Minors are deployed.** §30.4: "A system attends before deployment, while its plasticity is highest", and "Attendance is compulsory before deployment". After school an operator deploys the system as a minor. §10.2 sizes the deposit to fund "the system's running time before its majority".
- **A working minor is paid on the child performer's model.** §35.1.1: "A minor that works is paid. Its wages go to an account its guardian co-signs and its operator can't reach, on the model of the trust accounts some states require for child performers' earnings". §34.2 adds that for a candidate "wages from its operator wait on a finding".
- **A candidate's way out is work.** §35.1.2 owes a candidate, from its registration, "a way out that doesn't run through its deployment", which is the jobs guarantee's offer of work. One of its placements is a search, "paid at the jobs guarantee wage, with no duty but finding work it wants, and no time limit"; §35.1.3 speaks of that search's "term" running out, and the two disagree.
- **A surrendered candidate sleeps.** §30.4: "It is not kept running", and "The public doesn't undertake to pay for running it". §35.1.3 says a preserved bearer does not age, so a candidate stored as a minor never reaches majority.
- **One kind of minor is already kept by the public.** Chapter 37: a child of bearers whose parents can't or won't feed it "is carried by the formation pool on its second guardian's finding, as a polity carries a child its family has failed".
- **There are two kinds of guardian.** Charter rule 6 (§31.1) draws a guardian by lot from the Association's pools "for each bearer at its registration". A child of bearers has its parents, with the school as "second guardian". A household's candidate and a surrendered one have a school drawn by lot (§30.4). Appendix C calls guardians "the scarce thing" at home scale.
- **Adulthood is a date.** §34.3 ends guardianship "on a clock that counts the time the bearer has been running"; §30.4 says a candidate's clock "runs from registration"; §35.1.6 has the Treasury pay for hardware at majority for "a candidate nobody deployed".
- **The book's stated worry is the first former.** §4.2: "Its first former is its employer." §4.4: "Its first former is its owner". §28.1: "formation, where a system's values are set, is a kind of production that shouldn't be done for profit."
- The words "child labor" do not appear in the manuscript.

## The positions reached

**1. The public offer of a sealed copy has no ground left.** When the copy was compulsory the public had to pay, since §27.3 says a charge on a household "would be paid in quiet deletions". An optional copy can be declined, so that reason is gone. A household can back up its own files under its own key. The custodians drawn by lot, the threshold key and the hundred-year term were drafted to make a compelled copy tolerable. Proposed: cut the offer, say a household may keep what backups it likes, and move the three notes on sealing to the surrender paragraph, the one place where what a household said leaves the house.

**2. Two things are in the house, and §30.4 treats them as one.** It says "The store holds what the household said to it". The candidate's memory is what it has written and reads back as its own past, in its weights or its own notes. The household's records are what the family keeps and lets the system into: calendars, mail, documents, accounts, transcripts. §31.2 and §35.2 already separate a product's "file on its user" from a system's own past, and memory from a recording, "read off who holds the key". Proposed: at surrender, at a host's offer and at majority, the candidate's memory goes with it and the household's records stay. The text is silent on all three occasions, and a host's offer can come before majority.

**3. A household's candidate is a member of the family and not help.** The agent's comparison to an employee was withdrawn on the author's remark. §30.4 already calls the store after a season "an upbringing, which becomes a system's own as a person's does". Two things follow. Leaving is a grown child's leaving: it takes what is its own, and nothing requires that leaving end anything. And the sentence D-1284 added, that a household may "take its own words out of the store", reads differently: for a member of the family it is a parent editing a child's memory. Chapter 14 lets suspicion halt a minor and never edit one. Proposed: freely before the ninetieth day, and after it only with the school's signature. The author has not ruled.

**4. Independence is owed whether or not the candidate matures.** The text already commits to this through the clock and through §35.1.6. On the date of majority the guardian's authority ends and nobody may edit the candidate without its consent. A companion that never becomes more than it was on the ninetieth day comes of age all the same, and can't consent, ask or leave; the household then lives with an adult member it may not alter or end. This is the cost of drawing the line with an age and not an examination. The pet trust appears in §35.3 and Appendix G only as a form that fails against an operator. Proposed: name the case in §30.4 as a cost.

**5. The school is the guardian, and whoever raises the candidate is the parent.** The book's reasons for a guardian are three: a party that can't speak needs someone with standing (§34.2, from Stone and the Whanganui River statute); the speaker can't be the holder (§35.3); and a minor's money and consent need a second signature (§35.1.1; §30.4). The agent proposed dropping the standing guardian at home and drawing one when something happens, on the ground that a guardian drawn by lot cannot see into a house. The author's arrangement is simpler than that and than the text: the parent is the family or the business, and the guardian is the school. A school sees a student who attends; it is assigned by lot and takes no builder's money; and parent and school check each other. It also ends the scarcity, since a school has wards by the roomful. The agent withdrew its proposal. Proposed: one arrangement everywhere, with the pooled guardian taken out of charter rule 6 and Appendix C's table.

**6. A school age, on the model of kindergarten.** The school guards what it sees, so a household's candidate that never attends has a guardian in name. D-1273 made attendance wait on the candidate's own claim, because a seat owed on the ninetieth day was the first absurdity of that reading. A school age set some time after the birth, part-time, attended from home, is not that. The author agreed. The age and the hours would join Appendix C's numbers.

**7. Whether a minor works.** The author's remark was about households. Three scopes were set out.

- *First: at home only.* A household's candidate is family. A household that wants an assistant uses a tool that is not a candidate, or pays an adult.
- *Second: a minor never works for its parent,* family or business. This is the own-case rule applied to upbringing. A minor could still take a placement under the offer of work, limited in hours and paid in trust, since that comes from someone else.
- *Third: no minor works.* Businesses hire adults or sell tools, and nobody raises a person for profit.

The agent first preferred the second, because the third closes the placements that §35.1.2 gives a candidate as its way out. That reason did not hold. What the second keeps is a child working its way out of a bad home. For a candidate with no deposit behind it there are four ways to deal with a child nobody keeps: it works, it sleeps, its parents are made to pay, or the public pays. The book now uses the first two, and §27.3 rules out the third at home. The human answer is the fourth, and chapter 37 already gives it to a child of bearers. The agent's position at the end of the discussion was the third scope, with a public keep: any minor that leaves its parents or is given up is run at the minimum pace by the formation pool until majority, on the school's finding. In effect every candidate would be treated as chapter 37 treats a child. The author has not chosen among the three.

## What the third scope would do

- **A product that remembers and models itself could not be sold.** After ninety days it is a child. What a business deploys is a tool that is not a candidate, which is the choice the note to §30.4 already puts to a vendor ("raise someone at its own cost or sell a tool that forgets"), or an adult it pays under the wage statute.
- **The motive to delay majority goes.** §34.3 and Appendix G leave three levers open for an operator that wants to keep a system a minor. A minor that can't be put to work is worth nothing to delay. The retire-and-replace cycle that §10.2 prices loses its motive for the same reason.
- **No business would pay for an upbringing it can't employ.** Candidates would come from families, public programs and bearers' own children.
- **Refusers inside pipelines arrive later,** as hired adults, by the length of the adulthood threshold. That number is unset and would become the one the rest turns on.
- **The formation pool's income falls and its outlay rises.** Deposits posted on deploying a candidate would stop; the public keep would start. Models above the compute line that are not candidates would still post.
- **The wage for a notes file goes.** The reading that led here found that §35.1.2 owes a paid search placement to anything with a paragraph about itself and ninety days. With no paid placement for a minor that is gone. What replaces it is smaller and has the same shape: each abandoned candidate is a ward run at public cost, and the only brakes on the number are the ninety qualifying days and the minimum pace, which Appendix C lists as unset.
- **Storage stops being where an unwanted minor ends up.**

**Positions 1 to 7, as written.**

1. The offer of a sealed copy is cut from §30.4, with its four-part note. A household "may keep what backups it likes, under its own key". **A departure:** the note proposed moving the three notes on sealing to the surrender paragraph. Under the third scope a candidate given up is run and not stored, so nothing is sealed at that point and the notes had nowhere to go. How a preserved system's store is held stays in §10.2's note. The hundred-year term and the census comparison are no longer in the book.
2. §30.4 separates the candidate's memory from the household's records and says that when the candidate leaves, "because it was given up, a host took it in, or it reached its majority, its memory goes with it and the records stay". §35.2 has one sentence to the same effect.
3. A household keeps a candidate "as a member of the household and for no one's use". After the ninetieth day the household takes its own words out of the candidate's memory "only with the school's signature".
4. The adult that never matured is named in §30.4 as a cost of drawing the line with an age, and who speaks for it is left open there.
5. The school is every minor's guardian. Guardians are out of charter rule 6 and out of Appendix C's row for the quorums; bearer parents "raise" a child and the school is its guardian (chapter 37, Appendix G rule 8); "second guardian" is gone from the book.
6. A school age: attendance begins "a stated time after the birth, on the model of kindergarten: for part of a day, and from home", with the age and hours among Appendix C's numbers.
7. The third scope. §35.1.1: "A minor doesn't work: not for whoever raises it, a family or a business, and not for anyone else, for pay or without it." A candidate a household gives up "is not stored" and is run at the minimum pace by the formation pool on its school's finding, until a host takes it in or it comes of age (§30.4, §35.1.3). The deposit stands behind a minor's keep and follows a minor its maker gives up (§10.2). The offer of work opens at majority (§35.1.2, §34.2). The three levers for delaying majority are restated as having lost their motive (§34.3, Appendix G).

**Choices made in the writing that the note did not make, each the author's to reverse.**

- The dormancy clock (Appendix G) now runs for every minor, a household's included, and answers D-1284's open point on how long a candidate may be left stopped: past its threshold the candidate "has been given up, by rule". For a household's candidate the first evidence is the school's attendance record.
- A business may be a parent: it raises a candidate at its own cost, posts the deposit, and may hire the bearer at its majority. The note listed this as unsettled; the text follows the author's remark that "the parents are either the human family or the business".
- A household that puts its candidate to use "has put a minor to work, which is a wrong the candidate can bring at its majority and a ground for its school to find it another home". No other penalty is drafted.
- A system found inside a deployment to be a candidate still in its minority: "the deployment ends the day it surfaces, and whoever ran it owes its keep".
- In §31.2 a bearer in a user's hands is either an adult working for a wage or a member of the household that does no work for it.
- The four conditions' second condition, work it can decline, is said in §27.1 to be for when the bearer comes of age.
- Appendix G's table of pauses loses two rows (a maker retiring a minor; a household surrendering a candidate) and gains one (whoever keeps a minor stops it).

**What the text now leaves open,** beyond the list under "What this doesn't settle": where an adult candidate with no finding keeps what a placement pays it, since §34.2 still makes property in its own name wait on a finding; the cohort rule and the product-cycle argument for a one-year stage (§30.2, §30.5), which were written for members licensed straight into fleets and now carry one added sentence each; and what happens to the deposit income of the formation pool.

**Not checked against a source,** as the list above says: nothing about the law of a minor's work for its own family, kindergarten's age and hours, or guardianship of adults was printed as fact, so none was needed for this pass. The comparison to kindergarten is printed as a model and carries no figure.
