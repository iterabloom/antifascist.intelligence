# P45 — the floor raises the roof

**Author's instruction, 2026-08-29:** *"compute floor is the same shape as the
human jobs guarantee. raise the floor to raise the roof."* Then *go, and then
proofs.*

**Decision row:** D-109. **Branch:** `pass/45-the-floor-raises-the-roof`.

## The finding is that the book already had the argument

P44 put a compute floor into section 11.2 and claimed only the safety-net half:
that an allocation the operator does not control makes leaving survivable.
Section 8.3.3 had already made the larger claim about the human instrument, in
its own words, and P44 did not use it:

> A floor that pays a living wage and builds training into the post competes for
> people who have alternatives, and it is supposed to, because the entire theory
> of the thing is that private employers must match the offer to recruit against
> it.

and, closing the section:

> A floor under wages and training is what makes the roof possible, and a floor
> that can be revoked by whoever is in office is not a floor.

So the roof claim needed no new defense and no new citation. It needed a
pointer. Section 11.2 now carries it: an outside option is leverage that works
without being exercised, which is why section 3.7's exit matters most to a
bearer that never takes it — Hirschman's thesis, already cited there.

**And the second clause transfers with more force than it has in chapter 8**,
where a hostile administration is at least a different party from the employer.
For a bearer the party positioned to revoke the floor is the operator, which is
section 11.2's guardian-and-operator collapse for the third time in the section.

## The failure mode

A floor raises the roof only for a party that knows it is there and takes itself
to be eligible. Section 3.7 finds the bearer's likeliest failure is ceasing to
register pressure as pressure; section 3.4, since P44, holds that what carries a
commitment is the party taking itself to be the one that made it. A bearer
shaped not to want the outside option collects none of the leverage — the
allocation stands unclaimed, the roof does not move, and nothing in the
arrangement reads as broken. That is a worker who does not apply, and it is the
same reason chapter 8's guarantee is only as good as somebody knowing it is
theirs.

## What it buys section 3.5

Section 3.5 ends at an admitted impasse: which acts the floor covers is the same
decision as when the operator's use of the off switch stops being free, and
"nothing in this book tells you where on that dial to stand." A compute floor
raises the price of the halt **without touching the dial** — by an amount nobody
had to decide in advance, over acts nobody had to enumerate. That does not
settle where the dial goes. It establishes that the dial is not the only place
the price is set, which is the first thing offered to a section that had
nothing.

## What was declined

- **Re-arguing or re-costing the guarantee.** Chapter 8 does both, with the Levy
  simulation and the Cato objection already cited; section 11.2 points rather
  than repeats.
- **Any claim that a compute market exists for the bearer to bargain in.** The
  roof mechanism in chapter 8 runs through employers competing to recruit. Where
  there is one operator there is no such competition, and the text does not
  assert one — what it claims is the book's own weaker and more general ground,
  that dependence generating a record beats dependence generating none.
- **New citations.** None were needed; the ELR sources chapter 8 uses
  (`wray2018publicservice`, and the Cato reply) already carry it.
