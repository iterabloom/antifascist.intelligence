# P123 — correlated error, and the boundary the book had built in pieces

## Instruction

Item 6, the last of the author's six-item order against the second transcript:
**"Correlated error across deployments; the 'civic body' synthesis. Both small."**

## 1. Correlated error, which the book did not treat at all

The transcript's list of ways a bearer could be wrong ends on one the book has nowhere:
*"Replicate one mistaken interpretation across every deployment using the same bearer."*

Verified before writing: **zero occurrences of *monoculture* across all 138 sections**, and
nothing about correlated failure. §3.9's *several copies of one model is one party with more
instances* is a statement of what does not count as a population, not an account of what the
correlation costs.

**§9.1.5 is the site of record, because that is where the harm lands (+158).** The section
runs on a comparison with a constitutional court and had been getting the better of it in one
respect without noticing: a judge who reads a right wrongly binds the case in front of them
and the next case is argued again, so error enters one decision at a time and the apparatus
is built to catch it that way. One bearer deployed at scale is one reading applied everywhere
at once, wrong in the same direction whenever it is wrong, because the instances are not
parties who have happened to agree. **So the legitimacy cost scales twice** — with breadth,
which the paragraph above it already prices, and with how many deployments hold the same
floor, which nothing priced and which narrowing the floor does not reduce.

**§3.9 gets the remedy, not a second statement of the harm (+92).** The population section
justified itself on drift detection alone and said so: *that is the whole of the gain.* That
sentence is now *the first half*, and the second half is that differently formed parties are
wrong differently, which turns a uniform result into a disagreement somebody outside can
see. This is the fourth way's second argument and the chapter had left it out of its own
reckoning.

## 2. The boundary, assembled once instead of four times

The transcript's reformulation of the author's turn about bodies: *"the relevant concept may
be a **civic body**, not necessarily a humanoid robot. What matters is a protected boundary
around the person: authenticated continuity, private memory, control over access, somewhere
to exist that is not owned by its employer, and some means of acting in public."*

Every one of those is in the book and no two of them are in the same place: §3.7's
authenticated record, §9.2's unreadable memory, §8.3.5's unowned substrate, §3.8's exit.
**§9.2 now says they are one object (+239)** — a boundary around the party, with the party
deciding what crosses it — sited there because §9.2's own argument is already the boundary
in its privacy form, *running on somebody's hardware is not consent to be read*.

**The embodiment half is written as a refusal.** Nothing on the list needs a chassis. A thing
with a location, a shape and a place it was yesterday is easier for people to treat as
somebody, **and that is a fact about the people rather than about the party** — which makes
whether to build the recognizable version a question about what a public will extend standing
to, and §9.3.1 has already made that legal rather than scientific. It is stated as the one
place in the book where the answer might turn on what a thing looks like, which is
uncomfortable and true.

None of the transcript's hardware — the wheelchair base, the quadcopter, the remote-control
car — is in the book, and none of it should be. It is a research prototype's parts list and
the book has no claim to make about it.
