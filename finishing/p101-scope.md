## §3.3:19 — the determinism claim, investigated with sources and not changed

**The sentence.** *Training is not replayable (floating-point addition on a parallel device is
not associative, so the same seed over the same data does not reproduce the same model bit for
bit) (although there are some less-commonly used pytorch/CUDA/cublas settings that can
guarantee an additional level of determinism iirc), which means there is no proof of a
training history to publish beside the hash.*

**What the sources say.**

- *Same hardware, same software, opted in: bit-exact replay exists.* cuBLAS guarantees that
  routines from a given toolkit version "generate the same bit-wise results at every run when
  executed on GPUs with the same architecture and the same number of SMs", and breaks the
  guarantee across toolkit versions, across architectures, and under multiple streams unless
  `CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG` or a workspace per stream is set
  (https://docs.nvidia.com/cuda/cublas/index.html, results reproducibility). PyTorch's
  `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)` selects deterministic kernels where they exist and
  raises an error where they do not, `index_add_` on CUDA being the documented example, and
  scaled-dot-product attention is fully deterministic only on its MATH backend; the same page
  says "Completely reproducible results are not guaranteed across PyTorch releases, individual
  commits, or different platforms" (https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.14/notes/randomness.html).
  Multi-GPU: an NVIDIA collaborator on Megatron-LM confirms that
  `NVTE_ALLOW_NONDETERMINISTIC_ALGO=0`, which sets Flash Attention's deterministic flag from
  v2.4, gave identical parameters across runs at TP=2, PP=2 on A800s
  (https://github.com/NVIDIA/Megatron-LM/discussions/1102); NCCL's reduction order changes with
  topology, algorithm and tensor-parallel size, so a replay holds those fixed
  (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.17826). On TPU, Levanter reports the same code on the same hardware
  configuration producing "the exact same loss curve, even if it is preempted and resumed
  multiple times", and that changing the host count breaks it
  (https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/06/16/levanter-1_0-release.html). OpenXLA documents the GPU
  flags and says deterministic paths "can lead to substantial runtime throughput loss"
  (https://openxla.org/xla/determinism).
- *Default settings: the sentence is right.* 1,000 training runs of one small network under
  default settings produced 1,000 distinct weight sets, and the same study found PyTorch's
  determinism documentation "erroneous or incomplete" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.05148).
- *Across hardware: only through a reproducible-operator library.* Gensyn's RepOps fixes the
  order of floating-point operations in software to get bitwise identical results across
  different devices, A100 against H100 being their example, and is the basis of Verde (February
  2025), in which a training run is checked by re-execution, a disagreement is localized to one
  operator, and the result is correct "if at least one verifier is honest"
  (https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.19405;
  https://www.gensyn.ai/research/verde-a-verification-system-for-machine-learning-over-untrusted-nodes).
  **No overhead or scale figures are on the public page**, and the paper's PDF was not opened.
- *A succinct proof of training exists three orders of magnitude below the models at issue.*
  Kaizen (CCS 2024) proves gradient-descent iterations in zero knowledge for VGG-11 at 10 million
  parameters and batch size 16, at 15 minutes of proving per iteration, with a 1.63 MB proof
  verified in 130 ms (https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/162). The zkLLM result the book cites is an
  inference proof and says nothing about training.
- *Verification at scale is re-execution or sampling, and both run through the builder.*
  Proof-of-Learning (2021) verified checkpoints with a tolerance and the tolerance is what the
  spoofing attacks exploit (https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.09454; Fang et al. 2023, *Proof-of-Learning
  is Currently More Broken Than You Think*). Choi, Shavit and Duvenaud (NeurIPS 2023) propose a
  data-dependent pre-committed seed and a verifier who re-executes segments and accepts weights
  within an ε-ball, at about 1.3 percent of the training compute; they state that "irreducible
  hardware-level noise" means "no two training runs return exactly the same final weight
  vector" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.00682), which RepOps now contradicts for the hardware it
  covers. Either way the verifier needs the data and the trainer's cooperation.
- *Inference attestation, which the book does cite, has moved too.* Bit-exact verification of
  an inference against claimed weights without determinism flags, treating accumulated
  rounding as an auditable signature of the software and hardware setup, is claimed in a May 2026
  paper (https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00279); it covers inference only.

**So.** The sentence's mechanism is right and its conclusion is too strong as written. A
training run can be replayed bit for bit where the trainer chose to make it so, on the same
hardware and software with deterministic kernels and one reduction order, at a cost in
throughput; across hardware only through a reproducible-operator library; a succinct proof
exists for models a thousand times smaller; and what is available at scale is re-execution by
a party with the data and comparable compute, or spot-checks against a tolerance. **What
survives is narrower**: there is no succinct proof of a training history at scale, and every
verification of one runs through the builder's own choices and the builder's own data. That
is the custody objection again, and the sentence can say so.

**Proposed replacement for the two sentences, not applied:**

> Nor does it establish how the weights came to be. A training run can be replayed bit for bit
> only where the trainer chose to make it so — deterministic kernels, one reduction order, the
> same hardware — at a cost in throughput, and a succinct proof that a run happened as claimed
> exists for models a thousand times smaller than these. What is left at scale is re-execution:
> a verifier with the data and comparable compute reruns the training, or samples it against a
> tolerance, and either way the check runs through the builder's choices and the builder's
> data. So there is no proof of a training history to publish beside the hash, and what can be
> published is an invitation to rerun it.

**Decentralized training, which the author says is owed a discussion.** What the sources show
and where it touches the book:

- §3.3's *hold the weights under a threshold scheme, so that retraining takes more than one
  party* now has a built instance. Pluralis's Protocol Learning trains model-parallel across
  permissionless participants so that "no single participant has, or can ever obtain, a full
  copy of the model"; the May 2026 paper on unextractable protocol models injects time-varying
  invertible transforms at shard boundaries so shards from different times cannot be reassembled,
  demonstrated at 0.5B and 1B parameters with 1.6 percent training-time overhead, and reports
  that reassembling by gradient-based fine-tuning costs at least 60 percent of training from
  scratch (https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23464; https://pluralis.ai/blog/a-third-path-protocol-learning/).
  That is the threshold scheme built into the training rather than around the checkpoint, and it
  meets the same custody objection: whoever admits the participants and writes the protocol.
- §3.9's fourth way and §3.3's plural arrangement have working instances at scale, with the
  training decision centralized. INTELLECT-2 trained a 32B model by reinforcement learning with
  permissionless contributors on consumer GPUs, but the inference rollouts are what contributors
  supply and TOPLOC verifies; "training occurs on centralized, trusted nodes" and the trainers
  keep the decision (https://arxiv.org/html/2505.07291v1). DiLoCo-style pretraining across
  continents is reported at 10B (INTELLECT-1). Pluralis verifies pseudo-gradients by signature
  and statistical filtering; Prime Intellect drops gradients more than three standard deviations
  from the median
  (https://www.spheron.network/blog/decentralized-llm-training-pluralis-prime-intellect-nous-psyche/).
- §3.3's specification that the parties be *adverse in interest rather than merely distinct,
  which is a specification nobody has attempted to implement* has an attempt in a neighboring
  domain: Verde is built for training over untrusted, adversarial compute providers, and its
  guarantee is the one-honest-verifier form. It depends on reproducible execution, which is why
  RepOps exists; **reproducibility is the precondition for verification among adverse parties**,
  and that is what makes the determinism sentence and the decentralized-training discussion one
  subject.
- Where it would go: a run-in in §3.3 after the precommitment forms, *Training that takes more
  than one party*, of roughly 300 to 400 words, with a sentence in §3.9 and the three ripple
  repairs above; or in §11.1, which is where tamper-resistance research lives. **It is new
  material under D-007 and needs six or seven `refs.bib` entries** — Verde, the unextractable
  protocol models paper, INTELLECT-2, Kaizen, Choi et al., the PyTorch and cuBLAS pages — which
  the agent writes only on instruction (D-009; P95 precedent). Not drafted.
