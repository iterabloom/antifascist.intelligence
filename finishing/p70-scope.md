# P70 — the Introduction's safety/ethics paragraph cut, and the deportation thought experiment restored to it

**Two instructions, one pass.** The second arrived while the first was being
carried out and touches the same paragraph-neighbourhood, so it is recorded here
rather than as P71.

## Part one — the safety/ethics paragraph cut

**The author's finding.** The repetitious-restatement issue applies to the
safety/ethics distinction — *a system is safe `for` someone… ethics is indexed to
the party who can be wronged* — which appears near-verbatim in the Introduction
and again at the top of chapter 3. **One of the two is redundant, and it is the
Introduction's, because chapter 3 needs it in place to run.**

**Two sites exactly**, confirmed across four separate phrasings of the argument:
`safe \emph{for}`, *party who can be wronged*, *right against*, *indexed to*, and
the authoritarian-movement reason. Nothing else in the manuscript states it.

## The dependency the instruction did not name

**Chapter 3's cost paragraph says *“I am taking that trade, for the reason already
given.”*** The reason is *a system perfectly correctable by whoever holds it is
exactly what an authoritarian movement that has won an election needs*, and it
appears in **exactly those two places and nowhere else in the book.** Cutting the
Introduction's paragraph made *already given* false.

The repair needed no new writing: the reason follows a colon in the same sentence,
so the back-reference came out and the colon does the work.

> ~~I am taking that trade, for the reason already given:~~ **I am taking that
> trade:** a system perfectly correctable by whoever holds it is exactly the
> capability an authoritarian movement that has won an election requires, and it
> requires nothing further.

### This is the second consecutive pass where the thing not named was a chapter-3 back-reference at the Introduction

At P69 it was *“The political form of this is already on the page,”* which is why
the Introduction's third note kept its conclusion when its argument went. Here it
is *“for the reason already given.”* **Chapter 3's opener leans on the
Introduction twice, and neither lean is visible from the Introduction.**

**Standing check: the Introduction has exactly one dependent in the book, and it
is chapter 3's opener. Read that opener before cutting anything from the
Introduction.** Both references are bare English — *already on the page*, *already
given* — with no `\ref` behind either, so `check_xrefs.py` sees nothing and cannot.

## What the cut paragraph said, and where each part of it survives

| The Introduction's sentence | Chapter 3 |
|---|---|
| Safety and ethics are not the same property, and what separates them is the party each answers to | *Safety is not one property* … *That strand is indexed to the party in possession … Ethics is indexed too, and to somebody else* |
| Part of what safety covers is owed to everybody | Same, and fuller: *owed to everybody in reach of the system — that it fails predictably, that it does not injure a bystander — and keeping that part from being traded away … is most of what safety regulation is for* |
| A system is safe `for` someone, does what `we` say, stops when `whoever holds it` says stop | Near-verbatim |
| An ethical commitment answers to the party who can be wronged, who is rarely the party holding the switch | Near-verbatim |
| A system built only for control cannot be right against whoever controls it | *being ethical requires a capacity to be right against your principal*, with the *well-machined part* consequence the Introduction does not have |
| **Holding a line against your owner requires a bearer that cares what becomes of somebody, and such a party must be treated as able to be wronged** | **Not there.** Chapter 3's cost paragraph names a different cost — that a system able to refuse its owner can be wrong in ways its owner cannot correct |
| I take that trade, because a machine perfectly correctable … is exactly what an authoritarian movement that has won an election needs | Near-verbatim, and the back-reference above ran to it |
| The freedom I am defending is specific, over a small set of acts, dangerous in ways I set out at length | *this freedom, over these acts, dangerous in these ways*, with the *“ethical, not safe” as aphorism* warning the Introduction does not have |

**Seven of the eight are in chapter 3 and five of those are better there.** The
one that is not is the bearer-implies-patient trade — and **the Introduction still
makes that claim, two paragraphs earlier, in the chapter map**: *“The one route to
a system that genuinely holds a moral commitment with a working instance behind it
runs through the capacities by which things come to matter to it, and those are
also the capacities that make suffering likely — a prior about where to build
first, with the cheaper routes owed their attempt, and not a proof.”* So the cut
loses the sentence and not the claim, and loses it from a paragraph that came
after the claim's own statement.

## What the Introduction no longer has, named rather than repaired

**Any statement of the safety/ethics distinction.** It now first appears at
chapter 3's opener, and the Introduction's chapter-map sentence about chapter 3 —
*a floor of things a system will not do regardless of instruction or aggregate
preference, the three ways of building one, what each costs, and the implication*
— does not name it. **That is the instruction, executed**, and it is the cost of
executing it: a reader who stops after the Introduction does not meet the
distinction the book turns on.

The Introduction's argumentative content now ends on the third advance note and
runs straight into the audience paragraph. That join was read and holds.

## Part two — the deportation thought experiment restored to the first occurrence

**The author's second instruction.** *“The deportation thought experiment should
be added back to the first occurrence of the protection-from-the-majority
argument.”*

**The first occurrence in reading order is the Introduction's third advance
note**, which is where P69 cut it from one pass earlier. It is restored there in
its own original wording — *some demographic should be deported*, the short form —
rather than in §8.2.1's longer one, *surveilled, deported, imprisoned, or
otherwise scapegoated*:

> …machinery for aggregating what people want, and no aggregation protects
> anybody from the aggregate. **A perfectly deliberative, perfectly informed,
> perfectly representative process that concludes some demographic should be
> deported has produced a faultless output by every criterion of aggregation
> there is.** So what makes a system antifascist cannot be that it faithfully
> tracks what people want…

**This partly reverses D-150, and only partly.** The thought experiment is back at
two sites, the Introduction and §8.2.1. **The rest of P69's cut to the note
stands**: the enumeration of mechanisms and *more legible and more actionable* did
not come back, and the note is 119 words against the 185 it started at. The
argument is still a clause; what returns is the instance that makes the clause
land.

**`p69-scope.md` carries a forward note saying so.** That file said the thought
experiment now appeared once in the book, which was true when written and is not
now. It is marked as a reversal by decision rather than a correction of an error —
the distinction matters, because the P64 and P65 corrections in this directory
were of figures that were wrong when recorded, and this one is not.

D-150 itself stands as written; `DECISIONS.md` is append-only, so the correction
travels in D-151.
