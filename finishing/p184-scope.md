## The position, which had to be chosen rather than followed

**The gap the instruction names is no longer one gap.** P183 put
`\runin{Subject or component}` and its two paragraphs between the twenty-seconds
paragraph and *Removing the bottleneck was the stated objective*. The two
boundaries the instruction gives can no longer both be met.

**The paragraph went at the second boundary**, immediately before *Removing the
bottleneck*. The reason is the author's own statement of what the position is
for: *before the Brigadier General's stated objective arrives — which then reads
as the same want, written down by a commander*. That adjacency is preservable
exactly and is where the paragraph's last sentence has to land. **The other
criterion survives too**, because P183's first paragraph re-quotes the officer —
*zero added value as a human, apart from being a stamp of approval* — so the
quote is still live one paragraph above, which is what *the officer's sentence*
in the new paragraph's first line reaches back to.

The order now runs: the officer's twenty seconds; P183's reading of him as a
component rather than a badly governed subject; **the genealogy of the want**;
the want written down by a commander. **The other position is available** — before
P183's run-in rather than after it — and it would put the genealogy against the
quote at the cost of the epigraph adjacency.

## The citation

**`victoria2006zenatwar` authored**, under the author's standing condition that
anything added is verified by web research. Brian Daizen Victoria, *Zen at War*,
2nd edition, Rowman \& Littlefield, 2006; first published Weatherhill, 1997.

**What was verified.** That the book is the one described and the edition exists.
That Harada Daiun Sōgaku's statement is real and is the one the paragraph
paraphrases: writing in the November 1939 issue of *Daijō Zen*, in an article on
the one road of Zen and war, that marching when ordered to march and firing when
ordered to fire is the manifestation of the highest wisdom of enlightenment.
**The wording was checked against independent sources quoting it identically.**
That the doctrinal apparatus the paragraph describes — no-self and non-attachment
worked into a warrant for action without hesitation, the killing sword glossed as
the life-giving sword, warfare described as an expression of compassion — is the
book's own finding rather than an inference laid on it.

**No page locator, and the note says why.** The 1997 and 2006 editions paginate
differently, and published citations to the Harada passage give both 66--67 and
137. Neither was confirmed, so neither is asserted.

**The reception is recorded in the note, because the paragraph's second
qualification depends on it.** Institutional collaboration is documented and not
seriously disputed; **what is contested is the doctrinal reading and some
individual attributions**, most prominently Kemmyō Taira Satō's sixty-page study
in *The Eastern Buddhist*, which credits Victoria's contribution to the subject
and argues that he erred seriously in the case against D. T. Suzuki. The
paragraph states the contest rather than citing the book flat, which is why the
note carries the specifics.

**On the names policy.** Victoria is cited as the author of a published history.
Harada is named as the author of a documented published statement, quoted from
that history and attributed to no more than the source supports. Neither is rated,
ranked, or credited with anything a source does not carry.
