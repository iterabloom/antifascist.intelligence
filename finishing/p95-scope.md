## The analysis, and where it was wrong

P94's write-up had found the vocabulary defect. The author asked for the argument: what the Overleaf
edits did to it, and what they broke. **The analysis held on the four things and on the slips, and it
was wrong about the third way, twice, and the author corrected it both times.**

**First correction.** The analysis read *Slaughter* as a compulsion case and defended the original's
*a state can compel* over the author's *a jurisdiction can ignore*. The author: it is about the
executive being malfeasant and the judiciary accommodating it in the form of fancy writing, about
*Slaughter* and *Cook* released as a package on one morning, and about which is worse — the abuse or
the normalization of it. **The accommodation reading was already in the book, at §3.3's Menand
passage, cited, and §3.1 did not point to it** — D-185's topology again, two sections that complete
each other with no reference between them.

**Second correction.** The analysis then said *nobody took custody of the Court*. The author: the
Federalist Society, Leonard Leo. **That is the book's own mechanism** — *whoever forms the members
decides what gets reproduced* — which §3.1 and §3.5 had applied only to the fourth way. A court is a
population and a population is formed, so the third way's institution was captured by the fourth
way's mechanism. Compel-or-ignore was a false choice: the executive did not need to compel a bench
that formation had already supplied.

## D-190 — the third way, on formation (`52710ed`)

**§3.1's third-way paragraph** now says it fails two ways: the parties can be removed, which is the
shallow case and leaves a record, and the parties can be formed. The *Slaughter* and *Cook* paragraphs
keep their facts exactly. **A new paragraph after *Cook* names the pipeline, and every clause of it
was sourced before it was written**: the *Slaughter* majority is Roberts, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh,
Barrett, and Thomas as to all but Part III-B (the opinion header, Cornell LII); the bench is unchanged
since June 2022 (supremecourt.gov); those six are the six ProPublica's 2023 investigation connects to
Leo, who drew up the 2016 lists, advised on the three nominations chosen from them, and had helped
pick or confirm the other three; Leo's own 2018 account of the list is beside it. **Two `refs.bib`
entries, `propublica2023leo` and `cbs2018leo`, authored on the author's instruction** — standing rule
1 says the agent does not, and the log shows it has since P58. **Not put in**: *unprecedented* and
*while the markets were open*, for which nothing citable was found. The Society's official no-position
stance rests on secondary reporting; `fedsoc.org` returned 403.

**"Fail into custody" is retired from §3.5**, on the author's own words: *I don't know what the fuck
fail into custody means*. The replacement is plain — *each of the four ends with a party that can let
the floor go* — and *a state can reach those parties* became removed-or-formed. **§3.9's *why this is
not the third* now says removal is half of what the third fell to and formation the other half, this
way's own cost.** §3.3's Menand passage points back to §3.1.

**"State" collided with the chapter's own vocabulary**: *states that are bad for it* five times in
§3.2 and §3.4 against *a state can* four times in §3.3, §3.5, §3.9 and §11.2. The two government
senses left after the rewrites now say *government*; the author's *jurisdiction* in §3.1 stays.

**Chapter 1's *three ways to build one* becomes four** — a count P93 missed four lines above the one
it fixed.

**The *Cook* "test this chapter wants for every bearer" clause stays cut**, on a ruling worth
keeping: the point is to make wrongful removal costly, not to classify it, and *the reader doesn't
need to think about that point just then*. A true sentence at the wrong moment, which no class in
`tools/` catches. Checked: nothing else in the manuscript leans on that test; §9.3.5 cites neither
case.

## D-193 — §3.2 restructured, the expensive remedy (`373d894`)

**The author's diagnosis, in their words: *four things not really being four things (affective
concern vs full monty consciousness)*.** The analysis agreed and said why: the first two terms are
readings of *refusal* — output, mechanism — and the last two are properties of the refuser, two
depths of one thing on the axis §2.3.1 draws from nociception to suffering, which the section itself
conceded in *entails a minimal version of*. Two remedies were offered and the author chose the
expensive one.

**Retitled** *Four Things Refusal Can Mean* → *What Refusal Can Mean, and What a Refuser Can Be*, ten
words, synced into `ORDER.tsv`, `outline.tsv`, `ledger.tsv` and the TOC; label and number unmoved, so
no renumber map. **Inside**: the opener says two capacities; run-in heads *Two things refusal can
mean* and *Two things a refuser can be* stand over the pairs; a bridge paragraph under the second
says what the pair is about and which axis it sits on; phenomenal experience is told why it is on
the list — some accounts make it the bar for suffering and §3.4 has to answer them; and the three
gaps are named by what each is between, so *the hard problem in its usual form* and *carries that
weight instead* are said of named things. **The four labels are unchanged; nine files use them.**
**Outside**: §3.1's closing line and the glossary's affective-concern entry were the only two sites
that leaned on four-ness, checked by grep. Chapter 1 did not name the four.
